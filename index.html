The answer in one paragraph

In 2026 the Western world is spending about $1.0 trillion on AI data centers A, up from ~$290B in 2024, mostly through five companies: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle. About two-thirds buys chips and servers that wear out in five to six years, and Nvidia alone receives ~35¢ of every dollar A. The money mostly does not come from AI customers yet. About half is Big Tech's existing profit from search, social ads, retail and software, a fifth is new debt, and a fifth (~$200B) is what consumers, businesses and advertisers pay for AI. That last slice is growing about 3× a year, led by coding agents. The suppliers keep the profit: of a ~$637B AI operating-profit pool, chipmakers take ~44% and memory makers ~23%, the clouds earn ~5% and the model labs lose ~$48B A. To earn a normal return on what has already been built, AI revenue needs to reach ~$600B a year, about twice today's ~$280B run-rate A. Our base case closes that gap around 2028–29. Along the way, whoever owns the scarce input earns the rent. The scarce input moved from GPUs (2023) to packaging (2024), power (2025) and memory (2026).

Key insight

The layers the public associates with AI (clouds, labs and apps) together capture less than a tenth of the 2026 AI operating profit. The profit sits two and three layers down, with whoever owns the scarcest input: Nvidia's chips, then the memory makers A.

Where the money comes from and where it goes

This is the 2026 money map in five steps. Read it left to right. Money comes in from six sources. Some of it passes through apps and model labs. All of it reaches the companies that own compute, who pay the suppliers. It ends as someone's operating profit, or as wages, materials and interest. Band widths are proportional to dollars. Hover a band to trace it.

Three things stand out. First, customers supply less than a fifth of the money ($202B of $1,117B) A, so the build-out is still funded mainly on the expectation of demand. Second, about 51¢ of every capex dollar becomes a supplier's operating profit, with Nvidia ~23¢ and memory ~15¢ A. Third, debt now funds about as much as AI customers do. The full eight-column map, with every flow and its source dossier, is in W11 Synthesis.

The questions Track 2 will answer

Each question is set up so its answer lands next to the Western number on the same definition (from W11 §13).

  1. Capex. What is China's total AI data-center capex in CY2026 (Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance, Baidu, telcos, state funds), on W01's company-basis definition?
  2. Funding. What share comes from operating cash flow versus state-directed bank credit, local-government funds and policy banks?
  3. Silicon profit pool. Without Nvidia, who captures it (Huawei Ascend, Cambricon, Hygon, in-house chips), and at what gross margins?
  4. Where scarcity sits. SMIC 7/5nm capacity, domestic HBM (CXMT), advanced packaging or power? Is China's binding constraint chips while the West's is power and memory?
  5. End-customer AI revenue on W10's definition: subscriptions, enterprise API and agents, and AI-driven ads at ByteDance, Tencent and Alibaba.
  6. Unit costs. How much lower are China's $/MW, $/GPU-hour and $/million tokens, and why (power prices, construction cost, open-weight price wars)?
  7. Circularity. Are there vendor or cloud equity loops (Alibaba and Tencent in Moonshot, Zhipu, MiniMax, StepFun) comparable to the West's?
  8. Backlog. What is the equivalent of contracted backlog (RPO), given that Chinese firms disclose little?
  9. Power. How much headroom does "East Data, West Compute" provide, at what tariff, and how fast is generation being added?
  10. Token volumes. ByteDance Doubao and Alibaba Qwen tokens per minute versus Google's 22 billion.
  11. Open weights abroad. What share of Chinese open-weight usage is outside China (~13% of OpenRouter tokens), and does it make money?
  12. Capex intensity. Capex/operating cash flow and server depreciation lives at Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu, versus the US Big-4's 96%.
  13. Export controls. How do H20/H200 availability and the 25% revenue-share regime shape the chip mix, and how big is the grey market?
  14. Profit pools by layer, 2024–26E, in the same table format as W11 §4.1, so the tracks can be shown side by side.
  15. Scenario triggers. Property and local-government debt, chip controls, domestic demand and a Taiwan contingency.

How to read this atlas

Every number in the atlas carries three things: where it came from (a provenance badge), how sure we are (a confidence tag, where it matters) and when it was true (a date or fiscal period). Hover or tap any badge to see what it means.

Provenance badges

R
Reported: filed or published by the company or a regulator (10-K, earnings release, ISO auction result).
G
Guidance: the company's own forecast, such as a capex range or next-quarter revenue.
E
Estimate: from a third party (TrendForce, Synergy, IEA, press reports of private-company figures).
A
Atlas estimate: our own number, with the arithmetic shown on the page or in the data file.

Confidence tags

Contested or thinly sourced claims carry conf: high, conf: med or conf: low. A claim the fact-checkers could not trace to any source is marked unverified and never used as a headline. Example: a September report that Anthropic's run-rate passed $100B is unverified, so the atlas uses the last confirmed figure, $65B at end-July E.

Each layer page ends with its low-confidence items. The methodology page lists the claims still open.

Dates and periods

Every page shows an As of date (this edition: 25 Sep 2026). Every figure names its period.

CY2026E
Calendar-year estimate. Fiscal years are shifted to calendar years, which adds about ±5% error.
Q2 FY27
A company's fiscal quarter (for Nvidia, May–Jul 2026).
run-rate
The latest month or quarter, annualized. It is not contracted revenue.

One set of numbers

Where dossiers disagreed, the synthesis picked one value and explained why (W11 §9). Those values live in data/western/_canonical.json, and every page uses them, so the same metric reads the same everywhere.

The answer in two paragraphs

The West. In 2026 the Western world is spending about $1.0 trillion on AI data centers A, up from ~$290B in 2024, mostly through five companies: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle. About two-thirds buys chips and servers that wear out in five to six years, and Nvidia alone receives ~35¢ of every dollar A. The money mostly does not come from AI customers yet. About half is Big Tech's existing profit from search, social ads, retail and software, a fifth is new debt, and a fifth (~$200B) is what consumers, businesses and advertisers pay for AI. That last slice is growing about 3× a year, led by coding agents. The suppliers keep the profit: of a ~$637B AI operating-profit pool, chipmakers take ~44% and memory makers ~23%, the clouds earn ~5% and the model labs lose ~$48B A. To earn a normal return on what has already been built, AI revenue needs to reach ~$600B a year, about twice today's ~$280B run-rate A. Our base case closes that gap around 2028–29. Along the way, whoever owns the scarce input earns the rent. The scarce input moved from GPUs (2023) to packaging (2024), power (2025) and memory (2026).

China. On the same definitions China spends ~RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) A in 2026 (the West spends ~7.5× more), mostly through Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and Baidu, with the state directing ~19% (~RMB 170bn) A. It builds on its own chips (domestic vendors hold ~80% by value (Huawei ~50%; Nvidia ~8%) E) and cheap, abundant power. Usage is enormous but revenue is small: end customers pay ~RMB 157bn (~US$23bn) A. Almost nobody keeps a profit: the positive AI profit pool is ~US$17bn (West ~US$637bn), and ~US$0bn (net of lab and consumer-AI losses) A. China binds on chips, above all HBM memory; the West on memory, power and permits. The full comparison is in X00.

Key insight

The layers the public associates with AI (clouds, labs and apps) together capture less than a tenth of the 2026 AI operating profit. The profit sits two and three layers down, with whoever owns the scarcest input: Nvidia's chips, then the memory makers A.

Where the money comes from and where it goes

This is the Western 2026 money map in five steps. Read it left to right. Money comes in from six sources. Some of it passes through apps and model labs. All of it reaches the companies that own compute, who pay the suppliers. It ends as someone's operating profit, or as wages, materials and interest. Band widths are proportional to dollars. Hover a band to trace it.

Three things stand out. First, customers supply less than a fifth of the money ($202B of $1,117B) A, so the build-out is still funded mainly on the expectation of demand. Second, about 51¢ of every capex dollar becomes a supplier's operating profit, with Nvidia ~23¢ and memory ~15¢ A. Third, debt now funds about as much as AI customers do. The full eight-column map, with every flow and its source dossier, is in W11 Synthesis. China's map, RMB 1,032bn (~US$154bn) A on the same method, is in C11 Synthesis: platform legacy cash is ~38% (West 49%) of it and the state and SOEs ~15% direct + SOE (~18% incl. state-bank credit to LGFV/SOE) A.

Western build-out

Live

The US, Europe, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, the Gulf and the rest of the non-China world. Ten layer pages plus an overview and a synthesis, researched from filings, earnings calls, regulator data and reputable press, and re-verified against primary sources in Edition 2. Its three headline findings:

  • Who pays? Mostly Big Tech's old profit. Legacy cash flow is ~49% (~$547B) of 2026 flows; end customers pay ~$200B, growing ~3× a year A.
  • Who keeps the profit? The suppliers. Of a ~$637B (net of lab losses ~$589B) pool, compute silicon takes ~44% (62% in 2024) and memory ~23% (9% in 2024) A.
  • What binds? Memory, power and permits. The scarce input moved from GPUs (2023) to packaging (2024), power (2025) and memory (2026) A.

China build-out

Live

Mainland China and Hong Kong, including capacity Chinese firms own or rent offshore. Every layer mirrors the Western one with a C prefix (C00–C11). RMB is primary; US$ at 6.70 for 2026E. Edition 1 set three hypotheses. The verdicts:

  • H1 · More compute per dollar? Partly confirmed. ~7.5× fewer dollars (not 10×) and more MW per dollar, but ~1.5–2× dearer per EFLOPS, because domestic systems draw ~2.4× the power per FLOP A.
  • H2 · A flatter profit pool? Confirmed, and stronger. The pool is thin and roughly zero-sum: ~US$17bn (West ~US$637bn), ~US$0bn (net of lab and consumer-AI losses) A.
  • H3 · Chips bind, not power? Confirmed. Chips and HBM bind; power does not. Monetization is a second binding constraint A.

The 15 China questions, answered

Edition 1 set Track 2 fifteen questions, each designed so its answer lands next to the Western number on the same definition (W11 §13). Here is the one-line answer to each; the evidence is in C11 Synthesis §1 and the layer page named after each answer.

  1. Capex. How much does China spend on AI data centers in 2026? ~RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) A, up ~60% from ~RMB 560bn (~US$78bn); the West spends ~7.5× more. C11 §1.1 · C01
  2. Funding. Operating cash flow, or state-directed credit? Platform legacy cash ~38% (West 49%); the state and SOEs ~15% direct + SOE (~18% incl. state-bank credit to LGFV/SOE), five times the West's ~3% A. C11 §1.2 · C02
  3. Silicon profit pool. Who captures it without Nvidia? Huawei takes the revenue, ~US$12bn (~50% share) E, and Cambricon the profit; the whole pool is ~US$1.5-2.5bn net (~1% of Nvidia's) A. C11 §1.3 · C05
  4. Where scarcity sits. Chips, memory, packaging or power? HBM first, then SMIC 7/5nm-class capacity and yields, then packaging and tool servicing. Power does not bind A. C11 §1.4 · C06
  5. End-customer AI revenue. What do customers and advertisers pay for AI? ~RMB 157bn (~US$23bn) A, ~12% of the West's ~$200B, and only ~25-35% of it is fresh money. C11 §1.5 · C10
  6. Unit costs. How much cheaper per MW, GPU-hour and token? Far cheaper per MW (RMB 20m/MW (~US$3.0m; US US$11.3m) R), kWh and GPU-hour, but ~1.5–2× dearer per EFLOPS A. C11 §1.6 · C04
  7. Circularity. Vendor and cloud equity loops like the West's? Yes, but smaller and state-reinforced: ~12-16% strict; ~30% broad (West ~45%/60%) of lab equity A. C11 §1.7 · C09
  8. Backlog. Is there an RPO equivalent? No. Visible commitments are ~RMB 0.2-0.3tn (~US$30-45bn); ~1-2% of the West's US$2.46T A (conf: very low). C11 §1.8 · C08
  9. Power. How much headroom, at what tariff? Effectively unlimited: ~550 GW (US: 53 GW) added in 2025 R, at RMB 0.20-0.35/kWh (3.0-5.2 US¢) in the western hubs E. C11 §1.9 · C03
  10. Token volumes. Doubao and Qwen versus Google's 22 billion a minute? Doubao 180tn/day (~125bn/min; ~5.7× Google's 22bn/min); China >140tn/day (~97bn/min) R. C11 §1.10 · C01
  11. Open weights abroad. How much use is outside China, and does it pay? ≥49.8% (week of 14 Sep 2026) of OpenRouter tokens R, but only ~RMB 13bn (~US$1.9bn) of overseas spend A. C11 §1.11 · C09
  12. Capex intensity. Capex/OCF and server lives versus the US Big-4? ~167% (US Big-4: 96%) in Q2 2026 A; server lives ~3-5 years (US Big-4: 5-6). C11 §1.12 · C01
  13. Export controls. How do they shape the chip mix, and how big is the grey market? Nvidia ~8% (from ~39% in 2025; ~95% in 2022) E; H200 is <1% of Nvidia's DC revenue; ~660k H100e smuggled through 2025 E. C11 §1.13 · C05
  14. Profit pools by layer. Who keeps the profit, 2024–26E? ~US$17bn (West ~US$637bn) positive, and ~US$0bn (net of lab and consumer-AI losses); memory is the largest slice at ~33% A. C11 §1.14
  15. Scenario triggers. What would change the story? Local-government debt sets the state leg, chip controls the ceiling, domestic demand the monetization; Taiwan (~5%) is an overlay. Base 50% · Bull 20% · Bear 30% A. C11 §1.15

How to read this atlas

Every number in the atlas carries three things: where it came from (a provenance badge), how sure we are (a confidence tag, where it matters) and when it was true (a date or fiscal period). Hover or tap any badge to see what it means.

Provenance badges

R
Reported: filed or published by the company or a regulator (10-K, earnings release, ISO auction result).
G
Guidance: the company's own forecast, such as a capex range or next-quarter revenue.
E
Estimate: from a third party (TrendForce, Synergy, IEA, press reports of private-company figures).
A
Atlas estimate: our own number, with the arithmetic shown on the page or in the data file.

Confidence tags

Contested or thinly sourced claims carry conf: high, conf: med or conf: low. A claim the fact-checkers could not trace to any source is marked unverified and never used as a headline. Example: a September report that Anthropic's run-rate passed $100B is unverified, so the atlas uses the last confirmed figure, $65B at end-July E.

Each layer page ends with its low-confidence items. The methodology page lists the claims still open.

Dates and periods

Every page shows an As of date (Edition 2: 25 Sep 2026). Every figure names its period. China figures lead with RMB, converted at 6.70 RMB/US$ for 2026E and at annual averages for past years.

CY2026E
Calendar-year estimate. Fiscal years are shifted to calendar years, which adds about ±5% error.
Q2 FY27
A company's fiscal quarter (for Nvidia, May–Jul 2026).
run-rate
The latest month or quarter, annualized. It is not contracted revenue.

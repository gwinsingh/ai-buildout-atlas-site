The 60-second version

  1. China funds AI with a different stack from the West. Platform cash comes first, the state second and equity markets third. There is no hyperscaler jumbo-bond market, no off-balance-sheet SPV template and almost no GPU-backed private credit A.
  2. 2026 is the year Chinese platform free cash flow turned negative. Alibaba's June quarter burned −RMB 44.7bn and Tencent's Q2 −RMB 13.8bn; with Baidu, the listed three burned −RMB 66.8bn (≈ −US$10bn) in one quarter and spent ~167% of their operating cash flow on capex, against 96% for the US Big-4 R 5155.
  3. Equity is now the marginal funder. Alibaba sold HK$80bn (~US$10.3bn) of new shares, and ~US$33bn of public equity was raised for Chinese AI and AI chips in ten months, most of it on retail-driven STAR and HKEX listings A 5274.
  4. Money is cheap; chips are scarce. China's 10-year yield is 1.67% (UST 5.195%, gap ~350bp) and ByteDance borrows at SOFR+68bp. The binding constraint is what export controls let the money buy, not the price of money R 6159.
  5. The state has become a lead investor, not just a regulator. Big Fund III (RMB 344bn, with the big state banks as shareholders), the RMB 60.06bn National AI fund, which led Moonshot's round and anchored DeepSeek's, and RMB 800bn of policy-bank instruments in 2026 R 36352647.
  6. The risk sits with the state balance sheet and retail investors. Zhipu fell −74% from its peak and MiniMax −77%; local governments hold compute centres financed with 20-year bonds; and two-way investment controls make Chinese AI a closed capital system with a Hong Kong valve.

01 · Explain like I'm newWhere China's AI money comes from

China's AI build-out needs the same inputs as the West's: chips, servers, data centres, power and years of losses at the model labs. What differs is how it is paid for.

China has no deep corporate bond market for its tech giants, which were in regulatory disgrace until 2023. It has no sovereign wealth fund playing SoftBank, and since 2024 it has had almost no US venture money. It does have three things the West lacks: a state that invests directly through layered guidance funds, state banks that lend at policy rates, and an IPO window the regulator can open and close by decree. It opened that window wide for AI and chips from late 2025.

C01 covers how much the platforms spend; C05 the chips; C06 the fabs and memory; C09 the labs' models and revenue. This page covers who funds it, on what terms, and who holds the risk.

Analogy

Picture a town building a stadium when it is not allowed to buy imported steel. The richest families (Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance) pay first out of their savings, and lately by selling a few of their shares. The town government sets up a "stadium fund" that co-invests with anyone who builds locally (Big Fund III, the National AI fund, city funds). The state bank lends to the builders at the lowest rate in the world (policy-bank instruments, 1.67% ten-year government yields). The local steelworks (Huawei, Cambricon, Moore Threads) sell shares to the townspeople at 5–7× their offer price on the first day, then use the money to make more steel. The families also own large parts of the steelworks and of the teams that will play in the stadium (the labs), so the town's money circulates inside the town. Outsiders (US investors) have been told by their own government, and now by the town, to stay out.

The Western loop runs through Nvidia's profits (W02). Nothing plays that role in China: the chip vendors are still capital consumers, and Cambricon is the only one earning large profits (H1 2026 net profit RMB 2.31bn) R 78.

Why can't Alibaba or Tencent just pay cash, like they used to?

From 2023 to 2025 they mostly did. China's AI capex was modest and fit inside the platforms' cash flow from ads, games and shopping.

In 2026 spending jumped: Alibaba's quarterly capex rose +75% and Tencent's +176%. Their core businesses did not grow nearly as fast (Alibaba is also fighting an expensive quick-commerce war). So for the first time both are spending more than they bring in and raising outside money: Alibaba by selling shares, ByteDance by borrowing from banks. None of this is distress. Alibaba still holds RMB 474.5bn of cash and short-term investments R 52.

Definition

Government guidance fund (政府引导基金): a state-funded "fund of funds" that commits capital to private-sector managers or invests directly, usually with a requirement to invest locally and a capped return, so that it "pulls in" (撬动) private money. The Big Fund is the national chip version.

Why it matters now

China's financing layer is going through the same phase change as the West's, in the same year, with different instruments and a different holder of the risk. The state has moved from funding chips (Big Funds I and II) to funding models and compute (the National AI fund in Moonshot and DeepSeek). The domestic listing boom has handed chip designers and labs tens of billions of dollars. And the risk ends up, ultimately, with the state balance sheet and retail equity investors.

The mechanisms, one by one

Guidance funds

政府引导基金

State "funds of funds" with local-investment rules and capped returns. The Big Fund is a joint-stock company owned by the Ministry of Finance, the policy bank CDB, local state capital and, from Phase III, the big state banks. It deploys through specialist sub-funds R 36.

Patient capital

耐心资本

The 2024–26 slogan for long-duration state equity in hard tech. The National VC Guidance Fund runs 20 years (10 investing, 10 exiting), must put ≥70% into seed and early stage, and caps deals at RMB 50m into firms valued ≤RMB 500m. It is not a vehicle for frontier labs R 40.

New policy financial instruments

新型政策性金融工具

Policy banks (CDB, ADBC, EXIM) inject project capital (资本金) into qualifying projects, which then borrow from commercial banks. RMB 500bn in 2025 supported 2,300+ projects with ~RMB 7tn of investment; RMB 800bn in 2026 is expected to support ~RMB 9tn. No AI-specific split is published R 4647.

Local special bonds

专项债

Since the Dec-2024 State Council reform (国办发〔2024〕52号), "compute equipment and ancillary infrastructure" can count as project capital, and the cap rose from 25% to 30%. Local governments can now bond-finance GPU clusters, not just buildings: one root of the idle-compute-centre problem (C04) R 49.

Compute vouchers

算力券

Cities reimburse 30–50% of small firms' compute rental bills, up to ~RMB 1m per firm a year. Budgets are small: Hangzhou RMB 250m a year, Beijing E-Town RMB 100m, Chengdu RMB 10m R 4142.

STAR and HKEX Chapter 18C

科创板 · 18C

STAR (2019) lets loss-making hard-tech firms list, subject to CSRC timing; regulators revived its "fifth set of standards" in 2025–26. HKEX Chapter 18C (2023) admits pre-revenue specialist-tech issuers: 13 Ch. 18C IPOs raised HK$29.8bn in H1 2026, against none in H1 2025 R/E 70.

Dim-sum bonds

点心债

Offshore RMB bonds issued in Hong Kong. Onshore RMB rates are far below USD rates, so they are now the cheapest term debt for Chinese tech: issuance reached RMB 900bn–1tn in 2025, with RMB 1.8tn outstanding (Dec 2025) R/E 58.

Equity-for-compute

China's "circular" deal

A platform invests in a lab; part of the "cash" arrives as cloud credits that come back as cloud revenue. The platform also becomes the lab's model distributor, and often a buyer of the chips its portfolio companies make E 72.

03 · What needs fundingThe bill: about RMB 0.9tn of AI capex in 2026, plus lab losses

05 · The corporate fundersThe platforms: from cash hoards to placements and bank loans

06 · The state's roleThe state capital stack: large on paper, slow to deploy, now leading lab rounds

The headline state commitments add up to well over RMB 1.5tn (~US$220bn+), but they are multi-year commitments, not annual flows, and only a fraction goes to AI compute.

Our estimate of direct state money into the AI build in CY2026 is ~US$15–20bn A conf: low. The state's indirect role through bank credit, IPO approvals and procurement mandates that guarantee chip demand is far larger. The atlas-wide measures in C11: state-directed capex ~19% (~RMB 170bn) of 2026E AI capex, and state funding ~15% direct + SOE (~18% incl. state-bank credit to LGFV/SOE), against ~3% sovereign and enterprise own-build in the West A.

11 · Off balance sheetNo SPVs, no private credit: the local state is China's balance sheet of last resort

China lacks the Hyperion-style SPV market, but it has three functional equivalents A:

  1. Local-government computing-power centres (智算中心). Built by local SOEs or LGFVs with special bonds and policy-bank capital, then leased to platforms, labs and small firms, often with vouchers. The platform's lease obligation is short or nil. The local state keeps the residual-value risk, much as Meta's residual value guarantee keeps it with Meta, except the guarantor is a local government. MIIT puts the national racking rate at 71.4% in mid-2026 (C04); CAICT estimates card-hour utilisation of 36.8% in 2025 E.
  2. Carrier builds. China Mobile, Telecom and Unicom commit ≥RMB 93.5bn (~US$14bn) to compute in 2026 from their own balance sheets. All three are listed SOEs rated at the sovereign level, and they pre-sell hub capacity to platforms, which works like a build-to-suit developer lease.
  3. Third-party IDC with platform anchor leases (GDS/DayOne, VNET, Chindata, Qinhuai; see C04), funded by onshore bank loans, REIT-like vehicles and offshore equity (DayOne raised ~US$2.5bn, per Crunchbase Q2 2026) E 34.

Private credit and GPU-backed lending hardly exist. Onshore bank credit is abundant and cheap (1-yr LPR 3.0%), so private credit has no pricing niche, and offshore private credit avoids China tech because of sanctions and OISP risk conf: med. Restricted Nvidia GPUs cannot be seized and resold freely, domestic accelerators have no liquid secondary market, and banks lend against the parent's credit, not the chips A conf: med. That is a structural reason Chinese neoclouds did not emerge the way CoreWeave did.

15 · BottlenecksEight places the funding chain could pinch

  1. Chips, not money, are the binding constraint. China can raise RMB cheaply but cannot freely buy Nvidia Blackwell or Rubin; H200 purchases are capped per customer and gated by the NDRC (C05). Excess capital chases scarce domestic supply, which explains Ascend price rises of +20–50% (Sep 2026, C01) and 40–90× revenue multiples. The financing layer is over-supplied relative to what it can buy.
  2. FCF has turned negative at Alibaba and Tencent. At the June-2026 run-rate Alibaba's RMB 474.5bn of cash covers ~7 quarters of capex, with no OCF improvement assumed. More placements or debt are likely if capex holds at ~RMB 60–70bn a quarter A.
  3. The IPO window is a regulatory tap. STAR and HKEX approvals (CSRC filing for H-shares) can be slowed at will, as in 2023–24 conf: med. Today's AI financing depends on the CSRC keeping the "fifth listing standard" and 18C channels open.
  4. The lock-up wall. Moore Threads fell 20% limit-down at its 7 Sep 2026 unlock (float +87.7%) R 2. MiniMax (48.9% of shares, 9 Jul) and Zhipu (5.8% in Jul; ~40% in late Jan 2027) face similar walls R 7981; CXMT (Jan 2027) and Enflame (Mar 2027) follow conf: med.
  5. Local-government fiscal stress. Compute centres financed by special bonds and LGFVs sit on a RMB 37tn+ special-bond stock with documented idle funds R 50. A local-debt clampdown would cut the "state compute" leg.
  6. Two-sided investment controls. US OISP/COINS and China's NDRC approvals shut out US primary capital, leaving labs dependent on the state and the platforms. That concentrates risk and may misallocate capital, since the state picks winners R 6366.
  7. HKD/RMB and US rates. A hawkish Fed has widened the UST–CGB gap to near a record R 62, making USD loans relatively expensive and pushing issuers toward dim sum and onshore channels, which suits Beijing's RMB-internationalisation aims.
  8. Accounting opacity. ByteDance is private; Alibaba's fiscal year differs from the calendar year; Tencent's FCF definition deducts content spend; chip prospectuses show heavy customer concentration (Enflame 57% Tencent in 9M 2025) R 21. Data reliability is medium for listed companies and low for private labs and state funds.

16 · Competitive dynamicsSTAR vs HKEX, state vs platforms, and new kinds of investor

  • STAR vs HKEX. Chip designers list onshore on STAR (higher valuations, retail-heavy, CSRC-controlled). Labs list in Hong Kong (VIE and red-chip legacy, foreign investors, the faster 18C route), then seek a STAR secondary listing (Zhipu with CICC/Guotai Haitong from Feb 2026; MiniMax with CITIC from May 2026) to capture onshore valuations R 81. The A+H route is the 2026 fashion: 24 A+H deals raised HK$121.7bn in H1 2026, 58% of HK proceeds E 70.
  • Platforms vs the state as lead investor. In 2023–24 Alibaba and Tencent led the tiger rounds, partly in compute credits. In 2026 state funds lead (Moonshot, DeepSeek) and platforms follow or are pushed out (Alibaba reportedly failed to join DeepSeek conf: low) E 32. The state is diluting the platforms' grip on the labs.
  • Industrial strategics. Handset ODMs (Huaqin, Longcheer), a sensor maker (OmniVision) and a telecom vendor (ZTE) are buying into labs (StepFun) to secure on-device models R 28. This money source has no Western analogue at scale.
  • Gulf capital. Present in MiniMax and Zhipu pre-IPO rounds conf: med and in HK IPO books R 71; a potential swing investor as US pressure on Gulf funds eases or tightens.
  • Disruptor: DeepSeek's self-funding model. High-Flyer's hedge-fund profits financed DeepSeek until 2026, and the founder still supplies ~40% of the first round E 32. It shows a frontier lab can be built without VC, which pressures its VC-funded rivals' valuations.
  • Disruptor: the Nvidia H200 channel. If Beijing loosens H200 approvals, platform capex shifts back toward imported GPUs, and the domestic-chip IPO narrative weakens (C05).

17 · Bull vs bearThe cheapest capital stack in the world, or a policy-made bubble?

Bull case

China has the cheapest, most patient capital stack for AI

  • Sovereign-level funding costs. 1.67% ten-year yields, 2.5% platform 10-year bonds and SOFR+68 loans. No Chinese platform is near Oracle's BBB− position (W02).
  • Low leverage. Alibaba holds RMB 474.5bn of gross cash; Tencent's cash and term deposits still exceed its borrowings. The platforms can fund 2–3 years of negative FCF without stress R 5255.
  • The state absorbs tail risk. Big Fund III (RMB 344bn), the National AI fund, RMB 800bn of policy instruments in 2026 and the RMB 1tn VC guidance fund mean funding does not dry up in a downturn.
  • Equity markets are wide open. ~US$33bn of AI equity raised in ten months, HK IPO proceeds +153%, and CXMT's RMB 57.9bn IPO show deep domestic demand R 7074.
  • Profit is emerging at the chip layer. Cambricon's H1-26 net profit of RMB 2.3bn, MetaX near breakeven and Moore Threads' H1 loss of only RMB 12m suggest the IPO money is turning into real businesses R 278.

Bear case

State-directed capital, retail bubbles and a demand floor made of policy

  • Valuations are disconnected. Zhipu above HK$1tn on ~RMB 0.7bn of 2025 revenue, then −74% in three months; MiniMax −77%; chip designers at 40–90× sales R 91085.
  • Circular, policy-directed demand. Chip revenue relies on platforms under a "buy domestic" mandate (Enflame 57% Tencent); lab revenue on platforms and SOEs. If the mandate relaxes, so do the valuations.
  • Idle state compute and local debt. Special-bond-financed centres with ~70% racking rates and weak rents repeat the 2010s infrastructure overbuild R 50.
  • Capital misallocation. When the state picks winners, market discipline weakens; the Big Fund I era ended in corruption probes conf: med.
  • Platform shareholders are paying. Alibaba's placement (−9.1% on the day) and Tencent's shrinking net cash show they, not the state, fund the bulk of the build; Alibaba Cloud's adj. EBITA is ~RMB 5.6bn on ~RMB 48.4bn of revenue (~12%) R 52.
  • Closed to the world. With US money out and Beijing gatekeeping, a domestic risk-off would have no offsetting foreign bid.

History check conf: med

  • Big Fund I (2014–) built fabs but also funded Tsinghua Unigroup (defaulted in 2020) and the failed Wuhan fab HSMC; senior executives were investigated in 2022.
  • The 2019–20 STAR launch: early listings traded at 100×+ P/E, then de-rated 50–70%. The 2010s LGFV "industrial parks" are the template for today's compute-centre overbuild risk.

What is structurally different

  1. The state is an equity investor and lead underwriter, not just a regulator or LP. Big Fund III has bank shareholders; the National AI fund leads lab rounds; policy banks inject project capital.
  2. Chips, not capital, bind. Western financing strains come from too much buildable demand relative to cash flow. Chinese capital outruns what export controls let it buy.
  3. Debt plays a small role. No jumbo-bond, SPV or GPU-ABS market: less hidden leverage, but less financing flexibility.
  4. Retail sets marginal prices. 1,000–2,300× retail oversubscription and 5–7× day-one pops, against institution-dominated US IPOs.
  5. Circularity is policy-driven. In the West, vendors recycle profits into customers. In China the state funds both the chip vendors and the labs, then mandates that the labs buy from the vendors.
  6. Capital is fenced off. Two-way investment controls make Chinese AI a closed capital system with a Hong Kong valve.

Cross-track pointers

  • Cost-of-capital arbitrage: Chinese platforms borrow at ~2.5–3.1% (RMB) against ~5–6.5% for US hyperscalers. If Chinese AI returns matched Western ones, China's hurdle rate would be ~300bp lower (C11, W11).
  • Gulf capital sits on both sides: MGX, PIF and QIA in Western labs; Abu Dhabi and Saudi money in MiniMax and Zhipu conf: med; Singapore, UAE, Korean and Swiss investors in HK AI IPOs R 71.
  • US institutions still own Chinese AI through HKEX under the public-market exemption (J.P. Morgan AM in Montage) R 71; the COINS regulations due in March 2027 could narrow this.
  • Valuation contagion: Zhipu's round trip from HK$116 to HK$2,980 and back to ~HK$780 in nine months is a live stress test of retail AI enthusiasm, and a leading indicator for any Western AI IPO wave.
  • Western banks partly finance China: Citi and JPMorgan lead ByteDance's US$29.6bn loan, allowed under OISP's bank-lending exemption R 5963.

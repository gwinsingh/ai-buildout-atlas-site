The 60-second version

  1. China will spend about RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) on AI data centres in 2026 A, up ~60% on 2025, which is about one-eighth of the West's ~US$1.0T. Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and Baidu spend ~RMB 630bn of it.
  2. Platforms pay first, the state second, customers third. Of ~RMB 1.03tn flowing through the build, platform legacy cash is ~38%, state and SOE money ~15% (the West: ~3%), and end-customer AI money ~15% A. The platforms are more stretched than the US Big-4: capex is ~167% of their operating cash flow.
  3. The profit pool is thin and roughly zero-sum. Chinese companies earn ~US$17bn of positive AI operating profit in 2026E against ~US$637bn in the West; net of consumer-AI and lab losses it is about zero A. Memory (CXMT) takes the largest slice; chip designers only ~9%.
  4. Follow ¥1 of capex: ~10 fen becomes supplier profit and ~21 fen leaves the country for foreign memory, offshore Nvidia capacity, the pre-ban HBM stockpile and foreign CPUs and switch silicon. In the West ~51¢ of each capex dollar becomes supplier profit A.
  5. Export controls cap quality, not quantity, and Beijing now enforces them on itself. Domestic vendors hold ~80% of 2026 AI-chip value; HBM memory, not power, is the binding constraint. Power is China's advantage: ~550 GW of capacity added in 2025 against 53 GW in the US.
  6. Watch the September-quarter capex and Tencent's prepayment conversion (mid-November), the truce expiries on 9–10 Nov 2026, CXMT's HBM3 ramp (end-2026) and whether agents start earning a take-rate on commerce.

01 · Explain like I'm newChina's build in one picture: paid for by the platforms and the state

China's AI build-out is the same machine as the West's: customers pay apps and model makers, who rent from compute owners, who buy from suppliers, with a financing layer underneath. What differs is who fills the tank. In China it is four internet platforms and the state, not bond markets, and a fifth of what they spend is paid to foreigners.

Read the chain from left to right. Customers (enterprises, government, advertisers, consumers and overseas developers) put in ~RMB 157bn in 2026 A. Most of it goes straight to the Big-4 platforms (Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance, Baidu), which also run the country's largest clouds. The independent labs (DeepSeek, Zhipu, Moonshot, MiniMax, StepFun) are small: ~RMB 16bn of revenue. The compute owners, meaning the platforms, the three state telecom carriers and local-government computing centres, spend ~RMB 0.9tn of capex, which pays suppliers of chips, memory, servers, networking, buildings and power. Underneath, the financing base is mostly the platforms' own cash and state money.

Analogy

Picture a new food hall built by the owners of the country's busiest shopping malls. They pay for the kitchens from the rent their other shops already bring in, and the city builds a second hall next door on cheap loans. The best imported ovens are banned, so the halls use local ovens that burn more gas for the same bread, and a fifth of the fit-out budget still goes on imported parts. The diners mostly eat free, because the owners hand out coupons to get them in the door. The halls are cheap to build and always busy, but almost nobody in the chain makes much money yet.

The same comparison in funding shares. Both tracks are funded upside-down: most of the money entering the build is not paid by AI customers. China replaces the West's bond markets and vendor money with state money and platform balance sheets.

If China builds more cheaply, why does it earn so much less profit?

Profit in a build-out goes to whoever controls something scarce. In the West that is Nvidia's chips (75% gross margin), TSMC's wafers and, in 2026, memory. In China the scarce inputs (HBM memory, 7/5nm wafers, lithography tools) are either banned from import or made by national champions running at a loss while they learn, so the scarcity rent goes to foreign memory makers, offshore hosts and whoever owns old Nvidia stock.

The rest of the chain sells to a handful of very large, state-backed buyers who run tenders and are told to buy domestic. Suppliers compete on volume, not price. And at the customer end, consumer AI is given away for free while models are open-weight and cheap. The result: plenty of spending, thin margins at every step, and losses at the top.

Definition

AI-attributable: only the AI slice of a multi-business company. Tencent's AI ad uplift counts; its games do not. CXMT's memory sold into AI servers counts; the profit it makes from phone makers because AI caused a shortage is a memo line. The money map below is a flow-of-funds (who pays whom in 2026); the profit pools are a P&L view (who earns operating profit).

Why it matters

The China track answers the West's question in reverse. The West's build is profitable at the bottom (chips) and unproven at the top (labs and apps). China's is thin everywhere and loss-making at the top, and it is held together by policy rather than by prices. If you want to know whether China's build is sustainable, ask whether the platforms' ad and commerce cash flows and local-government budgets hold up, not whether customers will pay.

Q4 · Scarcity conf: med–high

Is China's binding constraint chips while the West's is power and memory?

Yes. HBM first; SMIC 7/5nm-class capacity and yields second; then packaging and tool servicing. Power is not binding nationally. CXMT's ~2m HBM stacks in 2026 feed only ~250–300k 910C-class packages E; chip prices rose 20–50% in September.

03 · The three hypothesesThe three hypotheses, tested: two confirmed, one only partly

W11 also set three hypotheses about China. Here is the verdict on each, with the evidence that decided it.

H1 · Spend and compute per dollar

"China spends roughly an order of magnitude fewer dollars but gets more compute per dollar."

Partly confirmed

  • ~7.5× fewer dollars, not 10× A.
  • More physical capacity per dollar: yes. ~3.8× more shell MW and ~2–3× more kWh per dollar.
  • More effective compute per dollar: no. ~1.5–2× more per EFLOPS (US$60–100m vs ~US$45m), because domestic systems use ~2.4× the power per FLOP and HBM is scarce; Ascend is priced near Blackwell parity per FP8 petaflop.
  • China's 2026 domestic AI-chip output is ~1–2% of the US's in B300-equivalents (IFP), ~5–10% on our broader count E.

H2 · A flatter profit pool

"Its profit pool is flatter: no ~75%-margin chip monopolist; open-weight price wars at the model layer."

Confirmed, and stronger

  • Thin and roughly zero-sum. Positive pool ~US$17bn (~2.6% of the West's); net ≈ 0 A.
  • No monopolist: Cambricon's 38.5% net margin is the peak; Huawei shares the market with eight rivals.
  • ~10 fen of each capex yuan becomes supplier profit vs ~51¢ in the West.
  • Nuances: the model-layer price war reversed in 2026 (Zhipu +101%, DeepSeek 2.3–4.5×), and memory makes the pool less flat than it looks.

H3 · The binding constraint

"Its binding constraint is chips (EUV, HBM), not power."

Confirmed, with two refinements

  • HBM is the tightest input, then SMIC capacity, packaging and servicing; power is abundant, cheap and fast.
  • Refinement 1: power is administratively scarce in the east (energy quotas, PUE caps).
  • Refinement 2: the second binding constraint is monetization, not a physical input. Consumer AI is free and enterprise software budgets are small.

04 · MethodHow to read the numbers on this page

This page synthesizes the ten China dossiers (C01–C10, ~165k words) and the five China fact-check logs (543 rows, ~1,600 figures, none judged fabricated) 1112131415. It adds almost no new research. Almost every cross-layer number is an Atlas estimate A: no Chinese company reports "AI profit", ByteDance is private and state deployment is rarely disclosed. Treat layer totals as right in order of magnitude, not precise; error bars are wider than W11's.

  1. Calendar years. Alibaba's March fiscal year is calendarized; ByteDance numbers are press-reported E. CY figures carry ±10–15% error (W11: ±5%), mainly because of ByteDance and the state.
  2. Cash vs profit. The money map is a flow-of-funds; the profit pools are a P&L view. Consumer-AI losses appear as a cash sink in the map and as an operating loss in the pools; lab losses appear only as the equity that covers them.
  3. "AI-attributable" follows W11: only the AI slice of multi-business firms. Power counts electricity sold to AI compute (~40% of DC use); cloud counts AI IaaS, PaaS and MaaS sold to outsiders; apps include the platforms' consumer-AI P&L.
  4. Domestic vs foreign. The pools count China-domiciled companies' profit on China's own build. Foreign suppliers' profit on Chinese demand, and Chinese exporters' profit on the West's build, are memo lines, so X00 can set W11 beside C11 without double counting.
  5. Operating profit is summed; revenue is context, because revenue double-counts along the chain.
  6. Scope. Mainland China plus Hong Kong plus Chinese-controlled capacity abroad (flagged "paid abroad"). "AI capex" (~RMB 0.9tn) includes ~RMB 110bn of general-purpose compute bought alongside AI.
  7. Provenance. R reported · G guidance or official plan · E third-party estimate · A Atlas estimate, with the math on this page or in data/china/C11-synthesis.json.

Where the money comes from

Three things stand out. First, the platforms pay, and increasingly from outside their operating cash: legacy cash, balance-sheet drawdown, most of the debt and Alibaba's placement add up to ~62% of all money in, so the decision to keep spending sits with four companies, one of them private. Second, the state is a funder the West lacks: carriers plus direct state money (~15%) are five times the West's sovereign share (~3%) and need no commercial return 16 §3.1. Third, end-customer money is a tributary (~15%), and ~RMB 60bn of platform cash flows the other way, to consumers, as subsidies, free inference and AI-app marketing 10 §5.1.

Five things the map shows

  1. Funded upside-down, like the West, but by platforms and the state rather than bond markets: ~85% of the money entering is not end-customer AI spend (West ~82%) 16 §3.5.
  2. Less than a tenth becomes operating profit (West ~48%): suppliers are domestic, numerous and disciplined by powerful state and platform buyers; the scarcity rents sit in loss-making national projects or with foreign memory makers 6 §10b.
  3. Nearly a fifth leaks abroad (~RMB 185bn): export controls changed whom China pays, not whether it pays foreigners. The leakage exceeds the domestic accelerator pool (RMB 165bn).
  4. Platforms pay consumers: the ~RMB 60bn consumer-AI cash sink, including ~RMB 9bn of Spring Festival red envelopes 89, is six times what domestic consumers spend on AI.
  5. Labs are small pass-throughs: ~RMB 16bn of revenue and ~RMB 30bn of equity, two-thirds spent on compute rented largely from their own shareholders or the state 9 §5.2.

The exception

DeepSeek shows what a lean Chinese lab can earn. It reports an 82.9% API gross margin (Jan–Jul 2026, leaked) on owned compute and reached a ~US$1bn run-rate by September E 82. Once capacity is full, software-like inference margins are possible in China too.

Why the profit sits where it sits

  1. No profitable domestic chokepoint. China's scarce inputs (HBM, 7/5nm wafers, EUV) are national projects run at a loss (CXMT HBM at ~25% yields; SMIC N+2 at 20–40%) 6 §10b. The scarcity rent goes to foreign memory makers, offshore hosts and owners of Nvidia stock: Tencent resells capacity at >30% profit 21.
  2. Few, big, state-backed buyers run centralized tenders and mandate domestic procurement; Cambricon's top five customers are 88.7% of revenue 5 §4.3.
  3. Open weights commoditize models; pricing power sits with serving efficiency 8 §7.2.
  4. Platforms monetize indirectly through ads, commerce and games, and run consumer AI as a loss leader 10 §5.4.
  5. The state takes strategic returns and pays in power, land and vouchers 1 §7.

Rule of thumb: as in the West, margins fall toward the customer, but the whole curve sits about 40 points lower because there is no 66%-operating-margin Nvidia at the top.

09 · Scarcity migrationScarcity moved from permission to production to memory, and power never became scarce

In the West, the binding constraint moved down the physical stack: chips, then packaging, then memory, power and permits. In China it moved from permission to production: first the right to buy Nvidia, then the ability to make domestic chips, now the memory to feed them. Each year the scarce input set the price and decided who earned the rent. The end-state constraint, willingness to pay, arrives in China earlier than in the West because consumer AI is free and enterprise software budgets are small.

The depreciation wall

AI depreciation already exceeds end-customer AI money in 2026 and, because capex keeps growing faster than old vintages roll off, stays above it through 2030 in the base case; the West shows the same pattern 16 §7.4. With listed-three capex/OCF at ~167% and server lives of 3–5 years, the platforms are running the build on balance sheets and cross-subsidy. The saving grace is the cheap cost of capital (CGB 1.67%) and the state's willingness to own a fifth of the build without a return.

Commitments versus revenue

The West's sharpest version of the gap is >US$1T of lab commitments against ~US$105–140bn of lab run-rate 16 §7.6. China has no equivalent: visible forward commitments are ~RMB 0.2–0.3tn, and the five independent labs' combined run-rate is ~US$4.6bn (Aug–Sep 2026) A 9 §3.2. China's gap is therefore a platform-cash-flow problem, not a counterparty problem: if AI revenue disappoints, capex is cut quickly (as Tencent's was in 2H25), and the losses land on platform shareholders and local governments rather than lenders 2 §10.

22 · The agents thesis"Agents are where fresh money enters": not yet true in China

The atlas tests one thesis on both tracks: "The application layer ultimately depends on agents; that's where fresh money enters the industry, and it's growing very fast." In China, agents are where the tokens go, but not yet where the money comes from.

Sub-claimVerdict for ChinaEvidence
"The application layer ultimately depends on agents"Partly: true for tokens, not yet for moneyChina has 602m gen-AI users 90. Agents drive token growth (national ×1,400 in ~26 months; Zhipu MaaS tokens ×40 since January; the OpenClaw craze) 159 §3.3. But the largest AI monetization, the ~RMB 45bn ad uplift, does not depend on agents, and the biggest agentic bet (Qwen in Taobao, 250m first-time AI shoppers) has no disclosed take-rate 10 §0.
"That's where fresh money enters the industry"Mostly false in 2026Only ~25–35% of China's AI end-revenue is fresh 10 §7.4; end-customer money is ~15% of the RMB 1.03tn of flows; and platforms pay consumers (~RMB 60bn of consumer-AI cash sinks vs ~RMB 10bn of consumer spend). The freshest money is overseas developers and creators (~RMB 13bn).
"It's growing very fast"TrueDirect gen-AI spend ~RMB 12bn → 33bn → 82bn (2024–26E), ~2.5–2.8× a year; independent-lab run-rates up 3–5× in the four months to August 10 §3.49 §3.2.
Implied: agent money will pay for the build-outNot before ~2030 in the base caseThe exit-2026 run-rate (~RMB 210bn) is ~44% of the ~RMB 475bn a year depreciation-based requirement; AI depreciation exceeds end-customer money through 2030.

Verdict

In China, both the engine and the fuel tank sit inside the platforms. The build is fuelled by ad, game and commerce cash flows and by the state, as the West's is by legacy profits and debt. The difference is that China's agents are mostly free products that platforms pay users to try, so the "fresh money" door is narrower than in the West, where coding agents already bring ~US$60bn of paid spend 16 §12. The thesis becomes true in China only if agents earn a share of the transaction (commission on agent-steered commerce, ride-hailing, travel and payments) rather than a subscription. That is plausible, because Alibaba, Tencent and ByteDance own the transaction rails, but it is not yet visible in any filing. Two refinements: the state is a co-payer of agents (tenders, vouchers, "AI+" targets); and China's cheapest agent products (Trae free; coding plans at ¥7.9–49.9 a month) export deflation to the West's app layer.

23 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

Cheap power, cheap capital and a captive market compound

  • Power and buildings never bind. ~550 GW added in 2025, RMB 0.20–0.35/kWh in the hubs, 12-month builds and shells at ~US$3.0m per MW.
  • Capital is cheap and patient. CGB 1.67%, platforms borrow 250–350bp below US peers, and the state owns a fifth of the build without needing a return.
  • Supply is catching up. CXMT HBM3 at end-2026 and SMIC toward ~80k wpm in 2027 unlock Ascend volumes; chip prices are rising, so domestic designers finally earn margins.
  • Demand is real and priced up. >140T tokens a day, token prices +48%/+80%, H100 rents +30%, lab run-rates up 3–5× in four months, and ≥49.8% of OpenRouter's tokens abroad.
  • The transaction rails are owned. If Qwen, Weixin and Doubao agents earn take-rates, customer money reaches ~RMB 1tn by 2030 and capex ~RMB 2.1tn.

Bear case

Thin margins, free products and a fiscal squeeze meet a memory wall

  • The pool is zero-sum. ~US$17bn of positive profit is cancelled by consumer-AI and lab losses; ~21 fen of each capex yuan leaves the country.
  • Consumers do not pay. Platforms spend ~RMB 60bn a year to get users in; ~RMB 97bn of annual losses at two platforms exceed China's direct gen-AI revenue.
  • The platforms are stretched. Capex/OCF ~167%, one quarter of −RMB 66.8bn FCF, a HK$80bn placement; property and deflation squeeze the ad and commerce cash that pays.
  • The state leg is fragile. Card-hour use 36.8%; 4–6-year assets on 20-year special bonds; ~250–350 loss-making centres.
  • Controls tighten. A MATCH-style servicing ban and the truce expiry hit yields; capex falls to ~RMB 750–800bn in 2028–29, with only the state floor holding.

27 · GlossaryTerms on this page

China-specific terms, with the Chinese where it helps. Layer glossaries are on C01–C10 and in the atlas glossary.

AI-induced shortage rent
Profit a memory maker such as CXMT earns from non-AI buyers because AI demand created the shortage.
AIDC
AI data centre; in China often a third-party landlord (GDS, VNET, Qinhuai) or a carrier facility leased to platforms.
Big Fund (国家集成电路产业投资基金, Guójiā Jíchéng Diànlù Chǎnyè Tóuzī Jījīn)
The national chip fund; Phase III RMB 344bn (2024), with the six big state banks as shareholders.
Capex ÷ OCF
Capital expenditure divided by operating cash flow. Above 100% means a company spends more on assets than its operations generate in cash.
City-investment company / LGFV (城投, chéngtóu)
Local-government financing vehicle; owns many "computing-power centres".
CloudMatrix 384
Huawei's rack-scale system of 384 Ascend chips joined by optics: ~1.6× the BF16 compute of an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 but ~2.4× less power-efficient, spread over 16 racks.
Compute voucher (算力券, suànlì quàn) / token voucher (词元券, cíyuán quàn)
City subsidies covering 30–50% of small firms' compute or token bills.
Compute–power coordination (算电协同, suàn-diàn xiétóng)
Siting and scheduling AI load where and when renewable power would otherwise be curtailed.
East Data, West Compute (东数西算, Dōng shù xī suàn)
The 2022 national plan of 8 hub nodes and 10 data-centre clusters that moves compute to cheap western power.
EFLOPS
Exaflops: 1018 floating-point operations per second. China's national intelligent compute was 2,185 EFLOPS (FP16) in June 2026.
Energy-consumption quota (能耗指标 / 能耗双控)
The provincial allowance that gates new large loads; the real constraint on eastern data centres.
Flow-of-funds
A map of who pays whom in a period, including capex and financing, as opposed to a profit-and-loss view.
Funded upside-down
A build-out in which most of the money comes from outside the customers who use the product: legacy profits, the state, debt and equity.
Government guidance fund (政府引导基金, zhèngfǔ yǐndǎo jījīn)
A state fund-of-funds that co-invests with private GPs.
H100e
H100-equivalents: a way to count mixed accelerators by their compute relative to one Nvidia H100.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: DRAM dies stacked and placed beside the processor. Banned for export to China since Dec 2024; the tightest input in China's build.
Intelligent computing centre (智算中心, zhìsuàn zhōngxīn)
An AI-accelerator data centre, often local-government funded.
MaaS
Model-as-a-service: selling model access by the token through a cloud API (Volcano Engine, Alibaba Bailian).
NDRC
National Development and Reform Commission, the state planner; in 2026 it approves each H200 purchase and US investment in AI firms.
New policy financial instruments (新型政策性金融工具)
Policy-bank project capital (RMB 500bn in 2025, RMB 800bn in 2026) that projects lever with bank loans.
Patient capital (耐心资本, nàixīn zīběn)
The policy slogan for long-duration state equity in hard tech.
Rack occupancy (上架率, shàngjià lǜ)
Installed racks ÷ built racks (71.4% nationally, Jun 2026); not the same as accelerator utilization.
Red-envelope war (红包大战)
Platforms paying users cash and free orders to adopt AI apps (~RMB 9bn at Spring Festival 2026).
Special bond (专项债, zhuānxiàng zhài)
A local-government project bond; since Dec 2024 compute equipment can count as project capital.
Supernode / SuperPoD (超节点, chāo jiédiǎn)
A scale-up domain of hundreds to thousands of accelerators (CloudMatrix 384; Atlas 950/960E).
Ten-thousand-card cluster (万卡集群, wàn kǎ jíqún)
A cluster of ≥10,000 accelerators; 52 in China by mid-2026 E.
wpm
Wafers per month, the unit of fab capacity (SMIC's 7/5nm-class capacity is ~45–60k wpm).
Xinchuang (信创, xìnchuàng)
"IT application innovation": the policy-mandated replacement of foreign IT in government and SOEs.

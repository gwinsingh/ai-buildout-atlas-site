The 60-second version

  1. China's AI capex jumped again in 2026 but is still about one-eighth of the West's. We estimate ~RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) of AI-related data-centre capex in China in 2026 A, against ~US$1.0T in the West. The four big platforms alone go from ~RMB 365bn to ~RMB 630bn (+72%).
  2. The spending now outruns the cash. In Q2 2026 Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu spent RMB 131.9bn, ~167% of their operating cash flow, and burned RMB 66.8bn of free cash flow R. The US Big-4 were at 96%. Share placements and bank loans are filling the gap (C02).
  3. The state is a second buyer, not just a referee. Telecom carriers guide ≥RMB 93.5bn of compute capex for 2026 and local governments tendered >RMB 113bn of projects in seven months. Roughly a fifth of the national bill is state-directed and does not need a commercial return.
  4. Export controls shape every yuan. Each US rule change set off a buying wave. Beijing now blocks most Nvidia purchases itself, so ~80% of 2026 chip spending goes to domestic vendors such as Huawei. China pays less per megawatt but roughly 1.5–2× more per unit of delivered compute.
  5. Demand is not the problem; supply is. Daily tokens rose ×1,400 in about two years, Baidu's GPU cloud grew 283% and carrier capacity is pre-sold. The binding limits are domestic chips and memory (HBM), whose prices rose 20–50% in September 2026.

01 · What this layer isThe buyers: who decides to pour the concrete in China

C01 is the "buyers" layer of China's AI build-out: the organisations that decide to buy accelerators, servers and buildings, and the evidence that someone will use what they build.

Every yuan spent here becomes revenue for the supplier layers: power (C03), data centres (C04), chips (C05), fabs and memory (C06), and servers and optics (C07). China's buyers differ from the West's in one big way: the state is a buyer, not only a regulator. There are five kinds of buyer.

Analogy

Think of China's AI build-out as a national high-speed-rail programme with private express operators. The central government draws the network map (the East-Data-West-Compute hubs and the EFLOPS targets). Provincial governments and state carriers pour the concrete and lay the track, sometimes ahead of traffic. Private operators (Alibaba, ByteDance, Tencent) run most of the trains that carry passengers, which here are tokens. Export controls are a ban on importing the fastest locomotives, so the country builds its own (Huawei, Alibaba's T-Head) and squeezes more speed out of older models.

Explain like I'm new: what is "capex", and why does it matter so much here?

Capex (capital expenditure) is money spent on things that last for years: chips, servers, buildings, land. When Alibaba buys RMB 67.7bn of servers in a quarter, all that cash leaves its bank account at once, but the cost reaches its profit statement slowly, as depreciation over three to five years.

In AI, capex is the whole game. Nobody can sell AI answers (tokens) without first buying the machines that produce them. So the capex numbers on this page are the best early signal of how big China's AI industry is trying to become, and who is betting the most on it.

Why it matters

Three questions decide China's AI cycle, and all of them are answered in this layer: (a) whether private platforms keep spending beyond their operating cash flow; (b) whether state-directed capacity actually gets used (71.4% of racks were in use nationally in June 2026 R41); and (c) whether domestic chips can be made in enough volume to absorb the budget.

02 · How it worksA primer on Chinese capex numbers, disclosure and demand signals

What is inside a Chinese "capex" number

Each company counts capex a little differently, and the biggest spender counts nothing in public at all.

  • Alibaba reports "capital expenditures" as purchases of property and equipment plus intangibles and land-use rights, on a cash basis. The June-2026 quarter (RMB 67.7bn) was driven by AI servers, CPUs for agent workloads and "semiconductor component price increases" R12. Management warned the figure is lumpy: "hardware deliveries follow different procurement cycles" R2. Its fiscal year ends 31 March, so the June quarter of 2026 is Q1 FY27.
  • Tencent reports total capex and "operating capex" (servers and data centres, excluding offices and land). In Q2 2026 total capex was RMB 52.8bn and operating capex RMB 51.8bn R1011. Capex is booked on delivery; prepayments are not capex. Tencent also paid ~RMB 51bn of prepayments for future compute in Q2 2026 A1012, a leading indicator of H2-2026 capex.
  • Baidu reports capex on a cash basis and defines free cash flow as operating cash flow minus capex R18.
  • JD splits "development properties" (logistics parks) from "other capex"; only a small part is AI R33.
  • The carriers publish a total capex budget each March with a "computing-power" (算力) share. They count IDC buildings, intelligent-computing servers and backbone upgrades together G36.
  • ByteDance is private and discloses nothing. Its budgets reach the press through the FT, SCMP, Bloomberg and The Information E232426.

Leases, rentals and "hidden" capex

Chinese platforms also lease capacity from third-party data-centre operators (GDS, VNET, Chindata, Qinhuai; see C04). Those rents are operating expenses, not capex. ByteDance and Alibaba have also rented Blackwell-class compute in Southeast Asia through overseas data centres E67. That spending appears in no China capex figure but serves Chinese demand; we treat it as an export-control workaround (section 12).

Why a yuan of capex buys more, and sometimes less, than a dollar

  • Cheaper shells and power. Construction per MW and industrial power in the western hubs cost far less than in the US (C03, C04).
  • Pricier, weaker chips. A Huawei Ascend 950DT listed at >RMB 250k (~US$37k) after the September-2026 price hike E70, close to an H200's ~US$30–35k E56, but with lower delivered performance and a less mature software stack (C05).
  • Result: dollar comparisons understate China's physical build-out (racks, megawatts) but may overstate its effective AI compute. We show both where we can.

Demand signals: no backlog, so watch tokens and pre-sold capacity

Chinese firms disclose almost no backlog; there is no equivalent of Microsoft's US$678bn of RPO. The demand evidence comes from four other places:

  1. Token volumes from the National Data Administration (NDA), ByteDance's Doubao (Volcano Engine), and Qwen and Hunyuan usage multiples R4950515211.
  2. AI revenue lines: Alibaba's "AI-related product revenue" (RMB 12.4bn in the June quarter, its 12th straight quarter of triple-digit growth) R1; Baidu's AI Cloud Infra (RMB 7.3bn, +50%) and GPU cloud (+283%) R1821; Kuaishou's Kling (>RMB 850m, +200%) R30.
  3. Supply statements: "compute demand will continue to outstrip supply" (Alibaba) R2; hub capacity "pre-sold" (China Mobile) R38; Tencent Cloud "has not actively leased out GPU capacity to ensure internal use" R14.
  4. Official capacity statistics (EFLOPS, racks, utilisation) from MIIT and the NDA R414247.

Definition

EFLOPS: 1018 floating-point operations per second, the state's unit of account for compute. MIIT quotes "intelligent computing" (智能算力) at FP16 precision R41. One Nvidia H100 delivers roughly 1 PFLOPS of dense FP16, so 1,000 EFLOPS ≈ 1 million H100-equivalents as a rough rule A. On that rule China's 2,185 EFLOPS (June 2026) is ~2.2m H100-equivalents Low confidence: vendor-rated peaks, mixed precisions, sparse versus dense counting and idle capacity all distort it.

Why compute demand compounds in China

  • Free consumer AI at huge scale. Doubao, Yuanbao, the Qwen app and DeepSeek are free; usage is paid for by ads, e-commerce and super-app engagement (C10). Doubao had ~345m monthly users in March 2026 E29.
  • Agents multiply tokens per task. Tencent's WorkBuddy and CodeBuddy, and Alibaba's agentic shopping (250m users "had their first AI-driven shopping experience through Qwen app's agentic features" R2) burn far more tokens per user than chat.
  • Price wars push volumes up. Token prices in China fell 90%+ in 2024–25 (C08), so volumes grow much faster than revenue.
  • Export controls raise the compute needed per unit of output. Weaker chips and inference-only limits on imported GPUs mean more cards per token served.

What stands out

  • Alibaba's cash flow is weak for reasons outside AI. Its quick-commerce subsidy war (C10) cut operating cash flow to RMB 76.2bn in FY26, −53% y/y R4. The AI bill lands on a smaller cash base; cash and liquid investments fell from RMB 520.8bn (March) to RMB 474.5bn (June) R14.
  • Tencent's net cash fell ~RMB 89bn in one quarter (RMB 146.9bn → 58.2bn) R1013, reflecting compute prepayments, its annual dividend and buybacks. It is still the best funded, with FY2025 free cash flow of RMB 182.6bn R15.
  • Baidu spends the highest share of revenue (36%) because its ad business is shrinking (revenue −4% y/y) while GPU-cloud demand booms R18.
  • ByteDance is the exception. Its 2026 capex is ~12% of revenue A (RMB 200bn ≈ US$30bn against a ~US$250bn revenue run-rate), which is why it can spend the most and stay profitable.

Key insight

China's platforms crossed the "capex above cash flow" line before the US Big-4 did, and at a much smaller absolute scale. The funding gap is being filled by Alibaba's ~HK$80bn placement, ByteDance's ~US$30bn of bank loans and cash drawdowns. How long that can last is the subject of C02.

05 · The national totalAdding it up: ~RMB 0.9tn of AI capex in 2026

The platforms are only part of the bill. Adding telecom-carrier compute, other internet platforms and state or local "computing-power centres" gives ~RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) of AI-related data-centre capex in China in 2026 A, in a range of RMB 0.8–1.0tn Low–med confidence. That is the Atlas canonical figure (C11). It includes ~RMB 110bn of general-purpose or non-AI spend and ~RMB 65bn spent offshore; the "pure" AI figure is ~RMB 0.75–0.8tn (~US$110–120bn). The C01 dossier's own build-up gave RMB 840–980bn, or ~US$125–145bn.

06 · Demand evidenceIs the demand real? Compute, tokens and AI revenue

China discloses no contract backlog, so demand has to be read from official compute statistics, token counts and AI revenue lines. All three point up, and faster than supply.

National compute: 4.5× more by 2030

MIIT puts China's intelligent-computing capacity at 1,590 EFLOPS at end-2025 and 2,185 EFLOPS (FP16) at end-June 2026, with 71.4% of racks in use R4142. That added ~595 EFLOPS in six months, about 2.5× the pace of 2024 A. MIIT's 15th Five-Year ICT plan (September 2026) targets 9,800 EFLOPS by 2030 and RMB 3.8tn (~US$567bn) of information-infrastructure investment in 2026–30 G444546. Getting there from mid-2026 needs ~40% a year for 4.5 years A (~45% if the target is read as end-2029).

  • Where it sits: the East holds 55.9% of intelligent compute, the West 32.6%, Central 10.6% and the Northeast 0.9% R41.
  • 10k-card clusters (万卡集群): 42 in January 2026 R42, 52 by mid-2026 E45.
  • In gigawatts: Moody's estimates China's data-centre capacity at 28 GW at end-2025 against 52 GW in the US, rising to 67 GW against 100 GW by 2030 E72.
  • In chips: 2,185 EFLOPS ≈ 2.2m H100-equivalents; the 2030 target ≈ 9.8m A Low confidence.

Tokens: the fastest-growing series in the China track

National daily token use went from ~0.1tn (early 2024) to >1tn (start of 2025), 30tn (June 2025), 100tn (end-2025) and >140tn (March 2026) per the National Data Administration, +40% in the first quarter of 2026 alone R4950. ByteDance's Doubao models reached 180tn tokens a day in June 2026, up from 63tn at end-2025, "more than 10× in a year" R5152. For scale, Google's first-party API traffic was 22bn tokens a minute (~32tn a day) in Q2 2026 76; Doubao's ~125bn a minute is ~5.7× that, though the definitions differ.

The labs are renters, not buyers

Chinese labs mostly rent compute: DeepSeek (High-Flyer's own clusters plus rentals), Moonshot, Zhipu, MiniMax and StepFun buy capacity from Alibaba Cloud, Volcano Engine, Tencent Cloud, the carriers and GPU-rental firms. Their spending shows up as cloud revenue in C08, not as capex here. A few (Zhipu and MiniMax after their listings) are starting to own clusters (C09). Unlike OpenAI and Anthropic in the West, no Chinese lab has signed multi-hundred-billion compute commitments; the concentration risk sits inside the platforms themselves.

Key insight

The largest buyer in China (ByteDance) spends the least relative to its revenue (~12%) and discloses nothing. The most transparent buyers (Alibaba, Baidu) are the most stretched, at 25–36% of revenue and ~3× their operating cash flow. Public filings therefore overstate the strain on the system as a whole and understate its size.

09 · Business modelsHow Chinese buyers expect to earn a return

C01 companies spend; they earn the return in other businesses or other layers. There are eight distinct mechanisms, and they differ sharply from the Western model of selling tokens and seats to outside customers.

1. Cross-subsidy from a cash-cow core

~RMB 10.5bn/qtr

The dominant model. Tencent's games and ads, Douyin's ads and Taobao's take rates pay for free AI. Tencent reports a ~RMB 10.5bn quarterly "drag" from new AI products, funded by core profit +19% R11; the Doubao app reportedly earned under RMB 1m a day while serving 200m+ daily users E51.

2. Ad-tech uplift

+22% y/y

The most measurable return so far: Tencent's marketing services grew 22% to RMB 43.6bn in Q2-26 R10. Like Meta in the West (W10), but Chinese firms do not break out the AI-driven share.

3. Selling scarce compute

+283% · >30%

In a supply-constrained market owning GPUs pays at once: Baidu's GPU cloud +283% R21; Tencent could resell pre-ordered compute at >30% profit and recover depreciation "almost immediately" R1112; ByteDance allegedly sold part of its Nvidia stock to rivals E64. An arbitrage created by export controls.

4. Model-as-a-service, cheaply

RMB 16bn → 30bn

Alibaba's MaaS ARR is >RMB 16bn, targeting RMB 30bn by end-2026 G2; Seedance ARR ~US$2bn E51; Kling >RMB 850m a quarter R30. Price wars keep margins thin: Doubao priced at ~38% of leading overseas models E29.

5. Agentic commerce and super-apps

250m users

Alibaba routes Qwen-app agents into Taobao shopping (250m first-time "AI shopping" users R2); Tencent embeds agents in Weixin and enterprise tools (WorkBuddy, CodeBuddy). The return shows up as GMV, take rate and engagement (C10).

6. Owning the chip

~381k cards

Alibaba's T-Head (650+ customers, ~265k cards in 2025) and Baidu's Kunlunxin (~116k) R2E53 capture the margin Western hyperscalers pay Nvidia, and sell chips to third parties. Each yuan of capex partly recycles into the group's own chip revenue.

7. State-mandated returns

RMB 52.9bn

Carriers earn compute-service revenue (China Mobile RMB 52.9bn in H1-26, +14%; intelligent computing RMB 5.3bn, +130%) R3740. Local governments seek jobs, tax and "AI+" adoption, filling centres with subsidised power, land and compute vouchers (C02). Commercial ROIC is secondary.

8. Stockpiling as treasury management

+20–50%

Buying ahead of each export-control step (H20 in Q1 2025, prepayments in 2026) is a bet on scarcity. When controls tighten, stock appreciates; when domestic chip prices rise (+20–50% in Sep-2026 E70), early buyers win again.

Analogy

A shopping mall that gives away cinema tickets to keep shoppers in the building. The cinema (Doubao, Yuanbao, the Qwen app) loses money on every seat; the mall (ads, e-commerce, games) earns it back on everything else the visitors do.

Where the Western model differs

In the West, capex is increasingly underwritten by external contracted revenue: ~US$2.46T of backlog, ~43% owed by OpenAI and Anthropic 76. In China, capex is underwritten by internal ecosystems plus the state. That means less counterparty risk, but also a weaker external price signal for whether the compute is worth what it costs.

Where RMB 1 of capex ends up as supplier profit

For every yuan China spends on AI capex, domestic suppliers keep only ~10 fen as operating profit, and ~21 fen is paid abroad, of which ~8 fen is foreign suppliers' margin A (C11). In the West, suppliers keep ~51¢ of every capex dollar, most of it at Nvidia and the memory makers. China's build-out is a low-margin affair for its suppliers, which is the flip side of the state's push for domestic chips.

Payback and returns

  • Alibaba: Eddie Wu says AI investments should be recouped within three years at current gross margins G6. AI-related product revenue runs at ~RMB 49.5bn a year R2 against a capex run-rate of RMB 155bn over the last four quarters A, about 3× revenue.

  • Baidu: AI Cloud Infra revenue RMB 7.3bn in Q2 against capex of RMB 11.4bn R18; GPU cloud is the fastest-returning use.
  • Carriers: China Mobile's intelligent-computing revenue (~RMB 10.6bn annualised A) is small against ≥RMB 50bn of compute capex; the returns are strategic.
  • The system view (C11): China's AI capex is ~5.7× its end-customer AI money in 2026 (West ~5×); depreciation reaches ~RMB 220bn in 2026E with servers depreciated over only ~3–5 years; paying back capex through 2026 needs ~RMB 475bn a year of end-customer revenue, ~2.3× the exit-2026 run-rate A.

Token economics: huge volumes, tiny revenue

Doubao's 180tn tokens a day is ~66 quadrillion tokens a year. At a blended RMB 0.5–2 per million tokens (Chinese list prices after the price wars, with much of the volume internal or free), their "list-price value" is ~RMB 33–130bn a year, against ByteDance's >RMB 200bn of 2026 capex A Low confidence. National daily tokens (140tn) at the same prices imply ~RMB 25–100bn a year. The gap between token volumes and token revenue is the core monetisation question for C08–C10.

A cap of 75,000 per customer was reported as under consideration in April and was in force by August, when deliveries had reached only ~13% of it (~10k each to ByteDance and Tencent; C09). Beijing let ~10 firms prepare orders with domestic-purchase quotas E60, then capped approvals at under 200k against >400k requested, for training only E55. Result: H200 sales to China were <1% of Nvidia's US$89bn data-centre revenue in the July-2026 quarter, Nvidia guides zero China data-centre revenue, and it took a US$400m H200 inventory charge R5758.

The result: the chip mix flipped to domestic

IDC counted ~4m AI accelerator cards shipped in China in 2025: Nvidia ~2.2m (55%), domestic vendors 41%, with Huawei ~812k, T-Head ~265k, Kunlunxin ~116k and Cambricon ~116k E53. Bernstein estimates Nvidia's 2026 share of China's AI-chip market at ~8% by value (from ~39% in 2025 and ~95% in 2022), with Huawei ~50% (~US$12bn) E54 Medium confidence. Domestic vendors take ~80% of 2026 value (~56% merchant plus about a quarter in-house chips).

The offshore loophole

Asia Times reports ByteDance and Alibaba used remote Blackwell capacity in Southeast Asia built with servers exported by Inspur's US affiliate Aivres (~US$3bn of Blackwell systems among US$5.6bn of exports, April 2024 to February 2026) E67. A separate report cites a ~US$2.5bn deal for 36,000 B200s routed via Malaysia E56 Low confidence, unverified. Epoch AI estimates ~660k H100-equivalents of smuggled Nvidia compute in China through 2025, about a third of its AI compute (C05). The May-2026 Trump–Xi summit left Blackwell banned E69, and the 24–25 September Xi visit to Washington produced no chip deal, only an AI-incident hotline E68.

13 · Profit captureWho earns the profit when a Chinese platform spends a yuan

Very little of it stays with anyone. China's positive AI operating-profit pool is ~US$17bn in 2026 against ~US$637bn in the West, and net of lab and consumer-AI losses it is roughly zero A (C11).

  1. Memory is the choke point, and CXMT is the winner. Rising DRAM and HBM prices move profit to memory makers, as in the West (where memory is ~23% of the AI profit pool 76). CXMT's progress on HBM (C06) decides how much of that stays in China.
  2. Domestic chip vendors are the new profit pool, but a small one. Huawei's AI-processor revenue is estimated at ~US$12bn in 2026 E5466, and Cambricon, Hygon and MetaX gained pricing power (+20–30% in Sept 2026 E70). In the West ~88% of chip-level gross profit goes to Nvidia 76; in China that pool is redistributed to Huawei, Cambricon and the platforms' own chip arms. The C11 synthesis puts domestic designers at only ~9% of the pool (West W05: ~44%). The C01 dossier's earlier rough split had accelerators at ~35–40%; the canonical C11 view, which sizes each supplier's actual profit, supersedes it.
  3. Nvidia's China profit pool has almost vanished. China was ~13% of Nvidia revenue in FY25 E63; H200 sales in the July-2026 quarter were <1% of data-centre revenue and Nvidia guides zero R57. Its US$400m H200 inventory charge shows the stranded cost.
  4. The buyers themselves earn thin margins. Alibaba Cloud's EBITA margin is ~12% R2 (AWS: 39.4%); Alibaba's net income fell 75% R1; Baidu's non-GAAP net margin is 8% R21; ByteDance's net margin fell to 16.7% E26. As in the West, the buyers are transferring profit to suppliers during the build phase.
  5. Server makers and data-centre operators (C04, C07) earn volume at low margins; the state absorbs part of the power cost (C03).
  6. The state captures strategic value, not profit. Carriers and local governments accept low returns in exchange for self-sufficiency and industrial upgrading.

15 · Competitive dynamicsA capex race among four giants, refereed by the state

  • Four very different racers. ByteDance has the most tokens and the most cash; Alibaba the most cloud, its own chips and the biggest public plan; Tencent the most distribution (Weixin), the slowest start and now the fastest-growing capex; Baidu is the smallest and the most exposed to GPU-cloud resale. Each quarter's capex now moves share prices: Alibaba fell ~5% on its June-quarter print as profit dropped 75% E3.
  • Domestic chips as a weapon. Owning a chip line (T-Head, Kunlunxin) lets a platform buy compute at cost and sell chips to rivals. Tencent and ByteDance have no merchant-scale chip of their own and depend on Huawei and Cambricon, whose prices rose in September 2026 E70. We expect more in-house silicon announcements Medium confidence.
  • Supernodes change the unit of purchase. Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 and successors, Alibaba's 500,000-card V900 scale-out claim G6 and the MIIT plan's call for "ten-thousand-card and hundred-thousand-card" clusters G46 shift purchases from cards to racks and pods (C05, C07).
  • Efficiency as a disruptor. DeepSeek-style efficiency (mixture-of-experts, low-precision training, distillation) lets Chinese labs serve ~1,400× more tokens on ~3–4× more compute. A further efficiency jump could slow capex growth; so far, Jevons-style demand growth has absorbed every gain.
  • The state as a swing buyer. The 9,800-EFLOPS target G44 and the reported RMB 2tn computing-grid programme E66 guarantee a floor of demand for domestic chips.
  • Overseas build-outs. Alibaba (the 20 GW "global" target G6) and ByteDance are building in Southeast Asia and the Middle East (C04). Overseas sites can legally use more capable hardware, subject to US rules on Chinese-headquartered firms.
  • Sovereign and enterprise demand. "AI+" action plans push SOEs, banks and local governments to deploy private models, often DeepSeek or Qwen appliances on domestic chips. It is fragmented, low-utilisation demand, but adds volume for Huawei and the server makers.

16 · Bull vs bearUnder-spending with cheap inputs, or overbuilding on weak chips?

Bull case

China is under-spending relative to demand, and has the cheaper inputs

  • Demand compounds faster than supply. National tokens rose from 30tn to 140tn a day in nine months R4950; Doubao hit 180tn R51; Baidu's GPU cloud grew 283% R21; China Mobile's hubs are pre-sold R38; Alibaba sees demand above supply to 2030 R2.
  • Capex is small next to the economy and the West. ~US$134bn vs ~US$1.0T A76; ByteDance spends only ~12% of revenue. There is room to double again.
  • Cheap power and state support. China adds far more generation than the US and has no grid moratoria (C03). The state guarantees demand for domestic chips.
  • Domestic chips are ramping. Huawei, Cambricon, T-Head and Kunlunxin went from ~34% (2024) to 41% (2025) of units and ~80% of 2026 value E435354. Chip margins stay at home.
  • Monetisation levers are real. Ads +22% at Tencent, cloud +45% at Alibaba, MaaS ARR on course to nearly double (RMB 16bn → 30bn target) R210.

Bear case

Cash-flow strain, weaker chips and state-driven overbuild

  • Capex already exceeds cash flow. ~167% of OCF for the listed three in Q2-26 A; Alibaba FCF −RMB 44.7bn in a quarter R1; Tencent's net cash fell ~RMB 89bn in a quarter R1013. Placements and bank loans are now needed.
  • Token revenue is tiny. Free apps, price wars and low willingness to pay mean token volumes run far ahead of revenue (section 10).
  • The export-control tax. China pays ~1.5–2× more per unit of delivered compute A; HBM shortages and 20–50% chip price hikes E70 eat into each yuan.
  • State overbuild and idle capacity. 71.4% rack utilisation R41 hides very low use in some local centres (C04); 9,800 EFLOPS by 2030 may produce capacity ahead of demand, in the wrong places, on chips that age quickly.
  • Policy whiplash. Every cycle of US or Chinese rules has stranded inventory (Nvidia's US$4.5bn and US$400m charges R6157) and made capex lumpy.

Digestion or acceleration? Our read, September 2026

Acceleration, supply-constrained. Every major buyer raised plans in 2026, carriers are adding compute capex mid-year and chip prices are rising. The brake is supply (domestic chips, HBM), not demand or power. The risk to watch: if Alibaba's H2 capex steps down sharply (management said spending "will not be linear") while Tencent's prepayments convert, the total could still grow, but the market would read it as digestion. The Atlas scenarios put 2027 China AI capex at ~RMB 1.1tn (~US$164bn) in the base case (bull RMB 1.3tn, bear RMB 0.95tn), with probabilities of 50/20/30 (C11).

What is structurally different

  1. The state is a buyer. ~19–21% of China's AI capex comes from carriers, local governments and SOE platforms that need no commercial return; the West has no equivalent outside sovereign projects in the Gulf.
  2. No external-lab backlog. Western capex is underwritten by trillion-dollar commitments from OpenAI and Anthropic; Chinese capex by the platforms' own ecosystems.
  3. Silicon is domestic, cheaper per card but more expensive per FLOP. Export controls redirect the chip profit pool to Huawei and in-house chips and impose a performance tax.
  4. Power is abundant; chips and HBM are scarce. The binding constraints are the inverse of the US.
  5. Free consumer AI dominates usage. Token volumes rival the West's, but revenue per token is far lower.
  6. Capex is lumpier. Export-control cycles and Beijing's purchase guidance create stockpile-then-pause patterns.
  7. Disclosure is thinner. The largest spender is private and nobody discloses a backlog, so demand is harder to verify.

20 · GlossaryTerms on this page

Capex (capital expenditure) 资本开支
Cash spent on long-lived assets such as servers, chips, buildings and land. Chinese platforms report it on a cash basis.
Operating capex (Tencent)
Capex for servers and data centres, excluding office buildings and land.
Compute prepayment
Cash paid to suppliers before hardware is delivered; it becomes capex on delivery. Tencent paid ~RMB 51bn in Q2 2026.
OCF / FCF
Operating cash flow / free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capex).
RPO / backlog
Remaining performance obligations: revenue a company has contracted but not yet recognised. Chinese platforms disclose none of note; Western clouds hold ~US$2.46T.
EFLOPS / PFLOPS
1018 / 1015 floating-point operations per second. China's official statistics quote intelligent compute in EFLOPS at FP16.
FP16
16-bit floating-point precision, the usual basis for AI-compute statistics in China.
H100-equivalent
A rough unit that converts compute into the number of Nvidia H100 GPUs that would deliver it; 1,000 EFLOPS (FP16) ≈ 1m H100-equivalents.
Intelligent computing 智能算力
AI-oriented compute (GPUs and NPUs), as distinct from general-purpose CPU compute (通用算力).
Intelligent computing centre 智算中心
A data centre built mainly for AI, often government- or carrier-funded; also called a computing-power centre.
East-Data-West-Compute 东数西算
The 2022 national programme placing data centres in eight hub regions (Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Gansu, Guizhou, Chengdu-Chongqing, Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, Yangtze River Delta, Guangdong-HK-Macau) and routing eastern workloads west.
National integrated computing network 全国一体化算力网
The successor concept linking the hubs with high-speed "compute corridors" (70+ built).
10k-card cluster 万卡集群
A training cluster with at least 10,000 accelerators; 52 existed by mid-2026.
Rack utilisation 上架率
Share of built racks actually installed with equipment; 71.4% nationally in June 2026. Not the same as accelerator usage.
Tokens 词元
Units of text processed by a model; the National Data Administration adopted "词元" as the official Chinese term in March 2026.
MaaS
Model-as-a-service: selling model access by the token (Alibaba Bailian/Model Studio, Volcano Engine Ark, Baidu Qianfan).
AIDC
AI data centre: carrier and operator term for facilities built for AI accelerators.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: DRAM chips stacked beside the processor to feed it data fast. Controlled for export to China since December 2024; the tightest input for Chinese accelerators.
H20 / H200
Nvidia's China-compliant (H20) and licensed-with-levy (H200) data-centre GPUs. Blackwell (B200/B300) remains banned for China.
Ascend, Zhenwu, Kunlun, MLU
Leading domestic AI accelerators: Huawei Ascend 910C/950, Alibaba T-Head Zhenwu M890/V900, Baidu Kunlunxin's Kunlun, Cambricon MLU 690 (C05).
Supernode
A rack-scale system that links hundreds of accelerators as one machine, such as Huawei's CloudMatrix 384.
CAC / MIIT / NDRC / NDA
Cyberspace Administration of China; Ministry of Industry and Information Technology; National Development and Reform Commission; National Data Administration.
LGFV
Local-government financing vehicle: a company owned by a local government that borrows to fund infrastructure.
RASA
Remote Access Security Act: a US bill requiring licences for remote (cloud) access to controlled chips; passed the House in January 2026.
Stockpiling cycle
Pull-forward of chip purchases ahead of an expected export-control change, followed by a pause.

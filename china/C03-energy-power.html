Energy & power

America has the chips and lacks the power. China is the mirror image: it adds about ten Americas' worth of generating capacity a year, sells western-hub electricity at 3–5 US cents, and connects a data center in months. Its binding constraint is chips, and it is using cheap power to work around them.

The 60-second version

  1. China builds power at a scale nobody else does. It added ~550 GW of capacity in 2025, about ten times the US's 53 GW, and is still adding 50 GW or more of coal and gas a year as backup R 24916. Installed capacity passed 4,100 GW in August 2026 5.
  2. AI is a small part of China's electricity story. Computing centers used 170 TWh in 2025, 1.6% of national demand R 18. Data services supplied only ~7% of demand growth in Jan–Aug 2026; EV charging supplied twice as much A 14. The official forecast of ~800 TWh by 2030 is ~3× independent estimates.
  3. Western power is cheap; eastern power is rationed by permit. Hub data centers in Inner Mongolia and Ningxia pay RMB 0.20–0.35/kWh (3.0–5.2 US¢), a third to a half of the US industrial average. Shanghai and Guangdong pay RMB 0.6–1.0 and face energy quotas and efficiency surcharges E 3135.
  4. Power is being used as chip policy. Three provinces reportedly cut power bills by up to 50% for data centers that use domestic chips E 36. Our math: that subsidy roughly cancels the ~2.4× power penalty of Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 against Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 A 4181.
  5. The profit goes to equipment makers and the state, not to generators. Three of the four listed Big Five generators saw H1-26 profits fall 18–29% as tariffs fell. Grid-equipment makers boomed: NARI's overseas revenue rose 197%, and transformer exports hit a record RMB 64.6bn R 666769.
  6. Verdict: power is China's strategic advantage; chips are the binding constraint. China will have >24 GW of AI data-center capacity by end-2026 against ~56 GW in the US E 30. The power advantage is real but is being spent on coal, idle western capacity and inefficient chips.

01 · Explain like I'm newWhat this layer is: the electrons behind China's AI

Every AI chip is an electric heater that also does maths. This layer is everything upstream of the data center's fence: the power plants, the two state-owned grid companies, the ultra-high-voltage lines that carry power 2,000 km from the deserts to the coast, the tariffs that price it, and the officials who decide where a data center may be built and how much energy it may use.

In the US, hyperscalers bargain with utilities, independent power producers and grid operators, and wait years in queues. In China nearly every node is state-owned or state-directed. NDRC sets transmission tariffs and capacity payments; the NEA approves plants and publishes the statistics; provincial governments hand out energy-consumption quotas and price concessions; and two monopolies, State Grid (~88% of the country, 1.1bn people) and China Southern Grid, move the power 49. That makes China's power system fast and cheap to extend, and also a lever the state can pull to steer where AI runs and which chips it runs on.

Analogy

American AI companies are restaurants in a city with a water shortage: plenty of chefs (chips) and cash, but a years-long wait for a bigger water main (the grid) and a reservoir that can't be built fast enough (gas turbines, nuclear). China is the opposite. It builds huge reservoirs and aqueducts every year, and water in the west is so cheap it is sometimes poured away (curtailment). What it lacks is professional stoves: advanced chips, restricted by US export controls. So Chinese AI kitchens run more, less efficient stoves, and the government pays them to move next to the reservoirs in Inner Mongolia and Ningxia.

Power versus energy: why "GW" and "TWh" are different things

Power (GW) is the size of the pipe; energy (TWh) is how much water flows through it. One gigawatt running flat out for a year delivers 8.76 TWh. A solar farm has a big pipe but flows only when the sun shines, so China's 1,299 GW of solar produced only ~11% of its electricity in 2025 4.

This matters for AI because data centers draw power around the clock. A 1 GW AI campus running at 85% needs ~7.4 TWh a year. China's entire 2025 computing-center consumption of 170 TWh equals about 19 GW of round-the-clock draw A.

The single most useful way to hold this layer in your head is as the mirror image of the American one. The table of meters below compresses the whole page.

Why it matters

If power is not China's constraint, then the size of China's AI build-out is set almost entirely by chips (C05) and by how well the western hubs are used (C04). Cheap, fast power also changes the economics of inefficient domestic chips: a watt-hungry accelerator is far less of a handicap when the watts cost half as much.

02 · How it worksA primer on China's power system

Nameplate overstates firm supply, but China still has a healthy margin

China's 4,103 GW of nameplate capacity is 2.6× its record 1,560 GW peak load 513. That ratio flatters, because ~1,990 GW of it is wind and solar, which contribute little at the evening peak. What matters is firm capacity: what can be relied on when demand is highest.

We derate each fuel by how reliably it shows up at peak A: thermal 1,492 GW × 0.90, nuclear 62 × 0.95, conventional hydro 382 × 0.65, pumped hydro 66 × 0.9, battery storage 145 × 0.5, biomass 47 × 0.8, wind 640 × 5%, solar 1,202 × 2%. That gives ~1,876 GW at end-2025, and ~1,925 GW after ~50 GW of 2026 thermal additions: a ~20–25% margin over the 1,560 GW peak. That is healthy, comparable to or better than US regional margins of ~15%, but it is not the "80–100% reserve margin" sometimes quoted in Western press 59 conf: med on derating factors. China is not short of power, but it is not wasting half its firm fleet either. That is why it keeps building 50–100 GW of coal and gas a year.

Five ways a Chinese data center buys electricity

  1. Grid agency purchase (电网代理购电). The default for users who don't trade: the grid company buys for them and publishes a monthly price per province.
  2. Direct market trading. Annual and monthly bilateral contracts through provincial trading centers, plus spot markets. Market-traded power was 6,639 TWh in 2025, 64% of consumption; spot was 31.1% of market volume R 4.
  3. Green-power trading (绿电交易) and green certificates. 329 TWh of green power traded in 2025 (+38%) and 930m certificates changed hands (+100%) 418. This is how hyperscalers claim their green shares: Tencent reported >4 TWh (48.5% of its use) and Alibaba >3 TWh (34%+) 20.
  4. Green-power direct connection (绿电直连). A dedicated line from a wind/solar (and storage) plant to the load. Allowed for single users by NDRC/NEA Doc. 650 (2025) and for multi-user projects from 20 May 2026, with computing facilities prioritized. Projects must self-consume ≥60% of their own output, and self-generation must cover ≥30% of the load (≥35% before 2030) 42. Nuclear direct supply to computing centers is now being "explored" 44.
  5. Source-grid-load-storage integration (源网荷储一体化). Park-level microgrids that combine generation, storage and load. Ulanqab commissioned the first "green direct + integration" data-center project in July 2025 19.

East Data, West Compute, and its 2026 upgrade

East Data, West Compute (东数西算, launched Feb 2022) designated 8 national hub nodes and 10 data-center clusters: Inner Mongolia (Horinger), Ningxia (Zhongwei), Gansu (Qingyang), Guizhou (Gui'an), Chengdu–Chongqing, Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (Zhangjiakou), the Yangtze River Delta and Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macau (Shaoguan) 31. The idea: run latency-tolerant work (training, batch inference, storage) in the west, where power is cheap and green, and serve latency-sensitive traffic from the east. A "20 ms latency circle" between hubs was declared basically complete in March 2026 31.

Compute–power coordination (算电协同) is the upgrade. It entered the 2026 Government Work Report for the first time and the 15th Five-Year Plan outline 19: co-plan data centers with power plants and grids, and schedule compute where and when green power is abundant. China Telecom ran the first production cross-region compute–power dispatch in July 2025 and moved a 100-card workload 800 km from Shanghai to Fujian in December 2025; China Mobile reports >80% green power at its parks 43. Insiders say a national dispatch system "may not be complete even in five years", because power dispatch and compute scheduling sit in different bureaucracies 43.

The policy anchors: the Data Center Green & Low-Carbon Development Action Plan (NDRC, MIIT, NEA, National Data Bureau, Jul 2024) requires ≥80% green power for new data centers at national hub nodes, with 100% in some western pilots, tiered electricity prices for inefficient data centers, and a national average PUE below 1.5 by end-2025 34194333. In April–May 2026 four departments issued an action plan on "AI and energy mutual empowerment" with 29 tasks, including 算电协同 and exploring nuclear and hydrogen direct supply 4344.

03 · Market sizeHow much China builds, generates and uses

China adds about ten times as much generating capacity per year as the US, and it is still adding firm coal and gas alongside record solar.

Ten Americas a year

In 2025 China added ~550 GW R: solar 315–318 GW, wind ~119–120 GW, coal ~71 GW, gas ~20 GW, hydro ~12 GW (conf: low; not stated in the primary releases) and ~1.6 GW of nuclear, plus 66 GW of new-type battery storage that the NEA does not count as generation 123472. The US added 53 GW, its most since 2002, and plans a record 86 GW in 2026 RG 16. One Chinese year of additions is more than 40% of the entire US fleet.

  • 2026 is a solar "air pocket", not a slowdown in firm build. Solar additions fell 61% in Jan–Jul 2026 because developers rushed to connect before the Doc. 136 market-pricing cutoff (1 Jun 2025), pulling demand forward. Thermal additions rose 20% to 50.3 GW, the most in 15 years E 9. Wind additions to August were 53.3 GW (−7.9%) 6.
  • The coal trackers disagree. NEA-based statistics show 71 GW of coal plus 20 GW of gas in 2025 4; Global Energy Monitor counts 78 GW of coal (80% of global additions) 12; CREA/GEM counts 95 GW "added to the grid", with 83 GW of construction starts, 161 GW of new proposals and a 291 GW pipeline 11. The gap comes from unit-size cutoffs, commissioning versus grid-connection dates, and whether captive industrial plants count.
  • Milestone: by mid-2026, solar capacity (~1,275 GW) had drawn level with coal capacity 8.

Generation: coal capacity up, coal output down

China generated 10,420 TWh in 2025 on the NEA/NBS basis (+5.2%; the CEC's "full-caliber" total is 10,600 TWh), about 2.5× the US R 42. The striking fact is that coal generation fell 1.9% even as coal capacity rose ~6% 412. New coal plants are increasingly capacity, standing by for the evening peak and dry years, rather than energy. Coal utilization is ~51% and could fall to ~42% by 2030 (CREA) 11. China pays for that standby through regulated capacity payments (>RMB 100bn in 2024), not auctions.

Utilization hours are falling across the board: −312 hours in 2025 and a further −163 hours in Jan–Aug 2026 5. China is building supply faster than demand, which pushes wholesale prices down and curtailment up. In 2026 clean power has covered all of demand growth: coal-fired generation fell 5.2% y/y in August 2026, the second monthly decline in a row (coal mining output fell 7.7%) 9, and thermal generation fell 3.5% in July 15.

Demand: steady growth, and AI is one driver among several

National consumption was 10,368 TWh in 2025 (+5.0%) and 7,173 TWh in Jan–Aug 2026 (+4.3%) R 414. Peak load set a record of 1,560 GW in August 2026, 49.7 GW above the previous maximum and about twice the US peak R 13. The CEC expects 10,900–11,000 TWh (+5–6%) in 2026 G 4. The 14th Five-Year Plan (2021–25) averaged 6.6% a year 2; one year of Chinese demand growth (~500 TWh) is bigger than Germany's or the UK's entire annual consumption 59.

Key insight

In China, AI is one growth driver among several; in the US, it is the growth driver. Internet data services added ~21 TWh of the ~296 TWh national increase in Jan–Aug 2026 A 14. In the US, data centers are expected to supply roughly half of 2025–30 load growth (W03; conf: med). Chinese AI load lands on a grid that already grows by a Germany every year.

Why the official forecast is ~3× the independent ones:

  1. Definitions. The NEA's 170 TWh "computing-power center" base is ~1.6× the IEA's 2024 data-center estimate.
  2. Policy intent. The official number describes a 15th Five-Year Plan target (national computing-power goals, "AI+" programs), not a demand forecast built from chip supply.
  3. Chip efficiency. If most growth runs on domestic accelerators drawing ~2× the power per FLOP, the same compute needs more TWh. The IEA's 2025 model assumed a global efficiency curve.
  4. Token explosion. Daily token calls reportedly rose from ~0.1 trillion (early 2024) to ~100 trillion (end-2025) and >140tn/day (Mar 2026) 19, inference growth that 2024-vintage models did not foresee (see C01).

Our base case: chips, not electrons, set the ceiling

We expect ~400–550 TWh by 2030 on the broad NEA definition A conf: low, or ~46–63 GW of average load (400–550 ÷ 8.76). The official 800 TWh needs ~91 GW of average draw (800 ÷ 8.76), roughly 3.7× today's ~24 GW of AI data-center capacity once utilization is counted 30. That would require domestic chip output to scale by an order of magnitude (C05). Even then, 800 TWh is less than one year of China's new solar additions in energy terms.

Sizing China's compute fleet in gigawatts

  • Racks. In-use standard racks totaled 10.85m by Jun 2025 (MIIT), at a national average PUE of 1.42 R 27. At the standard 2.5 kW per rack that is ~27 GW of IT nameplate, or ~38.5 GW of facility capacity A.
  • Energy check. 170 TWh ÷ 8,760 h ≈ 19.4 GW of average draw A, about 50% of facility nameplate. That fits the national rack utilization (上架率) of 71.4% (Jun 2026) 28 and partly loaded racks.
  • AI-specific. SemiAnalysis counts >24 GW (US ~56 GW) of AI data-center capacity by end-2026, with ~20 GW of dated and ~30 GW of announced pipeline E 30.
  • Compute. Intelligent computing capacity went from 788 EFLOPS (Jun 2025) to 1,590 (end-2025) to 2,185 EFLOPS (FP16) (Jun 2026) R 272928. 32.6% sits in the western region and 55.9% in the east 28: the west holds a third of AI compute but most of the cheap power, so East Data, West Compute is only partly realized.

How China compares with the US, and what it spends

US data centers used 176 TWh (4.4%) in 2023 and an estimated ~244 TWh (5.9%) in 2026 A (W03 73). Globally, data centers used ~485 TWh in 2025 (+17%), heading for ~950 TWh by 2030 (IEA) 23. On the official figure China's computing centers were ~35% of that 2025 global total; on the IEA-comparable basis ~22–25% A. China's data centers use a similar absolute amount of energy to America's, at about a third of the share of national demand, and they stay a single-digit share even on the official forecast.

What that costs A: the broad 170 TWh at a blended RMB 0.45–0.55/kWh is RMB 77–94bn in 2025 (~US$10.7–13.0bn); the IEA-comparable ~105 TWh is RMB 47–58bn (~US$6.6–8.0bn). The US bill is ~244 TWh × $80–95/MWh ≈ $20–23bn a year (2026, W03). China's data-center power bill is roughly a third to a half of America's for a similar or somewhat smaller volume. At the official 800 TWh in 2030 and RMB 0.40–0.45/kWh (a more western, greener mix), the bill would be RMB 320–360bn (~US$48–54bn) a year: material for generators and grids, but only ~4% of national power-sector revenue (conf: low).

06 · Money in / money outWhere the money comes from and where it goes

In the US, AI buyers pay a scarcity rent to the owners of power. In China the rent flows the other way: over-supply, falling contract prices and local subsidies move value from generators and provincial budgets to AI users.

The central money-flow insight

In the US, AI buyers pay scarcity rents to power owners: PJM capacity prices up ~11×, nuclear PPAs at $80–115/MWh, turbine makers with record backlogs (W03). In China the rent flows the other way. Over-supply, falling contract prices and local subsidies transfer value from generators and provincial budgets to AI users, and the state uses that transfer to steer chip choice and geography. The profit pool in Chinese power sits with equipment makers and the grid's regulated asset base, not with generators.

07 · Business modelsHow Chinese companies make money in this layer

Seven kinds of actor earn money here, and only two of them (equipment makers and, modestly, the grids) are clear winners from the AI build-out.

Grids

C03 Regulated

Regulated cost-plus with volume upside. SGCC and CSG earn an NDRC-approved return on their asset base through transmission tariffs, so capex is revenue. The RMB 4–5tn plan grows the regulated asset base. Returns are deliberately modest, and tariff cuts are a stimulus lever. AI load is attractive: large, high load factor, often on dedicated connections.

C03 Squeezed

Three revenue streams, one shrinking. Energy sales (tariffs −5% y/y), plus regulated capacity payments (rising from 2026), plus ancillary services and spot trading. With coal at ~4,346 hours a year, the model is shifting from "sell MWh" to "get paid to be available".

08 · Unit economicsWhat a kilowatt-hour costs, and how cheap power offsets inefficient chips

A western-hub AI site pays 3–5 US cents a kilowatt-hour, a third to a half of the US industrial average. With the reported subsidy, a Huawei cluster there pays less for power per unit of compute than an Nvidia cluster in the US.

The price ladder

Reading it. A western-hub AI site pays 3–5¢/kWh, roughly a third to a half of the US industrial average and on a par with the cheapest US wholesale markets. Eastern hubs pay 9–15¢, similar to or above US industrial rates. That is why Chinese AI training and bulk inference migrate west, while latency-sensitive inference stays east despite the cost.

Contract prices for 2026 fell across the coastal provinces: Guangdong's annual contract cleared at RMB 0.372/kWh (−5% y/y, −17.8% against the coal benchmark), and Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shanghai and Anhui annual contracts reportedly averaged RMB 344/345/430/371 per MWh, down ~7% 6162 (conf: med, search snippet only). Cheap power in 2026 reflects over-supply as much as structural cost.

Electricity cost per megawatt of IT per year

Assume 1 MW of IT load running at 85% average utilization with a PUE of 1.25 (the hub requirement for new sites): that is 1.0625 MW of average facility draw, or 9,308 MWh a year A.

How cheap power offsets the chip penalty

Domestic accelerators use more watts per unit of compute. The best-documented comparison is at rack scale: Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 delivers ~289 PFLOPs of dense BF16 compute (384 × 752 TF) at 559 kW, about 1.93 W per TFLOP; Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 delivers 180 PFLOPs at 145 kW, 0.81 W per TFLOP 4181. That is the canonical ~2.4× less power-efficient (0.52 vs 1.24 PF/kW); ~1.6× the BF16 compute A. (Tom's Hardware and SemiAnalysis round the CM384 to 300 PF, which gives 2.3×; the atlas uses ~289 PF, per C05.) Trade press reports that Chinese chips also draw more power per token than Nvidia's older H20-class parts, without an authoritative figure 363739 conf: low on any specific %.

Why SMRs matter less in China than in the US

Chinese SMRs cost RMB 30,000–50,000/kW (US$4,500–7,500/kW), about 2× a large PWR, with a levelized cost of RMB 0.40–0.50/kWh (6.0–7.5¢) 57. That is more than western-hub grid power at RMB 0.20–0.35. So in China SMRs are a niche for islands, industrial heat and eastern load centers, not a data-center necessity. In the US they are pitched as a way to get power at all. CNNC claims modular builds could cut SMR schedules from 60 to 48 months 57.

14 · BottlenecksIs power a constraint? Nationally no, with three caveats

Aggregate supply is not the problem. Firm capacity of ~1.9 TW sits against a 1.56 TW peak A, with 400–550 GW of new nameplate a year; the CEC expects >400 GW more in 2026 4. No credible scenario has AI data centers, even at 800 TWh (≈ 91 GW average) by 2030, exceeding the annual increment of China's firm capacity.

  1. Evening peaks and dry years. The 2021 power rationing (a coal-price spike) and the 2022 Sichuan drought (a hydro shortfall) showed that China can run short regionally. Coal additions of 70–95 GW a year 41112 are Beijing's insurance, and a sign that the state is not complacent.
  2. Eastern administrative scarcity. Energy quotas, PUE caps and differential tariffs make new large AI loads hard to site in Beijing, Shanghai and Shenzhen 3035. For latency-sensitive inference, power is effectively constrained by permit in the east.
  3. The green mandate. ≥80% green power for new hub data centers 19 is easy on paper (certificates) but hard physically (hourly matching). Horinger's first phase reaches only 43% 19.

Curtailment: the opposite of a shortage

Curtailment rose to 5.7% for wind and 5.2% for solar in 2025 (+1.6 pp and +2.0 pp) 4, and in H1 2026 utilization fell further, to 90.9% for wind and 91.4% for solar 8. Carbon Brief/CREA estimate wind and solar could have produced 170 TWh more in Q1 2026 alone without the rise in curtailment, and blame inflexible coal dispatch and annual inter-provincial trading contracts 10.

This is the core logic of 算电协同: put flexible AI load (training, batch jobs) where the curtailed electrons are, and shift jobs in time to midday solar. China Telecom's 8 MW demand-response pilot shows the potential, and how small it is so far 43.

Transmission: moving compute beats moving electrons

West-to-East transfer is ~340 GW today, targeted at >420 GW by 2030 4648. UHV lines are often underused, because provinces prefer local supply and inter-provincial trades are contracted annually 10. A Huxiu piece cites 15–20% "losses" for West-to-East transmission 43; physical ±800 kV losses are typically ~5–7% over ~2,000 km (conf: med), so the higher figure probably includes curtailment or price discounts, and we treat it as unreliable. Either way, moving compute west is cheaper than moving electrons east once there is enough fiber (the 20 ms latency ring 31).

The real bottleneck: chips, then utilization

Power abundance does not create compute. The US–China gap in AI data-center capacity (>24 GW vs ~56 GW by end-2026 30) comes from accelerator supply: US export controls, SMIC capacity and HBM (C05, C06). Western hubs already show low rack utilization (71.4% nationally 28, many western "computing-power centers" far lower; C04). Stranded power-plus-shell capacity is China's version of the US queue bubble.

Dependencies on other layers

  • C05 / C06 (chips): set the size of AI load; chip efficiency sets the MW per unit of compute.
  • C04 (data centers): siting and utilization of the western hubs decide whether cheap power is actually used.
  • C02 (financing): local-government finances fund the power subsidies; LGFV stress could cap them.
  • C01 (demand): token growth (>140tn/day by Mar 2026 19) drives inference load, which must sit closer to users in the east, where power is dearer. The 170 → ~800 TWh path implies ~+100 TWh a year, or ~11–14 GW of new average load a year A.

16 · Bull vs bearChina's electrons as an AI moat, or abundant power misallocated

Bull case

"China's electrons are its AI moat"

  • Supply at scale: ~550 GW added in 2025 (~10× the US), >400 GW in 2026, firm additions of 70–100 GW a year 249. AI is ~7% of demand growth A 14.
  • Cheap: western hubs at 3–5¢/kWh, ~2–3¢ with subsidies 3136; contract prices falling in 2026 61.
  • Fast: months to connect vs >5 years in US queues 1730; nuclear in 5–6 years at ~$2.6k/kW A 5255.
  • State alignment: RMB 4–5tn of grid plans 4546; 算电协同 in the Government Work Report and 15th FYP 19; green-direct policy prioritizing computing 42; nuclear-direct exploration 44.
  • It neutralizes the chip handicap: Ascend in the west with subsidies pays ~0.65× Nvidia-in-the-US for power per unit of compute A. China can brute-force its way toward compute parity with more, cheaper watts 41.
  • Export upside: Chinese equipment sells into the West's power bottleneck (transformer exports +36%) 6970.

Bear case

"Abundant power, misallocated"

  • Chips cap everything. Cheap power cannot conjure accelerators. China's AI capacity is <½ of the US's 30, and the power advantage sits unused until C05 ramps.
  • Stranded western capacity. Racks are 71.4% used nationally 28; many western computing centers sit idle (C04). Subsidies create power-plus-shell white elephants.
  • Coal lock-in and emissions. 71–95 GW of new coal in 2025 411, majority-coal data-center power 22. Inefficient chips × coal = high CO₂ per token, an eventual CBAM or ESG risk for Chinese AI exports.
  • Generator distress. Profits down 18–29% 66; utilization −312 h in 2025 4. If the state rescues generators with higher capacity payments or tariffs, cheap AI power becomes a fiscal transfer that could be withdrawn.
  • Curtailment shows an inflexible grid. 170 TWh wasted in one quarter 10; dispatch and market design, not megawatts, are the weak link, and 算电协同 "may take more than five years" 43.
  • Latency mismatch and opacity. Inference, the growth engine, wants the expensive east 3135. Subsidy terms are unconfirmed 3637, definitions differ 2–3×, and connection times are anecdotal.

Net judgment (conf: med)

Power is a strategic advantage for China through at least 2030. It is not a binding constraint at national level, and it partly offsets the chip handicap. But the advantage is being spent inefficiently (on coal, stranded western capacity and subsidies for less efficient chips), so it buys resilience and volume, not efficiency.

What's structurally different

  1. Mirror-image scarcity. The US has chips but not power; China has power but not chips.
  2. Planned vs merchant grid. China's grid obligation and state planning replace US interconnection queues and capacity auctions. Speed is higher; efficiency (curtailment, stranded assets) is lower.
  3. Power as industrial policy. Tariffs and subsidies steer chip choice (domestic accelerators) and geography (west). US power policy protects ratepayers from data centers; Chinese policy attracts them to the right place with the right chips.
  4. Coal as the firming asset. The US firms with gas and is turbine-constrained. China firms with coal, constrained by nothing but climate targets, plus pumped hydro and batteries.
  5. Value flows. In the US, AI buyers pay rents to power owners. In China, generators and local budgets subsidize AI buyers.
  6. Data transparency. US numbers (EIA, FERC, ISO queues, 10-Ks) are granular and auditable. Chinese numbers are official aggregates with shifting definitions and unpublished subsidy terms.

20 · GlossaryTerms on this page

Computing-power center 算力中心
The official umbrella term for data centers, intelligent-computing (AI) centers and supercomputing centers. The NEA's 170 TWh figure uses it.
Internet data services 互联网数据服务
The NBS/NEA consumption category used as the monthly data-center proxy (commercial IDC and cloud). Narrower than "computing-power centers".
East Data, West Compute 东数西算
The 2022 national program of 8 hub nodes and 10 clusters that moves compute to western energy bases.
Compute–power coordination 算电协同
Co-planning and co-dispatch of computing and electricity; in the 2026 Government Work Report and the 15th Five-Year Plan.
Green-power direct connection 绿电直连
A dedicated line from a wind/solar (and storage) plant to a user, bypassing the public grid. Allowed under Doc. 650 (2025) and the multi-user notice (May 2026).
Source-grid-load-storage integration 源网荷储一体化
A park-level microgrid combining generation, grid, load and storage under one plan.
Grid agency purchase 电网代理购电
The default supply route for users who don't trade: the grid company buys power on their behalf at a published monthly price.
Coal benchmark price 燃煤发电基准价
The provincial anchor price for coal power (RMB 0.25–0.453/kWh), around which market prices float within a ±20% band.
Mechanism price 机制电价
A contract-for-difference price for part of new renewable output under Doc. 136 (2025), set by provincial auctions.
Capacity price 容量电价
Regulated fixed payments (RMB/kW-year) to coal plants for being available, since 2024.
T&D tariff 输配电价
The transmission-and-distribution charge approved by the NDRC on a "permitted cost + reasonable return" basis.
Dual control 能耗双控 / 碳双控
Caps on total energy consumption and energy intensity, shifting to caps on carbon. In the east, the quota to consume energy often binds before the grid does.
Energy-conservation review 节能审查
The approval a new energy-intensive project needs before it may consume energy; the gate through which quotas are enforced.
UHV 特高压
Ultra-high voltage: ±800/±1,100 kV DC and 1,000 kV AC lines that carry power 1,500–2,500 km.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total facility power ÷ IT power. China's national average was 1.42 (Jun 2025); new hubs must reach ~1.25.
Rack utilization 上架率
The share of built racks actually in use: 71.4% nationally (Jun 2026). Not the same as chip utilization.
Big Five 五大发电
The five central state-owned generators: Huaneng, Datang, Huadian, CHN Energy (Guodian) and SPIC.
Hualong One / Guohe One / Linglong One
China's Gen-III pressurized water reactor (~1.2 GW), its CAP1400-based reactor (~1.5 GW), and its 125 MWe small modular reactor.
SMR
Small modular reactor: a reactor of up to ~300 MWe built from factory-made modules.
Firm capacity
Capacity reliably available at peak (thermal, nuclear, hydro, storage), as opposed to nameplate.
Nameplate capacity
The maximum rated output of a plant (GW). Wind and solar nameplate overstates what is available at peak.
Curtailment
Wind or solar output that could have been generated but was switched off because the grid could not absorb it.
Utilization hours
Annual output ÷ capacity, in hours (8,760 = running flat out all year). Coal was at 4,346 hours in 2025.
GEC
Green electricity certificate: a tradable claim on 1 MWh of renewable output. 930m traded in China in 2025.
LCOE
Levelized cost of electricity: the constant price per MWh that recovers a plant's lifetime costs.

BF16

EFLOPS

CSG

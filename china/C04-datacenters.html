The 60-second version

  1. China has the world's second-largest data-center fleet and it is growing fast. Standard racks in use hit 15.56m in June 2026, +43% in a year, and SemiAnalysis expects >24 GW of delivered capacity by end-2026 against ~56 GW in the US RE 6518.
  2. Buildings are cheap and quick; chips are scarce. GDS builds at RMB 20m per MW (~US$3.0m, about a quarter of the US) and a 100 MW hall takes ~12 months. What limits China is what goes inside: export-controlled NVIDIA parts and HBM-capped domestic accelerators (C05) RE 318.
  3. It is two markets at once. A tight hyperscale tier (GDS booked a record 470 MW in H1 2026; VNET's mature halls are 92.5% used; new-deal rents are rising) sits beside a subsidy-driven tier of ~500 local-government AI centres where only 36.8% of card-hours were used in 2025 and an estimated 250–350 lose money RE 361539.
  4. Rents are a fraction of the US, returns are not. New-build wholesale earns ~RMB 150–300/kW/month (US$22–45), 10–35% of US levels, but because the building costs a quarter as much, GDS still targets a 10–11% yield, in line with the US 9–13% AG 367.
  5. The state is the architect. It sets eight hubs and ten clusters, gates projects above ~10 MW through energy quotas, caps new-build PUE at 1.25 (1.2 in hubs), orders state-funded centres to use domestic chips, and has drafted a reported ~RMB 2tn (~US$298bn) national AI data-center grid run by the carriers RE 362223.
  6. Watch GDS's 1 GW booking target and VNET's deliveries (Nov 2026), DayOne's ~US$5bn US IPO, the next MIIT occupancy print, ByteDance's reported 5–6 GW Ulanqab campus, and any US move to close offshore cloud access for Chinese tenants.

01 · What this layer isThe factory building, and who owns it in China

A data center is a factory whose raw material is electricity and whose output is computation. This layer is the factory building, its power and cooling plant, and the companies that own and rent it.

The chips inside belong to C05, the servers and networking to C07, and the clouds that sell the computation to C08. Grid power and prices sit in C03, hyperscaler capex totals in C01 and state funds in C02. In China the operators are called IDC (internet data center) companies, and the new AI-specific halls are called AIDC or intelligent computing centers (智算中心).

Analogy

Think of China's high-speed rail network around 2012. The state draws the map (8 hubs, 10 clusters). Carriers and local governments lay track fast and cheap, often ahead of demand. Some western branch lines were built for traffic that has not arrived, and their stations sit half-empty, while the trunk lines are sold out and the biggest customers (ByteDance, Alibaba) start laying their own track. The trains, meaning the GPUs, are the scarce part. The track is abundant.

Explain like I'm new: why would a country build data centers it cannot fill?

Because the people who decide to build are not the people who fill them. A city government earns credit for "new infrastructure" and for using domestic chips. It borrows through a local investment company, builds a hall of a few hundred to a thousand accelerator cards, and offers compute vouchers to local firms. If model labs do not want those chips, or the hall is too small for training, the racks are installed but the cards sit idle.

Meanwhile ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent need well-connected, dense halls near users and near good chips. Those are sold out. So China shows 71.4% rack occupancy on paper and less than 30% GPU use in many AI centres at the same time. Both numbers are true.

Definition

Standard rack (标准机架): China's official unit of data-center capacity, normalised to 2.5 kW. A 20 kW AI rack counts as eight standard racks, so denser AI halls inflate the rack count. Rack occupancy (上架率) is the share of built racks with equipment installed. It says nothing about whether the chips in them are busy.

Why it matters

This layer is where China's overbuild meets its chip shortage, and the gap shows up as low utilisation. In the US the building layer earns scarcity rents. In China it earns utility-like returns, and the losses on idle capacity land on local-government balance sheets. If you want to know how much of China's AI capex is productive, start with the utilisation numbers on this page.

The Chinese rack and the Chinese hall

  • Legacy cloud halls (2015–2022) ran 4.4–8 kW per rack, air-cooled, with 2N UPS or 240/336 V HVDC (China adopted HVDC a decade before the West's 800 V DC push; see W04), at a PUE of 1.3–1.5.
  • AI halls (2024 on) run 20–40 kW per rack for domestic accelerators and H20-class chips, and 130–140 kW for GB200/GB300 NVL72-class racks E 41. MIIT's 15th Five-Year ICT plan names 30–80 kW liquid-cooled cabinets as the new standard G 48.
  • Supernodes spread out. Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 supernode spreads 384 Ascend chips over 16 racks, using more floor space and power per unit of compute than NVIDIA's NVL72 (C07). Cheap Chinese space and power make that trade tolerable.
  • Cold-plate liquid cooling is "the standard choice for intelligent computing centers" E 51; about 50% of China Unicom's new cabinets in 2026 are liquid-cooled R 65.
  • Prefabrication. Alibaba's CUBE 5.0 "100-day data center" takes 30 days in the factory, 50 on site and 20 commissioning, and cuts capex by more than 10% versus the prior version. A standard 100 MW facility now takes ~12 months (~18 in the cloud era), and 7–9 months from ground-break to commissioning E 18.

Power: how a Chinese data center gets its electricity

  • The energy quota (能耗指标). Any project using more than 10,000 tonnes of coal equivalent a year (~10 MW) needs provincial approval. SemiAnalysis calls this quota "the effective enforcement mechanism" of the hub policy. Guangdong stopped issuing DC quotas in 2021–22 and reopened only for the Shaoguan hub E 18.
  • Prices. Coastal industrial power costs RMB 0.6–0.75/kWh. Western green-power contracts are quoted at RMB 0.13–0.30/kWh conf: low-med E 1932; the Atlas western-hub benchmark is RMB 0.20–0.35/kWh (3.0–5.2 US¢) against ~9.8¢ for US industrial power (C03). Inner Mongolia power costs roughly half the Tier-1 level E 18.
  • Green-power mandate. New DCs in hub nodes must source >80% green power (2025 target), DC renewable use must grow 10% a year, and DCs below the efficiency benchmark pay stepped penalty tariffs R 36.
  • Subsidies. Local governments have offered power subsidies of up to 50% for DCs running domestic chips (reported Nov 2025) E 37. It is an industrial-policy transfer to make power-hungrier Ascend-class chips competitive (C05).
  • "Compute-energy" integration. VNET's Aug-2026 deal with CATL for GW-scale compute-energy facilities R 68, Huawei's "source-grid-load-storage" (源网荷储) AIDC designs in Ulanqab, and Tianneng's 1 GWh solar-storage-computing project in Malaysia E 43 all point to behind-the-meter green power plus storage: China's analogue of US on-site gas turbines.

The national hub system: "East Data, West Computing"

Launched in February 2022 by the NDRC and others, East Data, West Computing (东数西算) designates 8 national hub nodes and 10 national data-center clusters. The four eastern and south-western "demand" hubs sit near users; the four western "resource" hubs sit near cheap green power. Latency-sensitive inference stays east, and training and batch work is meant to go west.

The hub policy's hard rule

In any city where data centers that have been in service for over a year run at overall occupancy below 50%, "in principle no new data-center clusters, large or hyperscale DC projects" may be planned. The national occupancy target was ≥60% by end-2025 R 36. It was nominally met: 71.4% at end-June 2026 13. Note what the rule rewards: installed racks, not busy chips.

03 · Market size and growthA fleet that grew 43% in a year, measured three ways

On the official rack count, China's fleet nearly doubled between end-2023 and mid-2026. Growth slowed to ~9% in 2024, the digestion year after the 2021–23 overbuild, then re-accelerated to +43% year on year by mid-2026 (passing 13.73m at end-2025 69) on the post-DeepSeek boom (C01) R 1765. Part of the jump is density, not floor space: a 40 kW AI rack counts as sixteen standard racks A.

Compute grew faster still. MIIT counted 2,185 EFLOPS of intelligent compute (FP16) at end-June 2026, up 177% in a year, and its 15th Five-Year ICT plan targets 9,800 EFLOPS by 2030, 4.5× mid-2026 RG 1347. China counted 42 clusters of 10,000+ cards in January 2026 R 14 and 52 by mid-2026 E 48.

Data-reliability flag

Chinese compute statistics mix precisions (FP32, FP16, sometimes INT8), mix "self-owned + connected" capacity, and change method between releases. CAICT's like-for-like global comparison is more credible than the headline growth rates: it has China's share of world intelligent compute falling from 29% (2023) to 21% (mid-2025) while the US rose to 46% E 16. Treat the EFLOPS series as directional conf: med and the rack series as more reliable conf: med-high.

Key insight

China's building layer is small in money even though it is big in racks. At ~6–8 GW a year and RMB 20–25m per MW, China spends ~RMB 120–200bn (~US$18–30bn) a year on shells and M&E; North America spends roughly US$170–350bn (W04). China builds perhaps a quarter to a third as many MW, and each MW costs about a quarter as much, so its building-layer spend is ~5–15% of the US's A conf: low. GDS: the AI booking surge

GDS Holdings (Nasdaq: GDS, HKEX: 9698) is China's largest listed independent operator, focused on Tier-1 metro rings and now the western hubs. In 2Q26 (reported 13 Aug 2026) revenue was RMB 3,088.0m (+6.5%), adjusted EBITDA RMB 1,406.0m (margin 45.5%, from 47.3%), and gross margin 21.5% (from 23.8%), squeezed by "higher utility costs". Net income was RMB 837.6m, helped by a RMB 959.9m dilution gain on DayOne's Series C R 12.

The headline is bookings: 260 MW in Q2 and 470 MW in H1 2026, both records. The full-year target doubled from 500 MW to 1 GW. Backlog rose from 450 MW to 757 MW and should pass 1 GW by year-end, with ~3 GW of uncommitted developable capacity. About half of H1 bookings were in new markets (Ulanqab, Horinger, Shaoguan, Changshu). Workloads are ~50/50 CPU/GPU, with the GPU share rising in 2027 "as domestic chip supply catches up". All contracts are take-or-pay with fixed delivery dates R 3.

  • Unit economics R 3: unit capex RMB 20m/MW (~US$3.0m); backlog EBITDA RMB 2.2m per MW per year; stabilised project yield target 10–11%; project finance 60% debt / 40% equity at 5.5–6.0× leverage. Legacy contracts are still repricing down, with about 18 months of that left.
  • Balance sheet R 1: cash RMB 14.9bn; total debt RMB 46.1bn (~US$6.9bn); net leverage 4.7×; net interest RMB 366.8m a quarter.
  • Guidance G 1: 2026 revenue RMB 12.7–13.0bn (~US$1.90–1.94bn; raised from 12.4–12.9bn), adjusted EBITDA RMB 5.9–6.1bn, capex ~RMB 10bn (from 9bn).
  • FY2025 R 4: revenue RMB 11,432m (+10.8%; ~US$1.59bn), adjusted EBITDA RMB 5,404m (47.3%), a RMB 2,364m gain on moving assets into the ABS and C-REIT, and capex of only RMB 4.6bn, less than half the 2026 guide. That is how sharply the AI cycle turned.
  • Capital recycling R 455455: the C-REIT listed on 8 Aug 2025; a second injection (a Langfang campus) is in approval. GDS sold US$385m of DayOne shares (Jan 2026) and placed US$300m of convertible preferred shares with a Chinese institutional investor.
  • Moat and risk. Tier-1 power quotas newcomers cannot easily get, relationships with all three large hyperscalers, and REIT access. Risks: customer concentration, pass-through of utility costs, legacy repricing.

08 · Unit economicsWhat a megawatt costs, what it rents for, and what it is worth

Cost per MW: a quarter of the US for the building

GDS says its average unit capex is RMB 20m per MW (~US$3.0m), including Inner Mongolia builds R 3: about 26% of JLL's US$11.3m/MW US average for shell plus MEP A 67. A Wood Mackenzie comparison relayed in Chinese media puts China at US$5.5–6.5m/MW against US$8–12m, probably with more scope (Tier-1 metro, higher redundancy) E 32 conf: low-med.

Why is China ~25–50% of US cost? A

  1. Labour. Construction wages are a fraction of US union electrician rates, and SOE builders mobilise thousands of workers within weeks.
  2. Equipment. Domestic transformers, switchgear, UPS, chillers and CDUs are cheaper and available: no 100-plus-week transformer queue 32.
  3. Land and permitting. Hub land is cheap or free. The energy quota is the gate; once granted, permits are quick (18 months end to end vs 3–5 years in the US) 32.
  4. Specification. Lower redundancy in some AI halls (N+1 rather than 2N), less stringent seismic and fire codes in western hubs, and prefabrication (Alibaba CUBE: >10% cheaper per generation) 18.
  5. FX and measurement. The US$ comparison moves with the rate (6.70 now vs 7.2 in 2024), and GDS's figure may exclude land and substation.

The all-in gap is mainly cheaper chips: H20-class NVIDIA parts and domestic Ascend, Kunlun and Hygon accelerators at RMB 30,000–200,000 per card E 41, against Blackwell and Rubin systems in the US. The building is cheap, but the compute per building is lower.

Rents: what a MW earns in China

New-build wholesale earns an estimated ~RMB 150–300/kW/month (US$22–45) excluding power, ~10–35% of US wholesale A conf: low-med. Neither GDS nor VNET discloses new-deal rents; the range is triangulated from SemiAnalysis (power-exclusive market rents halved from ~US$80/kW/month in the 2022–23 reset E 18), GDS's EBITDA per MW, and the REIT's legacy RMB 570 R 55. Why so much is idle

  1. Wrong chips. Many local centres were built around domestic accelerators (or older NVIDIA A800/H800) that model labs do not want for training. Inference demand exists, but software stacks (CANN and others) and cluster sizes do not match. The Nov-2025 domestic-chip mandate for state-funded DCs made this worse in the short run: projects less than 30% complete had to remove foreign chips R 22.
  2. Wrong place, wrong size. Western-hub centres built for training pay latency and network costs to reach eastern users. Network cost is ~40% of total cost for video firms and 20–30% for cloud vendors; a Suzhou–Qinghai 100G link costs RMB 3m a month E 24. Hundreds of 100–1,000-card centres are too small for frontier training, which needs 10,000–100,000 cards.
  3. Speculative supply. Local governments competed for "new infrastructure" KPIs: 458 tenders in 2024 60 and 128 projects worth >RMB 430bn (~US$60bn) in 1Q25 15.
  4. An efficiency shock. DeepSeek-style efficiency and the concentration of training in a few hyperscalers and labs left independent GPU renters with falling prices (8×H100 rent RMB 180k → 75k a month) and cancelled contracts E 3859.
  5. Yet token demand exploded. Daily token calls rose from ~100bn (early 2024) to ~140tn (March 2026) E 15. That demand flows to hyperscaler clouds (Volcano Engine, Alibaba Cloud) running their own or leased high-grade capacity. The idle centres are not in that flow.

The policy response includes the 50%-occupancy rule for new approvals 36; the national compute network, with "~70% of national compute resources" now on a unified real-time database per academician Gao Wen R 13; compute vouchers; up to 50% power subsidies for domestic-chip DCs 37; and the reported ~RMB 2tn national grid under carrier operation 23, which in effect consolidates fragmented local builds under the state carriers.

Our read conf: med

The "70% idle" story applies to the policy tier, perhaps ~15–25% of China's AI compute by FLOPS, not to the hyperscale wholesale tier, where mature utilisation is >90% and bookings are at records A. Headlines that apply the idle-centre numbers to the whole market overstate the waste; headlines that quote 71.4% occupancy overstate the productivity.

Why it matters

China regulates efficiency by law: a new large data center with a PUE above 1.25 does not get built. That pushes the whole market toward liquid cooling and western sites, and it gives the state a lever to steer demand toward domestic chips (the 50% power subsidy offsets their higher power draw per FLOP). The state is also the loss-absorber: the idle policy tier's losses sit with local governments (C02). C11 estimates state-directed capex at ~19% of China's 2026 AI data-center capex.

Where the profit sits in China A

  1. Scale landlords with hyperscaler contracts (GDS, Qinhuai, VNET wholesale, Range) capture the healthiest profits: 45% EBITDA margins, 10–11% yields and, since 2025, a REIT exit.
  2. Carriers capture profit inside a bundle; their IDC economics are opaque, policy-driven and cross-subsidised from connectivity.
  3. Equipment vendors capture much less than in the US: dozens of liquid-cooling entrants, centralised tenders by state carriers and hyperscalers, no import bottleneck. Envicool's ~6% net margin against Vertiv's ~24% operating margin is the clearest contrast. The exception is export: Envicool's overseas revenue +71%, Kstar 58% overseas 3542.
  4. Builders (SOE construction groups) earn thin, commodity-like margins conf: low.
  5. Losses sit with local-government compute platforms and small compute-leasing firms: an estimated 250–350 of ~500 AI centres E 39 conf: low.

Share of the value chain

Of an all-in RMB 160m per MW including IT, the building is ~RMB 20–25m, or ~13–16%; in the US it is ~20–30% (W04) A. The chip and server layers (C05, C07) take the bulk of the capital. Because China's building layer is not the bottleneck, it earns utility-like, not scarcity, returns. The exception is the Tier-1 metro rings, where energy quotas are the scarce asset. C11 puts C04 at about 13% of China's ~US$17bn AI profit pool in 2026.

14 · Competitive dynamicsSelf-build, domestic capital, carriers as consolidators, and the move abroad

  1. The self-build shift. Alibaba (>20 GW by 2032) and ByteDance (a 5–6 GW Ulanqab plan) are moving to own more capacity 3161. GDS and VNET won only ~1/3 of their orders in 2024–26 18. Landlords answer with speed (delivery in ≤4 quarters) and land banks (GDS ~3 GW, VNET >4 GW).
  2. Consolidation into domestic hands. Bain's exit from Chindata China (RMB 28bn to HEC) 20 and GDS's move to onshore capital (C-REITs; US$300m of convertible preferred from a Chinese institution 5) complete the de-Americanisation of China's DC capital stack, while DayOne and Bridge de-Sinicise to stay eligible for US chips.
  3. Carriers as the state's consolidators. The ~RMB 2tn plan with carriers as operators 23 and China Mobile's >7 GW of signed AIDC 65 position carriers to absorb idle local capacity and host domestic-chip clusters.
  4. Compute-energy integration. VNET–CATL 6, Huawei's source-grid-load-storage AIDC and Tianneng in Malaysia 43. The edge goes to operators with direct green-power connections in Inner Mongolia, Gansu and Ningxia.
  5. Liquid cooling and 800 V DC. Liquid-cooled server penetration is estimated at ~20% in 2025 and ~37% in 2026 E 34 conf: low-med; the market grows at a 46.8% CAGR 51. Kstar's 800 VDC sample (May 2026) 42 shows Chinese vendors following NVIDIA's 800 V roadmap; China's early 240/336 V HVDC experience could help.
  6. Supernodes change the building. Huawei CloudMatrix 384 and successors use more racks, optics and power per unit of compute (C07). That suits cheap Chinese MW and hurts in Tier-1 cities.
  7. Overseas expansion as the growth engine. DayOne (>1.5 GW booked), Bridge (~2 GW target), VNET (478 MW overseas), Alibaba (Turkey, Finland, Netherlands; Dubai), Tencent (Saudi Arabia, more Middle East). SemiAnalysis sees Chinese hyperscalers' overseas leasing doubling to ~4 GW by 2029 18.
  8. Disruptors. (a) US limits on offshore cloud access for Chinese firms, which would hurt DayOne, Bridge and Johor; (b) a domestic-chip breakthrough (Ascend 950/960 volume, CXMT HBM) that fills idle centres; (c) local-government debt stress forcing fire sales of AI-centre assets to carriers or hyperscalers.

15 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

The top tier is sold out and the cost advantage is structural

  • Demand is real at the top. ~1.3 GW of GDS + VNET orders in 1H26 18; GDS's target doubled to 1 GW 3; token volume up ~1,400× in two years 15; BAT capex US$20bn in 2Q26 18; Alibaba targeting 20 GW by 2032 31.
  • Rents have bottomed. New AI contracts reprice up 20–30% 40; retail MRR +9.9% 6; legacy repricing ends in ~18 months 3.
  • Structural cost advantage. ~US$3m/MW, 12-month delivery and cheap western power make China the world's lowest-cost AI factory shell; when domestic chips scale, China can deploy them faster than anyone.
  • Capital recycling works. C-REITs (456× oversubscribed 54), insurers and local funds give landlords a ~2× developer's spread.
  • Overseas optionality. DayOne at a ~US$20bn IPO target is worth ~US$4bn to GDS A; Chinese hyperscalers need ~4 GW abroad by 2029.
  • State backstop. RMB 3.8tn of information-infrastructure investment is targeted for 2026–30 47; compute sovereignty guarantees continued build.

Bear case

An overbuilt policy tier, short leases and chip dependence

  • Structural overbuild. 250–350 of ~500 centres loss-making 39; card-hour use 36.8% 15; ~20 GW of aged pipeline and ~30 GW announced overhang the market 18. If domestic chips disappoint, much becomes stranded.
  • Customer concentration. ByteDance is ~1/5 of capacity and moving to self-build. Hyperscalers took the 2021–23 digestion as halved rents and could again.
  • Short contracts. ~3 years remaining at the GDS REIT asset 55 vs 15-year US leases; REIT valuations were cut ~11–12% in 2025 when escalators and residual values were stripped 56.
  • Low absolute rents. US$20–45/kW/month leaves little cushion if power costs rise or utilisation slips.
  • Chip dependence. Tighter controls (including on offshore clouds) and HBM-limited domestic supply could leave booked capacity with tenants who cannot fill it; GDS's 50/50 CPU/GPU mix shows AI racks are not yet dominant 3.
  • Local fiscal stress may dump subsidised capacity on the market at distressed prices; and equipment makers look over-valued (Envicool ~RMB 90–120bn on ~6% net margins, with receivable stress) 3435.

What is structurally different

  1. Scarcity is inverted. The US lacks buildings and power and has chips; China has buildings and power and lacks usable chips. US landlords and vendors earn scarcity rents; Chinese ones earn utility-like returns.
  2. The state allocates capacity. Quotas, hub designations, PUE mandates, the 50%-occupancy rule and the domestic-chip mandate replace price signals, producing both speed and misallocation.
  3. Two-tier market. A tight hyperscale tier beside a subsidised, idle policy tier. The US has nothing like the second.
  4. Shorter leases, lower rents, lower capex: similar yields.
  5. Offshore is a strategic valve. Chinese hyperscalers use Malaysia, Brazil and the Middle East to reach controlled chips, through Chinese-rooted operators restructured as foreign entities funded by US capital.
  6. Domestic equipment supply means no transformer or chiller bottleneck, and no vendor pricing power.

19 · GlossaryTerms on this page

AIDC
AI data center: a hall built for dense accelerator racks, usually liquid-cooled. Chinese carriers and landlords report AIDC separately from legacy IDC.
Behind the meter
Power generated or stored on site (solar, batteries, turbines) rather than bought through the grid. In China: "source-grid-load-storage" (源网荷储) designs.
Card-hour
One accelerator card used for one hour: the unit of GPU rental and of CAICT's utilisation statistic.
C-REIT (基础设施公募REITs)
China's public infrastructure REIT. Data centers became eligible in 2025; the first two (GDS, Range) listed on 8 Aug 2025.
Compute leasing (算力租赁)
Renting GPU servers by the card-hour or month, often by small A-share IDC firms.
Compute voucher (算力券)
A local-government subsidy that small firms use to rent compute.
Developer's spread
Value of a stabilised, leased data center divided by what it cost to build (~2.3× for a GDS-type asset).
East Data, West Computing (东数西算)
The national program, launched Feb 2022, that steers data centers into 8 hub nodes and 10 clusters.
EFLOPS
1018 floating-point operations per second. Chinese statistics mix FP16 and FP32.
Energy quota (能耗指标)
Provincial approval needed for projects above ~10,000 tonnes of coal equivalent a year (~10 MW); the real gate on where DCs are built.
Hub node / cluster (枢纽节点 / 集群)
The designated regions and parks where large DCs get priority for quotas and power.
HVDC
High-voltage direct-current power distribution inside the data center. China used 240/336 V widely in the 2010s; 800 V is next.
IDC
Internet data center: the Chinese industry term for colocation data centers and the companies that run them.
Intelligent computing center (智算中心)
An AI-accelerator data center, often sponsored by a local government or SOE.
MRR
Monthly recurring revenue per retail cabinet (VNET: RMB 9,799).
Power-inclusive vs power-exclusive
Whether the landlord's price bundles electricity. Legacy Chinese contracts were power-inclusive; new wholesale deals increasingly pass power through.
Pre-REIT / ABS
Private funds or asset-backed securities used to hold assets before a REIT injection.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total facility power ÷ IT power. China mandates ≤1.25 for new large DCs and ≤1.2 in hubs.
Rack occupancy (上架率)
Share of built racks with equipment installed: 71.4% nationally at end-June 2026. Not the same as accelerator utilisation.
Standard rack (标准机架)
A statistical unit normalised to 2.5 kW, so a 25 kW AI rack counts as 10 standard racks.
Supernode (超节点)
A rack-scale accelerator system spanning many racks, such as Huawei's CloudMatrix 384.
Take-or-pay
The tenant pays whether or not it uses the capacity. GDS says all its contracts are take-or-pay with fixed delivery dates.
Wholesale vs retail colocation
Wholesale: MW-scale leases to hyperscalers, priced per kW, often power-exclusive. Retail: per-cabinet leases to enterprises, with power and bandwidth included.
Yield on cost
Stabilised EBITDA (or NOI) divided by total development cost; GDS targets 10–11%.

The 60-second version

  1. China's biggest win in this layer is exporting optics to the US build-out, not building its own. InnoLight (中际旭创) booked RMB 41.8bn of revenue (~US$6.2bn) and RMB 13.7bn of net profit in 1H 2026, up 183% and 242%, with 95% of sales overseas R 13. Eoptolink (新易盛) added RMB 20.9bn R 13. Together they sold ~US$9.4bn of modules in six months, roughly half the world's AI datacom optics market A.
  2. At home, China substitutes system scale and optics for chip quality. Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 wires 384 Ascend chips with 6,912 optical links and draws ~4× the power of Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 for ~1.6× the compute E 3774. Huawei says >1,000 are deployed, and its new Atlas 960E pod replaces ~48,000 pluggable modules with 5,500 in-house optical engines R 3233.
  3. The domestic server business is huge in revenue and nearly margin-free. China's accelerated-server market reached RMB 250bn (~US$34.8bn) in 2025 E 44, but the leader, Inspur, kept a 4.77% gross margin R 48. The accelerator is passed through at cost-plus and the platforms design their own boxes.
  4. Chinese switches run on American silicon. Huawei, H3C and Ruijie build the boxes, but an FT-reported state audit found Broadcom chips in ~90% of state-owned data-centre switches E 6364. Module makers also import more than 95% of the high-speed signal chips (DSPs) for 800G/1.6T modules, mainly from Marvell and Broadcom E 24.
  5. So the profit pool is inverted relative to the West. About 83% of the layer's listed net profit in 1H26 came from selling to Western AI capex A; domestic-facing server and switch vendors earned ~4% net on ~RMB 240bn of revenue A. In the US, Nvidia keeps ~86% of the rack's gross profit.
  6. Washington has turned on the optics export channel. InnoLight was added to the Pentagon's 1260H list on 8 Jun 2026 R 29, and the FCC is reportedly drafting a bar on authorising new Chinese optical-module models E 2627. Watch whether the FCC rule lands by end-2026, and whether InnoLight's growth (+14% q/q in Q2) re-accelerates on 1.6T.

01 · Explain like I'm newThree businesses, two halls and one supplier

An AI chip on its own does nothing useful. Thousands of them have to be packed into servers, stacked into racks and wired together so tightly that they behave like one giant computer. This layer is everything that does the packing and the wiring.

China is building AI clusters under a double handicap: it cannot buy the best Nvidia chips freely, and its own chips (Huawei's Ascend 910C and the 950 series) are individually weaker (C05). The workaround is system-level brute force: tie many more chips together, over much bigger and more optical networks, and accept a higher power bill, which China can afford (C03). That makes networking and optics more central in China than in the West.

Meanwhile, Chinese companies are among the largest suppliers of optical modules to the American AI build-out. So this layer is also China's biggest direct earner from US AI spending, and a new sanctions front.

Analogy: an orchestra and a choir

A Western AI cluster is an orchestra of 72 virtuosos seated shoulder to shoulder (Nvidia's NVL72, wired with copper inside one rack). Huawei's CloudMatrix is a choir of 384 good singers spread across 16 racks, each wearing an in-ear monitor (an optical transceiver) so they stay in time. The choir out-sings the orchestra in volume (~1.6× the compute) but eats about four times the electricity and needs thousands of radios. China's optical-module makers are the firms that sell those radios, to both halls.

What is an optical module, and why does AI need so many?

Chips talk in electrical signals, which fade after a metre or two of copper. To connect racks across a hall, the signal is turned into light, sent down a glass fibre and turned back into electricity at the other end. The small box that does this, plugged into the front of a switch or server, is an optical module (or transceiver). Inside are lasers, light detectors, a signal-processing chip (the DSP) and some precise glass and metal parts.

An AI cluster needs one or more modules for every chip, at each end of every link. A 100,000-chip cluster buys hundreds of thousands of them, and each generation (400G → 800G → 1.6T, the "G" and "T" being gigabits and terabits per second) sells at a higher price. China's makers became the fastest and cheapest at producing each new generation in volume.

Definition

Scale-up vs scale-out: scale-up links a small group of chips so tightly that they share memory and act as one big chip (Nvidia's NVLink, Huawei's UnifiedBus). Scale-out connects thousands of those groups over a data-centre network (usually Ethernet in China). In China a scale-up group is called a supernode or SuperPoD (超节点).

Why it matters for the money map

In the West, this layer is where Nvidia's rack-level pricing power shows up. In China it is the reverse: domestic servers are low-margin assembly, switch silicon is imported, and the big profit is made by selling optics abroad. Export controls and the new US measures against Chinese optics (the 1260H listing and the FCC draft) aim at exactly that profit stream.

Key intuition

A single Ascend 910C delivers roughly a third of a B200's dense compute (prior knowledge, conf: med; see C05), so China needs ~3–5× more accelerators per unit of compute. Every extra accelerator needs its own links, so optics and switch ports per unit of useful compute are several times higher in China A. That is why Huawei built the first all-optical scale-up domain and is now moving optics right next to the chip.

Scale-up: Huawei's SuperPoDs and the other "supernodes"

CloudMatrix 384 (sold as the Atlas 900 A3 SuperPoD, unveiled Apr 2025) links 384 Ascend 910C in 12 compute racks of 32 chips plus 4 scale-up switch racks. It delivers ~289 PFLOPs of dense BF16 (384 × 752 TF), ~1.6× a GB200 NVL72 (SemiAnalysis rounds to 300 PF, ~2×), with 3.6× the memory capacity and 2.1× the memory bandwidth. The cost is ~4× the power (~559 kW vs ~145 kW; SemiAnalysis 4.1×) and ~2.4× worse power per FLOP. It uses 6,912 400G LPO transceivers, "100% optics, 0% copper" E 373874.

Huawei's roadmap takes the same idea much further R 323336:

  • Atlas 950 SuperPoD (announced Sep 2025; "in large-scale commercial use" per the Sep 2026 keynote): 8,192 Ascend 950DT in 160 cabinets (128 compute + 32 comms) on ~1,000 m², 16 PB/s of interconnect, 8 EFLOPS FP8 / 16 EFLOPS FP4, 1,152 TB of memory.
  • Atlas 960E SuperPoD (launched 17 Sep 2026): 4,096 NPUs, 8 EF FP8 / 16 EF FP4, up to 1 PB of HBM, fully liquid-cooled. It wires the pod with 5,500 "Hi-ONE" near-packaged optical engines (7.2 Tb/s each, built-in light source) in place of ~48,000 800G pluggable modules, saving >550 kW, with 99.8% availability.
  • Atlas 960 SuperPoD (Q4 2027 target): 15,488 Ascend 960 in 220 cabinets, 34 PB/s, 30 EF FP8 / 60 EF FP4, 4,460 TB of memory; a SuperCluster of >1m NPUs and 2 ZFLOPS FP8. The Ascend 960DT has been pulled forward to Q1 2027.
  • UnifiedBus (UB, 灵衢) 2.0: spec opened to partners in Sep 2025. Sep 2026 claims: round-trip latency 7 µs → 2 µs; a 176-port × 1.6 Tb/s (280 Tb/s) all-optical "LinkDevice"; a 1,024-radix UBG switch; 512k NPUs in a two-tier, four-plane Clos network and 1m NPUs multi-rail R 73; ~196 km less copper per 4,096-NPU pod R 34.

Alibaba's Panjiu AL128 (Sep 2025) puts 128 accelerators in one cabinet as two 64-GPU scale-up groups joined by ALink switches: copper inside each group, optics between the switches, a backplane-free design, ~350 kW of power and 500 kW of cooling per cabinet, and up to 6.4 Tb/s of direct links per GPU E 3839. Its UPN512 is a 512-accelerator all-optical scale-up network, expandable to 1,000+ and claimed to be 30% cheaper; T-Head's ICN Switch 1.0 offers 25.6 Tb/s and 64-card full interconnect (conf: med) E 39. Sugon sells a 640-card scaleX640 cabinet "supporting 100k-card clusters" R 55; Inspur, H3C, ZTE and Huawei-partner OEMs sell 32/64-card supernodes (prior knowledge, conf: med), and Unisplendour is raising up to RMB 5.41bn (~US$0.81bn) partly for supernode products R 50.

Scale-out: Ethernet won early in China

Chinese platforms bet on RoCE Ethernet years before Western ones. Alibaba's HPN 7.0 (SIGCOMM 2024) put ~15k GPUs in a two-tier, dual-plane pod on 51.2T single-chip switches; Tencent's Xingmai 2.0 claims 100k-GPU scale; ByteDance's MegaScale paper ran >10k GPUs (prior knowledge; conf: high that they exist, med on the numbers). Three reasons: cost and multi-vendor sourcing; InfiniBand ties buyers to Nvidia, whose China access is politically uncertain (China's market regulator SAMR found in Sep 2025 that Nvidia breached the conditions of its Mellanox approval; prior knowledge, conf: high); and domestic accelerators can't use Nvidia's InfiniBand NICs efficiently anyway.

The weak link is the switch chip. The boxes are Chinese, but inside the 25.6T/51.2T switches the silicon is mostly Broadcom Tomahawk, Trident or Jericho: the FT-reported audit by SASAC found Broadcom chips in ~90% of state-owned data centres E 6364. Huawei uses in-house HiSilicon switch chips; Centec (盛科) sells 12.8T/25.6T parts now "entering application" R 53; H3C touts an 800G AI switch with ">95% domestic chips" (conf: low) R 51. Broadcom still ships to China legally: China was 17% of its FY25 revenue, down from 20% R 64.

Optics: what China makes and what it imports

A module is a DSP plus lasers, photodiodes, a driver/TIA, lenses, a circuit board and a housing. Chinese makers dominate assembly, passive components (TFC), silicon-photonics integration (InnoLight's 1.6T SiPh modules are in mass production R 1) and, increasingly, CW lasers (Yuanjie). They depend on Marvell and Broadcom for DSPs and, at 100–200G per lane, on Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom and Mitsubishi for EMLs E 25.

LPO removes the DSP, a neat way around both DSP dependence and power, and Huawei's CloudMatrix uses 400G LPO at scale E 37. NPO and CPO move optics next to or into the chip package: Huawei's Hi-ONE (Sep 2026) is the first NPO SuperPoD R 33, and Accelink showed a 3.2T silicon-photonics NPO in Mar 2026 and a 6.4T NPO in May 2026 R 20.

Thailand is the tariff valve

InnoLight (70,000+ m² in Thailand), Eoptolink (Sriracha) and TFC moved US-bound production offshore between 2019 and 2025; InnoLight and CIG also added Mexico and Malaysia capacity E 22. The 1260H list targets company ownership. The FCC draft reportedly covers "new models of Chinese optical transceivers", and whether that means Chinese-owned or only China-made is unclear, so Thailand may no longer be a full hedge (conf: med) E 2729.

03 · Market sizeA RMB 250bn server market, a small switch market and a world-beating optics export business

AI servers: the biggest pot, measured two ways

CCID puts China's 2025 accelerated-server market at 760k units and RMB 250bn (~US$34.8bn), 54.3% of all server spending (~RMB 460bn) and the first year AI servers outsold general-purpose ones E 44 (seen only via a repost by an Ascend server maker; conf: med). IDC had 2024 at US$22.1bn (+134%) and 1H 2025 at US$16.0bn, and forecasts >US$140bn by 2029 E 4142. Chinese platforms account for ~65–69% of demand.

Inside those servers, IDC counted ~4.0m accelerator cards in 2025: Nvidia ~55%, Chinese vendors ~41% (Huawei ~812k), AMD ~4% E 43. That share shift reshapes this layer: Ascend systems pull Huawei's own UnifiedBus, optics and partner OEMs, not Inspur or H3C Nvidia boxes. (Chip detail is in C05.) CCID's ">3m" AI chips is the all-vendor China total and should be compared with IDC's ~4m, not with the ~1.65m domestic cards (conf: med).

Switches: a US$4bn data-centre market run on Broadcom silicon

IDC sizes China's 2025 Ethernet switch market at RMB 49.5bn (~US$6.9bn, +18.6%), of which data-centre switches were RMB 31.0bn (~US$4.3bn, +37.5%) and campus/enterprise RMB 18.5bn (−3.6%). It forecasts RMB 98.2bn (~US$14.7bn at 6.70) by 2030, a 14.7% compound growth rate E 45. Huawei has been #1 in China data-centre switches for "10 consecutive years" through 2025 R 46; H3C is #1–2 overall; Ruijie claims #1 in 200G/400G data-centre switches in 2024 R 72 thanks to design work for ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent; ZTE is rising in carrier tenders (prior knowledge, conf: med).

Sense check against the West

China's entire data-centre switch market (~US$4.3bn in 2025) is about a third of Arista's 2026 revenue guide (~US$12.6bn) and ~6% of Nvidia's networking run-rate (~US$70bn) A 65. Part of the gap is price (Chinese ports are cheaper), part is that NVLink-style scale-up spending is counted in servers, and part is simply scale.

Optics: China-headquartered module makers

LightCounting ranked InnoLight #1 (>US$3.3bn, +114%) and Eoptolink #3 (US$1.2bn, +175%) among 2024 transceiver suppliers, with Coherent #2 and Accelink, Hisense, HGGenuine (Huagong), Cisco, Huawei, Marvell and Lumentum also in the top tier E 23. Chinese firms held 7 of the top 10 places, and InnoLight plus Eoptolink hold >60% of 800G-and-faster modules E 26; InnoLight alone has ~35–40% of 800G and 50–70% of 1.6T (conf: med) E 822, and its annual report claims a 23.4% share of the whole global market in 2025 5. One third-party estimate puts the 2026 AI transceiver market at ~US$26bn (from US$16.5bn in 2025) and 800G+ units at 24m → 63m (conf: med) E 22; the Atlas uses W07's wider datacom-optics estimate of ~US$34bn for 2026 (range US$31–37bn) A.

1. Optics exporters: scarcity-priced contract manufacturing with intellectual property

Who pays. A handful of US hyperscalers and Nvidia, in US dollars, on annual or multi-year volume allocations. InnoLight says its order book covers all of 2026, some orders run into 2027 and key customers have given 2028 new-product demand guidance R 9. Its top three (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta) are ~40% of revenue (conf: low) E 8.

Pricing. Per-module prices step down each year within a generation, but each speed upgrade (400G → 800G → 1.6T) resets the average price higher. Management said in Jul 2026 that 1.6T prices were "significantly above market speculation" and there was "no destructive price war" R 9. During the 2025–26 shortages of lasers and DSPs, module makers passed costs through and pre-paid suppliers: Eoptolink's prepayments rose 39× quarter-on-quarter to RMB 682m in Q1-26 R 15.

Why margins are high. Yield and speed-to-volume on each new generation (1.6T silicon photonics), in-house silicon-photonics design, Thai plants already qualified by US customers, and scale purchasing of DSPs. Gross margins of 42–49% sit between Coherent (40%) and Lumentum (50%) R 51665.

Headwinds. RMB appreciation: revenue is in dollars, most labour and overhead in RMB or Thai baht. Moving from 7.18 (2025 average) to 6.70 lowers RMB revenue by ~6.7%; with ~60% of cost of goods in dollars, a 45% gross margin falls to ~43.4% A. Eoptolink booked a RMB 522m FX loss in Q1-26 R 15. Plus customer concentration and US policy (section 09).

State role. Modest: tax incentives for high-tech enterprises and export VAT rebates (prior knowledge, conf: med), local land and plant support (Suzhou, Tongling, Chengdu); Accelink is SOE-controlled. The industry was built by private firms serving foreign customers, which is why Washington's 1260H case against InnoLight rests on indirect SASAC ownership R 8.

2. Server OEMs: pass-through assembly financed by the balance sheet

Who pays. Chinese platforms (~65–69% of AI-server demand E 4142), carriers through centralised tenders, and local-government or SOE "intelligent computing centres" (智算中心).

Pricing. The accelerator (an Nvidia China SKU or an Ascend) is 70–85% of the bill of materials (prior knowledge, conf: low) and is passed through at cost-plus. Gross margins: Inspur 4.8% (servers 4.5%), xFusion 8.6%, FII 7.2%, against Dell ISG's ~15% operating margin and Supermicro's 17.5% gross margin in the West R 48576165. Platform tenders are auctions; carriers award to the lowest price; and platforms design their own servers (Alibaba Panjiu) and hand build-to-print work to Inspur or Huaqin, which strips out design margin E 38.

Cash model. Vendors carry huge inventories to secure scarce chips (Inspur RMB 46.5bn = 55% of assets; Sugon +92% year-to-date; Ruijie +90%) and fund them with bank debt. 1H26 operating cash flow was negative at Unisplendour (−RMB 4.2bn), Ruijie (−RMB 3.0bn) and Sugon (−RMB 0.19bn, better than −RMB 1.38bn a year earlier) R 48505255. The profit is a financing spread plus mix: liquid cooling, supernodes, xinchuang (domestic-CPU) servers and after-sales service earn more than commodity GPU boxes.

State role. Heavy. Inspur is controlled by Shandong's SASAC, xFusion by Henan state capital (31.4%) with China Mobile (13.8%), and Sugon sits under the Chinese Academy of Sciences (prior knowledge for Inspur and Sugon). Xinchuang procurement mandates domestic CPUs (Kunpeng, Hygon, Phytium) in state IT, and local computing centres buy servers with local-government money or policy-bank credit (C02, C04).

3. Huawei: vertically integrated "system + cloud + ecosystem"

Platforms (ByteDance, Baidu, iFlytek and carriers reportedly bought CloudMatrix; conf: low), carriers and local governments buy SuperPoDs, and Huawei Cloud rents CloudMatrix capacity (C08). Huawei sells the whole SuperPoD (chips, UB switches, optics, liquid cooling, software) at a system price, and sells Ascend modules to partner OEMs, many of them provincial state-backed firms such as Huakun Zhenyu in Sichuan and Henan Kunlun, which assemble servers for local projects (prior knowledge, conf: med). Cash-rich carrier and enterprise networking businesses fund Ascend R&D and the SuperPoD ramp. Because Huawei controls the chip, the interconnect and the optics, it captures the system margin that in the West is split between Nvidia, Broadcom, the ODM and the optics chain. The reported ~60% price rise on the Ascend 950DT (Sep 2026; conf: low) E 35 suggests Huawei, unlike the server OEMs, has pricing power.

4. Network equipment vendors: enterprise margins, hyperscaler volume

H3C, Huawei, Ruijie and ZTE sell branded switches with software licences through distributors to government and enterprise (higher margin; Ruijie's distributor revenue was RMB 2.92bn R 52) and JDM or white-box switches to the platforms at thin margins (Ruijie's direct sales +51% R 52). Carriers buy through centralised procurement where price dominates. The merchant switch chip, mostly Broadcom, is the largest single cost item and the largest external profit leak.

5. Platforms as in-house designers

Alibaba (Panjiu servers, the AL128 supernode, the T-Head ICN switch, the HPN fabric), Tencent (the Xingmai network, ETH-X) and ByteDance (custom switches via Ruijie and H3C, MegaScale) design their own systems to cut out OEM margin and to stay chip-agnostic between Nvidia, Ascend and their own accelerators E 3839 (prior knowledge for Tencent and ByteDance). That caps every Chinese systems vendor's margin, and is why server makers chase government and SOE demand.

Cross-subsidies, not ads

This is not a consumer layer, so there is no advertising model. The relevant cross-subsidies are: Hygon equity income propping up Sugon (47% of profit) R 55; Huawei's networking and carrier cash funding Ascend; Alibaba's cloud and e-commerce cash funding T-Head and Panjiu; and US hyperscaler capex funding Chinese optics R&D (InnoLight R&D RMB 1.15bn in 1H26, +97%) R 1.

07 · Unit economicsA module maker keeps 28 fen of every yuan; a server maker keeps 1.5

Start with one yuan of revenue and subtract each cost. For InnoLight in 2025, 28 fen was left as net profit A. For Inspur, 1.5 fen A. Same layer, same customers in part, a nineteen-fold difference.

Server-maker margin math A. Inspur's 2025 server revenue of RMB 154.6bn at a 4.52% gross margin gives ~RMB 7.0bn of gross profit; R&D alone took RMB 3.8bn; net profit was RMB 2.4bn, 1.5% of revenue. Every 1 point of server gross margin is worth ~RMB 1.5bn, ~60% of 2025 net profit. That is why 1H26 profit tripled on flat revenue (a mix shift), and why these companies are so sensitive to inventory write-downs (RMB 1.34bn of provisions in 2025) R 48.

11 · BottlenecksWhat limits this layer: imported chips, power and cash

  1. Upstream chips for optics. DSPs, EMLs, CW lasers and silicon-photonics wafers: shortages showed up as Eoptolink's prepayments and InnoLight's inventory build R 515. The global laser shortage (W07) binds Chinese makers too.
  2. Switch silicon at 51.2T and 102.4T. China has no volume domestic 51.2T merchant chip; Centec's flagship is 25.6T R 53. Huawei's in-house chips serve Huawei boxes only.
  3. Power per system. CloudMatrix 384 at ~559 kW and AL128 at ~350 kW per cabinet E 38 need liquid cooling and grid capacity that only new "intelligent computing" parks provide (C03, C04). China's cheap power makes this viable; it would not be in most Western markets.
  4. Working capital. Server and switch vendors are financing inventory with short-term debt (Ruijie short-term borrowings +263%; Inspur inventory 55% of assets) R 4852. A demand pause would force write-downs. Even InnoLight turned only RMB 1.8bn of its RMB 13.7bn first-half profit into operating cash R 1.
  5. Customer concentration. InnoLight: US ~62% of revenue (Q1-26) R 6, top three US customers ~40% (conf: low) E 8. TFC: one customer ~60% (conf: med) 71. FII: top five 62% R 61.
  6. Accelerator supply (C05, C06). Systems volume is capped by Ascend output: SMIC capacity and HBM stockpiles. SemiAnalysis counted 13m HBM stacks, enough for ~1.6m 910C packages, as of Apr 2025 E 37. Server makers can't ship what they can't source.
  7. Data reliability. Huawei's ">1,000 SuperPoDs" is unaudited; IDC and CCID market sizes diverge; press translations mis-scale RMB units.

12 · Competitive dynamicsEight contests that decide who wins

  1. Pluggable vs NPO/CPO. Huawei's Hi-ONE NPO SuperPoD (Sep 2026) and Nvidia's Spectrum-X/Quantum-X Photonics CPO (W07) both threaten the pluggable franchise that made InnoLight and Eoptolink rich. The Jul 2026 sell-off in "CPO-sector" stocks (RMB 400bn+ of net outflows over 60 days, per the 21st Century Business Herald) reflected those fears E 9. Chinese makers are hedging: InnoLight 1.6T silicon photonics in mass production and 3.2T from ~2028 R 15; Accelink 3.2T/6.4T NPO R 20; TFC optical engines R 18; Ruijie CPO switches R 52. Most analysts (and Arista, W07) still expect pluggables to dominate through 2027.
  2. Western re-shoring of optics. Nvidia/Corning, Lumentum (North Carolina fab), Coherent (Sherman, Texas), AAOI (Texas) and Fabrinet are expanding. The FCC draft and 1260H give hyperscalers a reason to dual-source, even at higher cost. Coherent (+10%), Lumentum (+9%) and Marvell (+8%) rose on the FCC report E 26.
  3. Huawei vs everyone in domestic systems. With Ascend at ~49% of domestic accelerator shipments (IDC, 2025) E 43 and SuperPoDs sold as full systems, Huawei captures chip, interconnect, optics and integration margins, leaving Inspur and H3C with Nvidia and non-Huawei domestic-chip servers. Partner OEMs assemble Huawei boards at thin margins.
  4. Platform self-design. Alibaba (AL128, UPN512, ICN switch), Tencent and ByteDance increasingly design systems and white-box switches, commoditising OEMs (Inspur, H3C) and JDMs (Ruijie) E 3839.
  5. Domestic switch silicon. The SASAC audit (Sep 2026) is the first sign that Beijing will do to Broadcom switches what it did to Nvidia GPUs: discourage purchases E 6364. Winners: Huawei (own chips), Centec, and H3C or Ruijie if they adopt domestic silicon. Risk: a performance and efficiency gap (the FT says Huawei chips "still lag Broadcom" on efficiency) E 64.
  6. Scale-up standards fragmentation. UB (Huawei), ALink (Alibaba), ETH-X (Tencent/ODCC) and OISA (China Mobile) compete; non-Huawei accelerator makers must pick one.
  7. Ownership reshuffles. The Hygon–Sugon merger is dead (Dec 2025) R 56; Unisplendour has tightened control of H3C (87.98%) R 50; xFusion's IPO and Eoptolink's and Accelink's H-share listings bring more capital, and more disclosure.
  8. Lenovo as a bridge. A China-headquartered OEM taking share in Western AI servers (ISG +98%, US$54bn pipeline) R 59: an exposure if US "trusted supplier" rules widen from optics to servers.

13 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

Optics demand outruns policy, and home clusters are optics-hungry

  • Western AI capex rises from ~$1.0T (2026) to ~$1.35T (2027) A; 1.6T ramps "≥5× faster" than 800G; Chinese makers hold >60% of 800G+ and ~50–70% of 1.6T E 826. Even with an FCC bar on new models, authorised SKUs and Thai/Mexican capacity keep shipments flowing through 2027. InnoLight's 2027 forecast (net profit ~RMB 47.6bn E 4) implies ~20–23× earnings at ~RMB 1.1tn.
  • Domestic clusters are optics-hungry. Optical scale-up needs ~2–4× more optical bandwidth per accelerator than NVL72 A; >1,000 SuperPoDs in the field and Atlas 950/960 ramps mean a large captive market for domestic modules and lasers (Yuanjie +640%).
  • System-level parity is arriving. Atlas 960E (8 EF FP8 at 4,096 NPUs) and UB's 2 µs latency suggest Huawei can match Nvidia's rack-scale designs in capability, if not power efficiency; cheap power neutralises the gap (C03).
  • Import substitution in switching (Broadcom → Huawei/Centec) creates a new domestic pool of ~RMB 5–8bn a year of switch-chip spend A.
  • Server makers' margins have bottomed: Inspur's profit +270% on +5% revenue in 1H26 as mix improves R 47; Lenovo ISG at a record 9.1% R 59.

Bear case

Washington can switch off the most profitable part of this layer

  • Policy. The 1260H product ban (Jun 2027) plus an FCC new-model bar would lock InnoLight and Eoptolink out of 3.2T and later generations in the US; ~62% of InnoLight's revenue is US R 6. Western capacity additions (Coherent, Lumentum, Fabrinet, AAOI) are timed for 2027–28.
  • Technology substitution. CPO/NPO removes switch-side pluggables (roughly half the modules) and Huawei's NPO removes domestic pluggables from its pods A. Growth is already slowing: InnoLight Q2 revenue +14% q/q; Eoptolink Q1 profit −13% q/q R 215.
  • Valuation and cash flow. InnoLight's 1H26 operating cash flow was RMB 1.8bn against RMB 13.7bn of profit; inventories and receivables are ballooning across the sector (Ruijie, Sugon, Unisplendour negative) R 1505255. RMB appreciation squeezes exporters' margins A.
  • Domestic systems stay a no-margin business: platform self-design, carrier tenders and Huawei's vertical integration leave Inspur, H3C and ZTE at 1.5–4% net. ZTE's profit fell 46% while pivoting to compute R 54.
  • Upstream dependence (800G/1.6T DSPs <5% domestic, 200G EMLs imported) means US controls on DSPs or lasers would hurt China's optics exports and its domestic clusters at once E 2425.

What is structurally different

  1. China substitutes scale and optics for chip quality. Because domestic accelerators are weaker, China builds larger, all-optical scale-up domains and pays in power, which is cheap there. The West keeps scale-up in copper as long as it can.
  2. The profit pool is inverted. In the West the chip vendor (Nvidia) captures the system margin; in China domestic systems vendors earn 1.5–4% net and the rich margins sit with optics exporters selling to US hyperscalers, plus, opaquely, Huawei.
  3. China's champion in this layer depends on its rival's capex. InnoLight's and Eoptolink's growth is US AI capex, and US policy (1260H, FCC) can cap it. There is no Western equivalent, other than Nvidia's China revenue (C05).
  4. Dependence runs both ways. China depends on Broadcom switch chips and Marvell/Broadcom DSPs; the US depends on Chinese modules and Chinese indium and gallium. Neither side can decouple quickly.
  5. The state is a buyer and an owner. Henan owns xFusion, Shandong SASAC controls Inspur, CAS sits behind Sugon, CICT behind Accelink, and SASAC can redirect SOE switch purchases. In the West the state mostly regulates and subsidises.
  6. Platforms are more vertically integrated in design, less in silicon. Alibaba, Tencent and ByteDance design servers, switches and supernodes but mostly buy switch silicon from Broadcom and accelerators from Nvidia or Huawei, with in-house chips (T-Head) growing.

17 · GlossaryTerms on this page

1260H list
The US Defense Department's list of "Chinese military companies" (NDAA FY2021 §1260H). Under §805 of the FY2024 NDAA the Pentagon may not contract with listed firms from 30 Jun 2026 or buy their products from 30 Jun 2027. InnoLight was added on 8 Jun 2026.
ALink
Alibaba's accelerator interconnect protocol, used in its 128-accelerator Panjiu AL128 supernode and its 512-accelerator all-optical UPN512 network.
Ascend
Huawei's AI chip (NPU) family (昇腾): 910B, 910C, 950PR/DT, 960. See C05.
Ascend partner OEM
A server maker, often provincial and state-backed, that builds servers around Huawei Ascend modules, such as Huakun Zhenyu (华鲲振宇) in Sichuan or Henan Kunlun.
Atlas SuperPoD
Huawei's scale-up systems: Atlas 900 A3 (the CloudMatrix 384, 384 chips), Atlas 950 (8,192), Atlas 960E (4,096, with NPO optics) and Atlas 960 (15,488).
CCID
CCID Consulting (赛迪顾问), a research firm affiliated with China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology.
Clos
A multi-tier switch network (leaf and spine) in which every endpoint can reach every other through a few hops; the standard data-centre topology.
CloudMatrix 384
Huawei's 384-Ascend-910C all-optical scale-up system in 16 racks; CloudMatrix is the cloud-service name, Atlas 900 A3 SuperPoD the product name.
CPO
Co-packaged optics: optical engines inside the switch or accelerator package, removing pluggable modules.
CW laser
Continuous-wave laser: a steady light source used with silicon-photonics modulators. Yuanjie is China's leading maker.
DSP
Digital signal processor (in optics): the PAM4 chip inside a module that cleans up high-speed signals. Marvell and Broadcom dominate; China's self-sufficiency at 800G/1.6T is under 5%.
EML
Electro-absorption modulated laser: a laser with a built-in modulator, used at 100–200G per lane. Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom and Mitsubishi lead.
ETH-X
An Ethernet-based scale-up specification led by the ODCC and Tencent (2024).
FCC equipment authorization
US approval required before electronic devices can be marketed. A reported FCC draft would withhold it from new Chinese optical-module models.
Hi-ONE
Huawei's 7.2 Tb/s near-packaged optical engine with a built-in light source, used in the Atlas 960E SuperPoD.
HPN / Xingmai
Alibaba's High Performance Network and Tencent's Xingmai (星脉) network: the platforms' Ethernet AI fabrics.
Intelligent computing center
An AI data centre (智算中心), often built by a local government or a carrier.
JDM
Joint design manufacturer: a vendor that co-designs white-box hardware with the buyer and builds it, as Ruijie does for Chinese platforms.
LPO
Linear-drive pluggable optics: a pluggable module with no DSP, which saves power and cost over shorter reaches. Huawei's CloudMatrix 384 uses 6,912 400G LPO modules.
NPO
Near-packaged optics: optical engines mounted next to the chip package on the board. Huawei's Atlas 960E is the first NPO SuperPoD.
OISA
A scale-up interconnect architecture led by China Mobile.
Optical module
A transceiver that converts electrical signals to light and back, plugged into switches and servers. Also called an optical transceiver (光模块).
RoCE
RDMA over Converged Ethernet: lets servers read each other's memory directly over Ethernet. The transport used in Chinese AI fabrics.
SASAC
State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (国资委): owner and regulator of central SOEs; provincial SASACs own local ones, such as Inspur.
Scale-out
The data-centre network that links thousands of servers or scale-up groups, usually Ethernet or InfiniBand.
Scale-up
The very fast network that makes a group of chips act as one large chip with shared memory: NVLink in the West, UnifiedBus or ALink in China.
SiPh
Silicon photonics: building optical components on silicon wafers, used in 800G/1.6T modules. It needs an external CW laser.
SuperPoD
A scale-up domain larger than one server, where accelerators share memory-semantic links. Chinese: 超节点 (supernode).
UnifiedBus
Huawei's scale-up and scale-out interconnect protocol (UB, 灵衢); the 2.0 spec was opened to partners in 2025.
Xinchuang
Xinchuang (信创), "IT application innovation": state procurement rules favouring domestic CPUs, operating systems and servers.
易中天
Market nickname for the three optics leaders: Eoptolink (易, 新易盛), InnoLight (中, 中际旭创) and TFC (天, 天孚通信).

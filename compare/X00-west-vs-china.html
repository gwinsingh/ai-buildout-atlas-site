The 60-second version

  1. The West outspends China about 7.5 to 1 on AI data centres, and the ratio is stable. ~$1.0T against ~RMB 0.9tn (~US$134bn) in 2026 A; it was ~5.5× in 2024 and ~7.1× in 2025, and both base cases keep it at 7–8× to 2030.
  2. Both builds are paid for mostly with old profits. Legacy cash covers ~49% of Western flows and ~47% of China's (including balance-sheet drawdown). At the margin, the West adds the bond market, SPVs and vendor money; China adds the state and state-owned firms (~15% vs ~3%) A.
  3. China builds cheap physical capacity but pays more for useful compute. Power costs a third to a half, a data-centre shell a quarter, and grid hook-ups come 4–5× faster. But its chips need ~2.4× the power per FLOP, so each EFLOPS of compute costs ~1.3–2.2× more to build A.
  4. The profit gap (~38×) is five times the spending gap. The West has a ~75%-gross-margin chip monopolist and scarcity rents at every chokepoint: $1 of capex leaves ~51¢ of supplier profit. China's middle is crowded and state-disciplined: ¥1 leaves ~10 fen, and net of platform and lab losses its AI profit pool is about zero A.
  5. Tokens are abundant in China; revenue is scarce. China serves more than 140 trillion tokens a day at list prices ~1.3–4× below the West's, and Chinese open-weight models carry about half of OpenRouter traffic. Yet its independent labs book ~US$2.4bn in 2026 against ~$96B for OpenAI and Anthropic (~1:40).
  6. The two systems are decoupling at the chip and re-coupling everywhere else. Nvidia's China data-centre sales are under 1% of its data-centre revenue, but Chinese optics makers earn ~US$15bn of profit on the West's build, China holds the rare-earth lever, and both sides' levers are paused until 9–10 Nov 2026.

01 · Explain like I'm newTwo build-outs, one ladder: how to read this comparison

The atlas describes each AI build-out as a ten-rung ladder, from electricity at the bottom to apps at the top. The West and China climb the same ladder with the same definitions, so every rung can be compared directly. The short answer: China spends about an eighth as much, builds its physical rungs faster and cheaper, runs short of good chips, and keeps very little profit.

This page adds very little new research. It sets the Western track (W01–W11) beside the China track (C01–C11) and explains how and why they differ. Four web searches filled comparisons neither track had: global installed AI compute, Google's token volume across all its products, and the size of the US Genesis Mission 515455. Where this page derives a new comparison (a ratio, a reconciliation, a joint scenario probability) it is tagged A and the arithmetic is shown.

Analogy

Picture two restaurant chains opening kitchens at the same time. The Western chain rents expensive city-centre space, waits years for a gas hook-up, and buys the best ovens in the world from one supplier who charges whatever he likes, but its customers pay full price and the oven maker gets rich. The Chinese chain builds huge kitchens on cheap land with cheap gas in months, but it is barred from buying the best ovens, so it installs five weaker ovens for every good one and pays for the extra gas. Then it gives most of the food away to win customers. Both kitchens are busy. Only one of them is making anyone rich.

How do I read a ratio like “7.5×” on this page?

Every ratio is West ÷ China, both in US dollars. “Capex 7.5×” means the West spends 7.5 dollars for every dollar China spends. A ratio below 1× means China's number is the bigger one (China added ~550 GW of power generation in 2025 against the US's 53 GW, a ratio of ~0.1×).

China's yuan figures are converted at 6.70 RMB per US$ for 2026 and for point-in-time values, 7.18 for 2025 and 7.19 for 2024 23. One side effect: China's 2025→2026 growth looks ~7% faster in dollars than in yuan (7.18 ÷ 6.70 = 1.07), so growth rates here are computed in yuan where possible A.

Provenance and confidence

  • Almost every cross-layer number on either track is an Atlas estimate A: no company reports “AI profit”, ByteDance is private, and Chinese state spending is inferred. The Western synthesis carries about ±5% error on calendar-year figures; China's carries ±10–15%, mostly from ByteDance and the state 1123.
  • Western layer totals are “order-of-magnitude-correct and directionally reliable” (medium confidence for compute silicon and the chip supply chain, low elsewhere); China's have wider error bars (medium for demand, silicon and the chip supply chain, low elsewhere). Every ratio inherits the worse of the two.

Seven things that are not like-for-like

  1. Disclosure. The West has 10-Qs, RPO, ISO queues and EIA data; China has official aggregates with shifting definitions (e.g. data-centre electricity: NEA 170 TWh vs IEA-comparable ~100–105 TWh) and no backlog disclosure 1513. We use official Chinese figures and flag the IEA-comparable where it matters.
  2. ByteDance. China's largest spender is private: its 2026 capex is >RMB 200bn (~US$30bn); up to US$70bn considered E. At Bloomberg's upper bound China's 2026 capex is ~RMB 1.1tn (~US$164bn) A conf: low, which would cut the West ÷ China ratio from 7.5× to ~6.1× (1,000 ÷ 164) A.
  3. State spending. Special-bond computing centres, policy-bank capital and guidance-fund equity are inferred conf: low 23. The West's sovereign line (~$35B) is a much smaller, better-documented residual.
  4. Token counts. Doubao's 180tn/day includes internal and free traffic; Google's 22B/min is first-party API only; Google's ~3.2 quadrillion/month (May 2026) is all surfaces. None are like-for-like 23.
  5. Compute stock. China reports FP16 EFLOPS (MIIT); the West has no official stock figure. Our cross-check (§5.4) uses Epoch AI's H100-equivalents conf: low.
  6. Accounting. Server useful lives are ~3-5 years (US Big-4: 5-6) A conf: med, so Chinese reported profits are more conservative for the same asset 23.
  7. Grey compute. Smuggled Nvidia compute (~660k H100e (≈⅓ of China's AI compute; 90% CI 290k-1.6m)) E has no Western analogue and is the largest single uncertainty in China's effective compute.

Five patterns in the dashboard

  1. Money ratios cluster at 7–9× (capex, Big-4 capex, quarterly capex, end-customer money, debt, cloud) A. In AI spending, China is about one-eighth the size of the West.
  2. Profit and valuation ratios are 15–40× (accelerator market, profit pool, lab revenue, lab valuation). China captures far less value per yuan spent than the West does per dollar.
  3. Physical ratios invert (power added, power price, shell cost). China builds more physical capacity per yuan.
  4. Usage ratios invert too (tokens, users of free AI). China's constraint is not demand; it is willingness to pay and useful compute.
  5. The financial-risk ratio is 50–80× (contracted backlog). The West's build is underwritten by contracts with two cash-burning labs; China's by four platforms and the state.

Three conclusions

  1. Each useful EFLOPS costs China ~1.3–2× more to build and ~1.3–1.9× more to own and run A. This is the “performance tax” of export controls 13.
  2. Neither cheap power nor cheap capital closes the gap. 07 · Business modelsHow each system makes money, layer by layer

The West's model is suppliers sell scarcity for capex dollars raised against future end-customer revenue. China's is platforms cross-subsidize AI out of ads and commerce, the state fills the gaps and mandates domestic suppliers, and consumers are paid to adopt.

Why the West has a ~75%-margin monopolist and China a thin, zero-sum pool

  1. Where the chokepoint sits and who owns it. Western scarcity (CoWoS, HBM, EUV, leading-edge wafers, CUDA) is owned by profitable private specialists (Nvidia, TSMC, SK hynix, ASML) that price to the market. China's scarce inputs (HBM, 7/5nm wafers, EUV) are national projects run at a loss (CXMT HBM ~25% yields; SMIC N+2 20–40%); its scarcity rents go to foreign memory makers, offshore hosts and owners of Nvidia stockpiles 23.
  2. Buyer power. Western buyers bid against each other for supply; a month's delay on 1 GW costs ~$500m of idle depreciation, so they pay for speed 3. Chinese buyers are few, big and state-backed, and the state mandates domestic procurement: Cambricon's top five customers are 88.7% of revenue 23.
  3. Software moat. CUDA makes a Nvidia cluster re-rentable to anyone, which lowers its financing cost; CANN/MUSA/DTK fragment China's market across nine vendors 1117.
  4. Open weights commoditize Chinese models and hand surplus to users and inference hosts; Western closed weights keep pricing power at the lab 2321.
  5. The state takes strategic, not financial, returns in China: it pays in power, land and vouchers and wants self-sufficiency, not margin 23.
  6. The margin gradient is the same shape, ~40 points lower. Both tracks show operating margin falling toward the customer (West: tools/memory 35–80%, Nvidia/TSMC 58–66%, clouds 30–39%, labs −47%; China: memory ~50%, Cambricon ~40%, clouds ~6%, labs −125%, consumer apps −200%), but China's curve "sits ~40 points lower because there is no 66%-OM Nvidia at the top" 2311.

Is China's pool zero-sum?

Net of lab and consumer-AI losses it is ~US$0bn A: every yuan of supplier profit is offset by a yuan of platform or lab loss. The West's net pool (~$589B) is positive because its suppliers' profits are paid by capex funded with legacy cash and debt, and its losses are concentrated in a handful of labs. Both pools are “capex-funded”; the West's is simply far better at turning capex into supplier margin.

13 · ScorecardWhat each side does better, and who leads each layer

The West does better at

  1. Turning capex into profit and frontier capability — ~51¢ of supplier profit per capex dollar, ~7-month capability lead, ~40× the lab revenue A.
  2. Charging for AI — ~$130B of direct spend with consumers and enterprises paying; seats, usage and outcome pricing; China's consumers are paid to adopt 1022.
  3. Compute efficiency — ~2.4× the PF/kW, ~3–4.5× the EFLOPS per GW, a mature software stack (CUDA) that keeps utilization high 17.
  4. Deep, flexible capital markets — IG bonds, SPVs, private credit, a $75B IPO; they fund speed, at the price of hidden leverage 2.

China does better at

  1. Building physical infrastructure fast and cheap — ~10× the generation additions, ¼ the shell cost, 12-month builds, 4–5× faster to power 1516.
  2. Serving tokens cheaply at enormous scale — >140tn/day nationally; flagship prices ~¼ of the West's; open weights that half the world's developers now route to 2321.
  3. Integrating the stack inside one firm — model, cloud, app, payments and merchants in one P&L, no "lab tax" 22.
  4. Carrying a build with less financial fragility — no lab backlog, little leverage, ~350bp cheaper capital, a state floor; a demand miss becomes a capex cut, not a credit event 23.

What each could learn from the other

  • The West from China: planned grid interconnection and time-to-power (the US queue is >5 years); PUE and siting rules that move load to cheap power; the discipline of a buyer side that refuses to pay a 75% gross margin (the ASIC push is the West's version); open weights as a distribution strategy.
  • China from the West: that monetization, not tokens, pays for capex (the West's end-customer money is ~8.5× larger on ~7.5× the capex); that utilization is a software problem (CUDA's fungibility lowers financing costs); that transparent, contract-backed demand (RPO) makes capital cheaper for private builders, not just for the state.

16 · GlossaryTerms on this page

West / China (this page)
West = every non-China buyer and supplier, including Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Gulf. China = mainland-domiciled companies plus Hong Kong, including capacity they own or rent offshore.
Positive / net profit pool
The sum of AI-attributable operating profits of profitable layers; net subtracts lab, app and consumer-AI losses.
Paid abroad
The part of China’s AI capex that goes to foreign suppliers or offshore capacity (~21%). The West has no equivalent line because its supply chain sits inside its own definition.
State-directed vs state-shaped capex
State-directed = spent by carriers, SOEs and local-government centres (~19%). State-shaped = any capex whose chip mix is set by procurement mandates or NDRC approvals (~89%).
EFLOPS
ExaFLOPS: 10^18 floating-point operations per second. China’s official compute statistics are in dense FP16 EFLOPS (智能算力规模, “intelligent computing scale”).
Capex per EFLOPS
All-in data-centre capex divided by dense FP16 compute delivered; the measure on which China is dearer.
EFLOPS per GW (compute density)
Dense FP16 exaFLOPS installed per GW of facility power: ~1,250 for Blackwell-class, ~280–420 for Ascend-class.
H100e
H100-equivalents: compute expressed as a number of Nvidia H100 GPUs (~1 PF dense FP16 each).
Capital recovery factor (CRF)
The annual payment that repays an asset and earns a required return over its life; used here to compare the cost of owning compute.
Performance tax
The extra capex and power China pays per unit of compute because export controls force it onto weaker chips.
Circular (strict / broad)
Lab equity from compute and chip suppliers (strict), plus strategic partners and component makers (broad).
Equity-for-purchase
An investor’s equity stake paired with a commitment to buy the investee’s product (Kunlunxin cornerstones; AMD warrants to OpenAI and Meta run the other way).
Levy regime
The 15% (H20) and 25% (H200) share of licensed Nvidia/AMD China revenue due to the US government, collected via a narrow Section 232 tariff (conf: med on the mechanism).
Truce expiries
The US Affiliates Rule suspension (to 9 Nov 2026) and China’s pause of rare-earth, gallium and germanium controls (to 10 Nov 2026).
Offshore capacity
Chinese-controlled compute outside the mainland (Johor, Japan, Brazil), mostly Nvidia Blackwell, bought or rented to sidestep controls.
Genesis Mission
The US federal AI-for-science programme launched by executive order on 24 Nov 2025; >$5bn announced in Jul 2026.
Red-envelope war (红包大战)
Chinese platforms paying users cash and free orders to adopt AI apps (~RMB 9bn at Spring Festival 2026).
East Data, West Compute (东数西算)
China’s national plan that sites data centres in eight hubs, pushing compute load toward cheap western power.
LGFV (地方政府融资平台)
Local-government financing vehicle: a state-owned company that borrows for local projects, including computing centres financed with 20-year special bonds (专项债).
NDRC (国家发展和改革委员会)
National Development and Reform Commission, China’s planning agency; it approves data-centre projects and now each Nvidia H200 purchase.
SASAC (国资委)
State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission; owner of the carriers and many SOEs, auditing Broadcom chips in state data centres since Sep 2026.
STAR Market (科创板)
Shanghai’s technology board, which fast-tracks chip and AI IPOs; retail investors set marginal prices.
Big Fund (大基金)
China’s National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund; phase III holds RMB 344bn (~US$51bn), with six state banks as shareholders.
SuperPoD (超节点)
Huawei’s rack-scale systems that link hundreds to thousands of Ascend chips optically (CloudMatrix 384; Atlas 950/960), trading power and optics for chip quality.
AIDC
AI data-centre developer: a company, often state-linked in China, that builds and leases AI data centres.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: stacked DRAM beside the processor. Banned for export to China since Dec 2024; CXMT is China’s only volume producer.
CUDA / CANN
Nvidia’s and Huawei’s software stacks for programming their chips. CUDA’s maturity makes Nvidia clusters easy to re-rent; CANN’s immaturity depresses Ascend utilization.
OCF
Operating cash flow: cash generated by the business before capital spending. Capex ÷ OCF above 100% means borrowing or drawing down cash to build.
UST / CGB
US Treasury and Chinese Government Bond yields; the 10-year gap was ~350bp on 24 Sep 2026.
OpenRouter
A marketplace that routes developers’ API calls to many model providers; its token shares are a public proxy for open-weight usage.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total facility power ÷ IT power. China’s law caps new hub data centres at 1.25.
CoWoS / EUV
TSMC’s advanced packaging for AI chips, and extreme-ultraviolet lithography (ASML only); two Western chokepoints China cannot access.

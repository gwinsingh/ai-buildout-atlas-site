The 60-second version

  1. AI chips are useless alone; the network makes them one machine. NVIDIA's networking revenue went from about $3bn a quarter in late 2024 to about $17.5bn in May–Jul 2026, roughly a $70bn annual run-rate and a fifth of its data-center sales A53.
  2. Ethernet has won the between-rack network; the fight has moved inside the rack. Ethernet is about two-thirds of AI back-end switch sales E53, and Dell'Oro expects in-rack (scale-up) networking to be over half of a nearly $1 trillion five-year market by 2030 E52.
  3. Lasers are the tightest bottleneck, and their makers are winning. Lumentum's revenue doubled to $1.006bn a quarter at a 36.6% operating margin R35; Broadcom says laser demand is "far surpassing supply" 17; Arista expects shortages "until 2028" 12.
  4. The optics market roughly triples in two years. Datacom optics went from about $11bn (2024) to $19bn+ (2025) E75, and the Atlas triangulates ~$31–37bn for 2026 (central ~$34bn) A74. Two China-headquartered module makers, InnoLight and Eoptolink, sold about $8.8bn in H1-26 alone R8386.
  5. Servers are a huge business with thin margins. Dell has a $95bn AI backlog R49 and Supermicro guides $65–72bn of revenue G46. Foxconn earns a 3.75% operating margin R51, and Quanta doubled its revenue in Q2-26 at 3.3% R88, because most of a rack's value is NVIDIA silicon passing through.
  6. NVIDIA keeps about 6 of every 7 gross-profit dollars in a GB300 rack (~86%) A. Outside NVIDIA, the fattest margins are in connectivity chips (Astera 73.7%, Credo 68%) and scarce lasers R3029.
  7. Watch NVIDIA's networking growth vs compute, Broadcom's networking share of AI revenue, Arista's deferred revenue and its reliance on Microsoft and Meta (42% of FY25 revenue 71), laser lead times, 1.6T module prices and Dell's backlog.

01 · Explain like I'm newWhat this layer is, in plain words

One GPU is useless for frontier AI. Training or serving a large model takes tens of thousands to millions of accelerators working as if they were one computer, swapping data many times a second.

The network is the nervous system that makes this possible. The optics are the lasers and fiber that carry signals farther than copper can. The servers and systems layer is the factory that bolts chips, boards, power supplies, cold plates and cables into racks and ships them to data centers, 120–140 kW at a time. The chips themselves are W05; HBM memory, CoWoS packaging and foundry are W06; the building, power and cooling plant are W04.

Analogy

Think of an AI cluster as a huge orchestra. The GPUs are the musicians. Scale-up networking (NVLink) seats one section close together so its players hear each other instantly. Scale-out networking (InfiniBand or Ethernet) carries the conductor's signal to every section in the hall. Scale-across networking links orchestras in different cities playing the same symphony. Optics are the sound system: past a few metres the unamplified voice (copper) doesn't carry, so you need amplification (lasers). The ODMs are the stagehands who build the stage: indispensable, but paid by the hour, not by ticket sales.

The picture below zooms out in three steps. Each step is a different network, bought from different vendors, with a different amount of bandwidth per GPU.

Why not just use one big network for everything?

Because the traffic is very different. Inside a rack, GPUs share memory and trade the pieces of one model (for example the "experts" in a mixture-of-experts model) all the time, so they need enormous bandwidth over short distances. Copper does that cheaply up to about 2 metres.

Between racks, the traffic is lighter (mostly syncing results in data-parallel training) but has to reach every GPU in a building, which takes switches in two or three tiers and lasers for the longer runs. Between sites, distance dominates and you need telecom-grade optics. Three jobs, three price points, three sets of vendors.

Definition

Back-end network: the GPU-to-GPU networks (scale-up plus scale-out) that carry training and inference traffic. The front-end network is the ordinary data-center Ethernet that links servers to storage, users and the internet.

Why it matters

In 2023 networking was an afterthought, about 8–10% of NVIDIA's data-center sales. By mid-2026 it is about 20% A, NVIDIA calls itself "the largest… network company in the world" 34, and lasers are the scarcest input in the AI supply chain after CoWoS and HBM. Whoever controls the fabric controls how big a cluster can be. That is why NVIDIA, Broadcom and the hyperscalers fight so hard over NVLink versus Ethernet.

03 · Scale-upInside the rack: NVLink and the open challengers

Scale-up is where NVIDIA's lead is widest and where the next fight is. The unit of sale is no longer a server but a whole liquid-cooled rack.

The NVL racks, generation by generation

  • GB200 / GB300 NVL72 R5657: 72 Blackwell (or Blackwell Ultra) GPUs and 36 Grace CPUs in 18 compute trays, plus 9 NVLink switch trays holding 18 NVSwitch chips. The rack has 130 TB/s of aggregate NVLink bandwidth. GB300 carries about 20 TB of HBM3E (GB200: 13.4 TB). The trays are joined by a copper "spine" of about 5,184 differential-pair cables 60. Power is about 120–140 kW per rack (SemiAnalysis measured ~123.6 kW for GB200 E60), and the racks are fully liquid-cooled.
  • Vera Rubin NVL72: 72 Rubin GPUs and 36 Vera CPUs, 20.7 TB of HBM4 at 1,400 TB/s, 216 TB/s of NVLink 6, 32.4 TB/s of scale-out bandwidth through ConnectX-9, and 3.6 EF of NVFP4 inference. NVIDIA says it is "ramping into full production" R58; Supermicro began shipping racks on 23 Sep 2026 48. (NVIDIA renamed "NVL144" to NVL72 at CES 2026, counting packages rather than dies; older knowledge.)
  • Rubin Ultra / Kyber (NVL576-class): NVIDIA's GTC 2025 roadmap put a 576-die Kyber rack in 2H 2027 (older knowledge, conf: med). SemiAnalysis noted that an NVL576 built with the GB200-era copper/optics mix would need more than $5.6m of interconnect per rack (~$9.7k per GPU) E60. That cost is what pushes the industry toward optics inside the rack.
  • NVLink Fusion (announced May 2025) licenses NVLink to other companies' CPUs and ASICs. Partners include MediaTek (NVIDIA put $3.5bn into a MediaTek convertible bond on 31 Aug 2026 R65), Marvell (a "$2bn NVIDIA partnership" per trade press, conf: med 26), Intel (NVIDIA's $5bn investment, Sep 2025; older knowledge), Fujitsu and Arm 8.

The open alternatives to NVLink

Everyone who is not NVIDIA wants an open scale-up standard, so that AMD GPUs, Google TPUs and custom chips can build racks as large as NVL72 without paying NVIDIA for the fabric.

04 · Scale-outBetween racks: Ethernet won the fight, and NVIDIA won anyway

InfiniBand came to NVIDIA with Mellanox (a $6.9bn deal announced March 2019, closed April 2020; older knowledge). It is lossless by design, has very low tail latency and can do sums inside the switches (SHARP). NVIDIA is essentially its only vendor: Quantum-2 (400G), then Quantum-X800 (800G), then Quantum-X Photonics with co-packaged optics.

Ethernet for AI needed fixes for its lossy, congestion-prone behaviour: RoCEv2, adaptive routing, packet spraying and better congestion control. Three routes emerged:

  1. NVIDIA Spectrum-X: Spectrum-4/-6 switches plus SuperNICs (ConnectX-8/9, BlueField). The end-to-end "Ethernet that behaves like InfiniBand". Adopters include Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and xAI 8. It grew 2.6× year on year in Q2 FY27 R3.
  2. Merchant silicon plus network software: Broadcom Tomahawk 5/6 and Jericho 3-AI/4 inside Arista, Celestica/white-box, Cisco and Juniper switches. Hyperscalers write or customise their own software (Meta FBOSS, Microsoft SONiC).
  3. Ultra Ethernet Consortium (UEC): an open transport (UET) with packet spraying, multipath and new congestion control. Spec 1.0 (Jun 2025) → 1.0.3 (Aug 2026) R62.

Where share stands

Ethernet was about two-thirds of AI back-end switch sales in 1Q26. InfiniBand sales "more than tripled" in the quarter but stayed the minority, and 800G ports dominated deployments E53. Dell'Oro did not publish exact percentage shares, so the split below is approximate.

05 · TopologiesHow the wiring pattern decides how many lasers you buy

  • Fat tree / Clos: leaf switches (top of rack or end of row), then spine, then super-spine. A non-blocking design gives every GPU full bandwidth to every other GPU. Two tiers of 64-port 800G switches reach 2,048 GPUs; three tiers reach 65k. With 128-port or 512-port (102.4T) switches, two tiers reach about 8k–32k GPUs, and NVIDIA's Q3450 co-packaged switch connects "over 10,000 GPUs in a non-blocking two-level fat tree" R59.
  • Rail-optimized: GPU k of every server connects to "rail switch" k, so most collective traffic stays one hop away.
  • Why tiers matter: each extra tier adds another set of links, and above the rack almost all of them are optical, because copper runs out at about 2–3 m at 200G per lane. Flatter networks built from bigger switches (Tomahawk 6, Spectrum-6) cut optics per GPU. That is a quiet headwind for transceiver volumes per GPU even as GPU volumes explode.
  • Scale-across: NVIDIA Spectrum-XGS (Aug 2025) and Broadcom Jericho 4 (Aug 2025) add deep buffers and distance-aware congestion control so two or more campuses tens to hundreds of km apart can train one model (older knowledge). Arista says scale-across is already about 30% of its AI revenue R13.

Reality sits in between: rails, oversubscription at the upper tiers, and AECs inside rows. Industry rules of thumb of ~2.5–3.5 800G modules per Blackwell GPU fit the 2–6 range (older knowledge, conf: med). With Rubin's 1.6T network cards, module count per GPU stays similar but each module doubles in speed, so optics dollars per GPU rise about 1.5–1.8× (a 1.6T module costs roughly 1.6–1.8× an 800G one; older knowledge, conf: low) A. Co-packaged optics removes the switch-side pluggables (about half the modules) but not the network-card side until card-level CPO arrives.

Unit prices (older knowledge, directional): 400G ~$300–400 → 800G ~$600–900 → 1.6T ~$1,100–1,500 per module at volume. Cost per bit falls ~30–40% per generation while dollars per GPU rise. Caution: InnoLight's H1-26 blended price was only ~$305 per module A85, which suggests 2026 street prices for 800G sit below this range (see the optics market section).

06 · Optics 101Lasers, transceivers and the move to co-packaged optics

A pluggable transceiver is a module, roughly the size of a pack of gum, that converts electrical signals to light and back. Inside: a DSP chip (Broadcom, Marvell, Credo) that cleans and encodes the signal; a laser (an EML, or a CW laser plus a silicon-photonics modulator; a VCSEL for short reach); a photodiode; a driver and amplifier (TIA); and the housing. The common shapes are QSFP-DD and OSFP.

Speeds step up about every two years

800G is the 2025–26 workhorse. 1.6T is ramping now, at least 5× faster than 800G did according to Dell'Oro E52, and 3.2T parts are sampling (Astera's 3.2T Taurus cable module, July 2026 R32).

The alternatives around the pluggable

  • LPO / LRO (linear-drive and linear-receive optics) remove or halve the DSP, saving power and cost at the price of tighter signal-integrity demands. XPO/NPO (near-packaged optics) move the optical engine onto the board next to the switch. Dell'Oro sees "XPO and NPO gaining earlier momentum and CPO ramping up over time" E52.
  • CPO places the optical engine on the same package as the switch chip and supplies light from an external laser module (ELS/ELSFP). NVIDIA claims 5× better power efficiency and 5× longer sustained runtime than pluggables R59. NVIDIA's Quantum-X Photonics InfiniBand switch is in production, and Spectrum-X Photonics (up to 409.6 Tb/s) arrives in 2H 2026 59. Broadcom's line runs Bailly (TH5 CPO, 2024) → Davisson (TH6 CPO, Oct 2025), and Broadcom calls its CPO "de facto standard in the industry" 20. Lumentum will demo an 8-wavelength DWDM ELSFP for the OCI MSA, with product in 1H 2027 R37.
  • Arista expects "the majority of the world will still remain pluggable optics and copper" through 2027 R13.
  • AECs (active electrical cables) are copper cables with retimer chips in the ends. They reach about 7 m versus about 2–3 m for passive copper. Credo invented the category, and AECs now replace optics for card-to-top-of-rack and some switch links 29.

07 · SystemsFrom chip to rack: who builds what

The factory side of the layer runs in numbered integration levels. NVIDIA sells the rack builders the compute board (GPUs, CPU, HBM and LPDDR on one board) and the NVSwitch trays. The ODM buys everything else (chassis, power, cooling, cables, SSDs, network cards), integrates it and sells the rack to a hyperscaler, or to an OEM such as Dell or HPE that sells to neoclouds and enterprises.

10 · Money in / money outWho pays whom, in dollars

Hyperscalers, neoclouds, sovereigns and model labs (W08, W09) pay this layer in two ways. They buy racks through an ODM or OEM, and they separately buy scale-out switches, network cards, optics and cables. The server makers then pass most of the rack money on to NVIDIA.

Behind the vendors sits a second tier of payments. ODMs and OEMs pay NVIDIA for boards and switch trays (about $286bn a year at the Q2 FY27 compute run-rate, 71.5 × 4 A). They also buy power shelves (Delta, Lite-On), cold plates and CDUs (Vertiv, CoolIT, AVC, Auras), the copper spine (Amphenol), PCBs, SSDs and chassis. NVIDIA in turn pays TSMC and the HBM makers (W06). Switch vendors (Arista, Cisco, Celestica, Juniper) buy Broadcom, Marvell or NVIDIA silicon and optics. Module makers (InnoLight, Eoptolink, Coherent, AAOI) buy DSPs from Broadcom, Marvell and Credo, lasers from Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Mitsubishi and Sumitomo, and silicon photonics from TSMC and Tower.

NVIDIA's total data-center revenue runs at about $356bn a year on Q2 FY27 alone (89.0 × 4, a lower bound given the $108bn Q3 guide) A. The Atlas canonical estimate for CY2026 is ~$365bn A, with an exit run-rate above $400bn.

Key insight

Consistency note

Scaling this per-rack stack to a gigawatt (~5,500 rack-equivalents × ~$6.5m) gives roughly $36bn, below the Atlas canonical all-in cost of ~$50–60bn per GW for Blackwell (W04/W05). Treat the facility line here as a floor.

Switch economics

  • A 51.2T or 102.4T switch chip sells for roughly $10–25k (older knowledge, conf: low). A finished 64 × 800G switch sells for ~$40–80k (white box at the low end, NVIDIA or Arista branded at the high end). That is ~$600–1,200 per 800G port before optics A.
  • White box versus branded: hyperscalers such as Google and Meta save perhaps 20–40% by buying white-box switches and running their own software. Arista's 63% gross margin against Celestica's ~9% segment margin shows where the software value sits.

The profit pool in one rack

Follow the ~$4.24m spent on one GB300 rack-equivalent (rack plus its share of the network) to each participant, and then split what each keeps as gross profit from what it pays its own suppliers.

On an NVIDIA-based cluster, NVIDIA earns roughly 86% of the layer's gross profit A: 2,440 ÷ (2,440 + 50 + 110 + 35 + 40 + 110 + 35 = 2,820). The Atlas canonical figure is ~86% at rack level and ~88–89% at chip level (W06). The fastest-growing pools outside NVIDIA are lasers (Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom) and connectivity silicon (Credo, Astera, Marvell, Broadcom), which earn scarcity or IP margins, while ODMs earn volume.

Key insight

On custom-chip clusters (TPU, Trainium, MTIA, OpenAI/Broadcom) the split tilts toward Broadcom and Marvell (chips plus networking), Arista and Celestica (Ethernet) and the optics chain, and away from NVIDIA. That is the strategic reason hyperscalers push Ethernet, UALink and ESUN: every open standard moves profit out of the 86% slice.

15 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

The network outgrows the chips

  • Networking outgrows compute. NVIDIA networking grew ~2.6× faster than compute in Q1 FY27 E6. Revenue per GW rises from $25bn to $40bn with Rubin 3. Each GPU generation doubles bandwidth per GPU, and scale-across adds a new network layer.
  • Visibility is extraordinary. Dell's $95bn backlog 49, Arista's $6.9bn deferred revenue and $9.7bn purchase commitments 12, Broadcom's $115bn/$230bn FY27–28 AI framework 19, Cisco's $9.3bn of FY26 AI orders 22, Supermicro's >$60bn of orders 46.
  • Optics scarcity sustains pricing. Lasers are sold out until about 2028 1217, and supplier margins are expanding (Lumentum operating margin guide ~40% G35).
  • Scale-up goes optical. When in-rack links go optical (2027–29), optics gets a second market that could be as large as scale-out. Dell'Oro sees scale-up above half of AI back-end switching by 2030 52.
  • More buyers. Neoclouds, sovereigns, enterprises and model labs (Anthropic and OpenAI multi-GW builds 19) widen the base beyond four hyperscalers, which helps Arista, Cisco, Dell, HPE and Supermicro.

Bear case

Fewer modules, fatter NVIDIA, thin builders

  • CPO and flatter networks cut module counts. Switch-side CPO removes about half of pluggables per GPU; higher-radix switches (102.4T → 204.8T) remove tiers. Module makers could see unit growth decouple from GPU growth by 2028.
  • NVIDIA vertical bundling. Spectrum-X + SuperNIC + CPO lets NVIDIA capture switch and optics economics and squeeze merchant vendors and module makers; its multi-year optics agreements 5 could become price leverage.
  • Concentration and in-sourcing. Credo's top 4 are 84% 29; Arista gets 42% of revenue from two customers 71. Hyperscalers design their own NICs (AWS Nitro/EFA 65), switches and even DSPs, and can shift volume overnight.
  • Double-ordering and inventory. In a shortage customers over-order optics and switches. Record purchase commitments (Arista $9.7bn) become write-down risk if capex slows in 2027–28 (see W01, W02).
  • ODM margin trap. Foxconn's 3.75% operating margin 51 and Supermicro's history show volume does not guarantee profit; a rack problem or price war can wipe out a year's earnings.
  • Ethernet commoditisation and policy. As UEC, ESUN and SUE standardise, switch margins could converge toward white-box levels (Arista's gross margin is already down ~220 bp y/y 11). Tariffs on Thai- or China-built optics, export enforcement 47 and "trusted supplier" rules add risk.

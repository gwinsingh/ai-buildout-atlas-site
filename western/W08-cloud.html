The 60-second version

  1. Clouds are landlords for AI compute. They buy the GPUs, lease or build the buildings, and rent out time by the hour, by the multi-year cluster or by the token. Labs and enterprises pay them; they pay NVIDIA, server makers, landlords, utilities and lenders.
  2. The market is growing at its fastest pace in eight years: $143.4bn in Q2-2026, +43% E26. All four hyperscalers accelerated in the same quarter, and all say they are short of capacity through 2027.
  3. The backlog is enormous and concentrated. About $2.46tn of contracted future revenue sits at five companies A, and a handful of labs, above all OpenAI, own a large share of it. At the neoclouds that is starting to spread: CoreWeave's largest customer fell from 71% to 36% of quarterly revenue in a year R79.
  4. A GPU cloud is a financing business dressed as a tech business. Cash margins are 60–75%, but the asset is expensive, depreciates fast and is usually bought with debt. AWS keeps 39% as operating profit; CoreWeave keeps 5% before paying $640m of quarterly interest R29.
  5. GPU rental prices bottomed in mid-2025 and are rising again. The H100 fell from ~$8/hr (2023) to under $2 on spot markets, and is back to $3.39 median; CoreWeave raised prices ~25% in July 2026 and now prices short-dated contracts at ~$40m of annual revenue per MW, about double long-term deals R3071. A GB200 rack needs about $3.10/GPU-hr for a 10% return A.
  6. Watch renewal prices on 2023–24 H100 contracts, Oracle's cash flow and gross margin, CoreWeave's interest bill against its operating profit, and whether public markets fund the next wave of neoclouds (Nscale filed for an IPO on 18 Sep 2026 74).

01 · What this layer isWho owns the AI computers, and why they rent them out

A frontier AI model needs tens of thousands of GPUs wired together in buildings that draw as much power as a small city. Very few companies want to own that directly.

So a set of companies buys the chips, leases or builds the buildings, runs the fleet, and rents out time on it: by the GPU-hour, by the reserved cluster, or by the token. These landlords are "the cloud." They sit in the middle of the money map. Labs and enterprises pay them. They pay NVIDIA, AMD and Broadcom for chips (W05), Dell, Supermicro and Foxconn for servers and Arista, Broadcom and NVIDIA for networking (W07), data-center developers for rent (W04), and utilities for power (W03).

There are two kinds of landlord. The hyperscalers (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud and, for AI, Oracle OCI) built a general cloud first and added AI. The neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe, Together AI, Fluidstack, Nscale, IREN and others) were born for GPUs, often out of crypto mining. Meta and xAI build huge fleets for their own use; they appear on this page only as customers of clouds, and their own capex is in W01.

Analogy

Hotels, apartment leases and room service. An on-demand GPU is a hotel room: a premium nightly rate, leave any time. Reserved or committed capacity is a multi-year apartment lease: cheaper per night, but you pay whether you sleep there or not (take-or-pay). Model-as-a-service (Bedrock, Azure AI Foundry, Vertex AI) is room service: you never see the room and pay per meal, meaning per million tokens.

When OpenAI signs a $300bn, five-year contract with Oracle, it is pre-leasing a whole tower before it is built. The landlord borrows against that lease to pay the builders.

Explain like I'm new: what does "renting a GPU" actually mean?

A GPU is a chip that does AI math. You cannot buy one hour of a chip at a store, so a cloud buys racks of them, puts them in a data center with power and cooling, and gives customers remote access. A lab training a model might reserve 50,000 GPUs for five years. A start-up might rent eight GPUs for an afternoon. A developer might never touch a GPU at all and just send text to a model API and pay per token.

The cloud's money question is simple: will the rent it collects over the chip's working life cover what it paid for the chip, the building, the power and the interest on the loan that bought it all? The rest of this page is about that question.

Why it matters

This layer is where capex turns into revenue. Every dollar a hyperscaler spends on GPUs (W01) has to come back as cloud revenue here, at a margin that covers depreciation and the cost of capital. If it doesn't, the whole stack below it (chips, memory, power) was overbuilt.

02 · How it worksWhat clouds sell, and how the contracts work

Four layers of product

Clouds sell compute at four levels of finish. The rawer the product, the lower the margin. The hyperscalers' structural advantage is that they sell all four; a neocloud mostly sells the first.

Five ways to buy an hour of GPU

  • On-demand. Pay per hour or second, no commitment, list price. For an H100 this is ~$3–11/hr depending on provider (Sep-2026) E54.
  • Reserved, committed-use or savings plans. One- to three-year commitments at a 30–60% discount. For hyperscalers this sits in RPO.
  • Take-or-pay capacity contracts. Multi-year, now often five years or more: AWS says "most AI capacity these days [is] contracted for at least five-year terms" R2. The customer pays a fixed monthly fee for a dedicated cluster whether it uses it or not. This is the neocloud standard and increasingly the hyperscaler one (OpenAI–Oracle, OpenAI–Microsoft, Anthropic–Google).
    Prepayments are common: Nebius takes up to 100% on one-year commits E49, IREN 45–55% of GPU capex R47, and Oracle booked $75bn of prepaid or customer-supplied hardware in Q3–Q4 FY26 R18. The lender (or the cloud) finances the rest against the contract.
  • Spot or preemptible. Interruptible capacity at up to 50–60% off. Together AI's new preemptible tier is 50% of on-demand R44.
  • Marketplaces and exchanges. SF Compute, Vast.ai and index or derivative venues (Ornn, Silicon Data) make prices visible. They are where the "H100 at $2" headlines came from E505758.

Definition

RPO (remaining performance obligations): contracted revenue not yet recognized under GAAP (ASC 606), usually called backlog. Hyperscaler RPO mixes AI capacity with ordinary multi-year software and cloud commitments. Google says "just over 50%" of its $514bn cloud backlog converts within 24 months R12. CoreWeave reports "revenue backlog" of $104.2bn, of which $103.7bn is RPO R31.

The GPU-hour is the unit of account

A GPU-hour is one accelerator used for one hour. A GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 GPUs, so it can sell 72 × 8,760 = 630,720 GPU-hours a year at 100% utilization. Revenue is GPU-hours × price × utilization. Six things move the economics:

  1. Price per GPU-hour, set by the market and by contracts (section 06).
  2. Utilization, the share of hours actually billed. Contracted clusters bill ~100%; on-demand fleets run 60–90%. Oracle reports 97.9% R15.
  3. Capex per GPU: chip + server + network + storage + installation. About $42k for an H100 in 2023–24 and about $54k for a GB200 (all-in, rack-scale) A (conf: med).
  4. Opex: power (about $0.06–0.10/kWh in the US), colocation rent (~$130–220/kW-month for AI-ready wholesale), operations staff, SG&A.
  5. Depreciation life: 4–6 years (section 07).
  6. Cost of capital: roughly 5% for AA hyperscalers, 6–9% for contract-backed neocloud debt, 9–12%+ for unsecured high yield.

Key insight

The GPU cloud is a financing business dressed as a tech business. Cash margins are rich, but the asset is expensive, depreciates fast and is usually bought with debt. Profit depends on (a) locking in contract prices above the asset's break-even for its useful life, (b) the tenant's credit, (c) the funding spread and (d) what the GPU is worth when the contract ends. Hyperscalers add three more levers: custom silicon (AWS claims Trainium gives about 50% lower total cost than NVIDIA R1, a company claim), attach (storage, databases, security sold alongside) and software margin (model APIs, Copilot).

03 · Market size & growthThe fastest growth in eight years, and every hyperscaler accelerating at once

Synergy Research puts worldwide cloud infrastructure services revenue at $143.4bn in Q2-2026, up 43%, the fastest growth in eight years. Trailing-twelve-month revenue is about $500bn, and GenAI-specific services (GPU-as-a-service, GenAI platforms) grew 165% E26. Neoclouds already make up about 5% of the market, and nine of them are in the top 40 providers E27.

The four hyperscalers, quarter by quarter

All four accelerated in the June-2026 quarter: AWS $42.2bn (+37%) with a 39.4% operating margin R12; Azure +43%, crossing $100bn a year R67; Google Cloud $24.8bn (+82%) at a 35.6% margin R1112; and Oracle OCI $7.4bn (+121%) in the August quarter R14.

04 · Backlog & customers$2.46 trillion of contracted revenue, owed by a handful of labs

Remaining performance obligations now stand at $678bn for Microsoft (commercial RPO), $664bn for Oracle, $514bn for Google Cloud, $496bn for AWS and $104bn for CoreWeave (about $129bn by 11 Aug 2026) R6141212932. That adds up to about $2.46 trillion of contracted future revenue at five companies A (678 + 664 + 514 + 496 + 104).

The backlog is concentrated in a handful of labs. About 45% of Microsoft's RPO was OpenAI at December 2025 (conf: med), and excluding OpenAI, Microsoft's RPO grew only 25% R9. About half of Oracle's $664bn is reportedly OpenAI E17. Microsoft was about 67% of CoreWeave's 2025 revenue E33. That share is falling: CoreWeave's Q2-26 10-Q shows its largest (unnamed) customer at 36% of quarterly revenue (71% a year earlier), the second at 26% and a third at 10% R79. The Atlas's cross-layer figure is that OpenAI and Anthropic alone owe about 43% (~$1.05tn) of the five-company backlog A (range 40–50%, conf: low–med); counting Meta, Microsoft-as-reseller and xAI too, more than half traces to five buyers A (conf: med).

Key insight

Revenue recognized today is more diversified than backlog. Backlog is heavily lab-weighted because labs sign the giant multi-year blocks; enterprises mostly buy on-demand, reserved capacity or model APIs, which rarely show up as $10bn+ RPO deals. Concentration risk sits in future revenue, not in the current P&L.

Everyone is short of capacity. AWS says it will not have enough through 2027 and that "the lion's share of '27 capacity is already reserved" R25. Microsoft says demand exceeds supply R7. 06 · Rental pricesGPU rental prices bottomed in 2025 and are rising again

The H100 went from about $8/hr on-demand in 2023 to under $2/hr on marketplace spot by the second half of 2025 E5053. Then it turned. Neocloud H100 rates recovered to about $2.7–2.9/hr by mid-2026 5152, and on 25 Sep 2026 the median on-demand price across ~40 providers was $3.39 (+12% y/y) E54.

How to read it: there are three price tiers. Hyperscaler list prices are administered and sticky; few buyers pay them, because hyperscaler customers mostly pay reserved or committed rates. Neocloud on-demand is market-clearing, and this is the tier that moved. Marketplace and spot prices are the distressed end: short-dated resale of idle capacity. The hyperscaler premium over other providers compressed from about 3–4.5× (Q1-2025) to about 2–2.5× (Q3-2026) E52.

In 2026 prices rose. The neocloud index was up ~11% from April to May 51; the median H100 is +12% and the H200 +25% year on year 5455. Named increases: CoreWeave ~+25% across SKUs (Jul-2026) R30; Nebius two pay-as-you-go hikes (Jul and Oct 2026) E38; Oracle renewals +20% R16; IREN from $20m to ~$25m per MW(IT) on new three-year deals R47. The scarcity premium shows most in short contracts: in September CoreWeave said new 3–6 month deals were priced at about $40m of annualized revenue per MW, roughly double IREN's multi-year rate R71. Why: Blackwell ramp delays and tight supply; HBM and DRAM inflation raising the replacement cost of any new GPU 1; inference demand (reasoning and agentic workloads) soaking up older GPUs; and lab mega-contracts pre-empting supply.

Rack-scale GB200 and GB300 NVL72 systems are rarely sold on-demand. Contract pricing is estimated at ~$3.5–5.0 per GPU-hour A (conf: low–med). Our triangulation: IREN's more than $20m of revenue per MW(IT) per year on three-year contracts, at ~497 GB300 GPUs per MW(IT), implies ≈$4.60/GPU-hr at 100% billing (20,000,000 ÷ (497 × 8,760)); at ~$25m per MW it is ≈$5.75. Spot venues trade well below the contract market: Ornn's H100 index shows ~$1.80–1.90 57, and SF Compute shows $3.00 reserved and $4.29 realized resale 58.

What the model says

  • H100s bought in 2023 on $4+ three-to-five-year contracts were big winners: 37% IRR, paid back in about two years.
  • H100s bought in 2024 and rented on-demand at 2025's ~$2–3 earn around or below the cost of capital; at $2 they lose money on a five-year view.
  • Nearly all of the 2025 "H100 bubble burst" pain landed on spot-exposed small neoclouds, not on contract-backed ones.
  • The neocloud bet: leverage makes the model spectacular if contract prices hold above ~$3.10/GPU-hr for the asset's life, and ruinous below ~$2.75. That is why neoclouds insist on five-year take-or-pay with investment-grade counterparties before ordering a rack.

Cross-checks. CoreWeave's actual Q2-26 margins (66% gross, 59% adjusted EBITDA R29) sit between our $3.50 and $4.60 cases. AWS says servers break even in "less than three years" 2, consistent with ~$3.50–4.60-equivalent pricing. Oracle's 35% life-of-contract gross margin after depreciation 20 fits ~$3.50–4.00/GPU-hr on a six-year life, or a higher price with heavy data-center lease costs.

The depreciation-life debate

Depreciation life is the hidden lever. Hyperscalers depreciate servers over five to six years; Nebius uses four. On one GB200 rack at $3.50/hr, moving from six-year to four-year depreciation cuts EBIT margin from 33% to 18% A.

The bear case on depreciation

  • Hyperscalers extended useful lives just as they started buying GPUs on ~one-year product cadences, which inflates earnings (Michael Burry, Nov-2025, and followers).
  • Burry's estimate: ~$176bn of understated depreciation across 2026–28, with Oracle's 2028 earnings overstated by ~27% and Meta's by ~21% (from his X posts as widely reported; conf: med).
  • His Sep-2026 piece "The Big 5 Hyperscalers & the Missing $3 Trillion" extends the argument to off-balance-sheet commitments (paywalled; not verified) E59.

The bull case on depreciation

  • Old GPUs keep earning. CoreWeave renews 2020-vintage A100s at or above old prices into 2029 30, and Oracle renews at +20% 16.
  • Inference extends useful life: a GPU retired from frontier training gets a second life serving tokens.
  • Economic life is not technological leadership. If a six-year-old chip still rents above its cash cost, six-year depreciation is defensible.

How big is the gap for a hyperscaler? A Microsoft spent ~$27bn a quarter on short-lived assets in FY26 Q4 (two-thirds of $41bn 7), about $110bn a year. One year's vintage depreciated over six years costs $18.3bn a year; over four years, $27.5bn: $9.2bn a year less expense per annual vintage. With three or four vintages stacked (2024–27), the gap reaches ~$25–35bn a year of pre-tax income for Microsoft alone, roughly 15–20% of its FY26 operating income of $155bn 6. Across Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle, with ~$450bn a year of server capex (conf: low), the four-versus-six-year gap is ~$37bn per vintage-year and more than $100bn a year once stacked: the same order of magnitude as Burry's figure. Cash flow is unaffected; ROIC and P/E comparisons are not.

The cost of money decides who wins

The same GB200 rack at $3.50/GPU-hr yields ~37% levered equity IRR with 6% debt but ~27% with 12% debt. More importantly, its downside tolerance shrinks, because the debt amortizes over the contract. Neocloud funding has matured from GPU-backed high-yield debt toward investment-grade structures: IREN raised $3.6bn at 6.0% against its Microsoft contract 47; Lambda raised a $926m Baa2 term loan B at SOFR+300 41; CoreWeave says its weighted cost of debt fell about 300 bp in a year 30. Tail risk is still priced in: CoreWeave's five-year CDS was about 855 bp in late July 2026 32, and Oracle's CDS hit record highs in September 25.

09 · Profit captureHyperscalers keep the durable profit; neoclouds pass the rent through

Hyperscaler and neocloud margins differ by an order of magnitude at the operating line. AWS operates at 39%, Google Cloud at 36% and Oracle (company-level, non-GAAP) at 42%. CoreWeave's adjusted operating margin is 5% and its GAAP margin −2%; it lost $626m in Q2-26 with interest of $640m a quarter R29. The gap reflects the cost of capital, not gross profit: CoreWeave's gross margin is 66%.

Take $100 of AI infrastructure revenue at a neocloud running at CoreWeave's Q2-26 cost structure A:

The depreciation line is NVIDIA's (and the server makers') gross profit, paid up front. Economically, the neocloud passes most of the rent through to its chip vendor and its lenders. It earns its equity return only in the back half of each contract, once the assets are paid down and the fleet is still billing.

At a hyperscaler, $100 of AI infrastructure revenue carries similar depreciation per GPU. But the hyperscaler (a) pays roughly no interest premium; (b) buys its own silicon for ~30–50% of GPU-equivalent capacity (TPU, Trainium), avoiding NVIDIA's ~75% gross margin; (c) sells model APIs and software on top; and (d) blends AI with legacy cloud, which runs at 40%+ operating margins on long-depreciated assets. That is why AWS and Google Cloud operating margins rose while AI grew, whereas Oracle's (single-tenant, NVIDIA-only, debt-funded) gross margin fell.

Profit-capture verdict

Hyperscalers capture the most durable cloud profit: 35–40% segment operating margins, cheap capital (A/AA credit), custom silicon that lowers their cost of goods, and higher-margin platform and model services on top (Bedrock, Foundry, Vertex). Neoclouds are leveraged, contract-backed GPU lessors with 55–60% EBITDA margins but single-digit operating margins until their assets are paid down. The hyperscalers' bet is that custom silicon and software shift profit up from the silicon layer over 2027–30 A.

10 · BottlenecksWhat limits how fast clouds can grow

  1. Power and powered shells (W03, W04). Capacity is gated by energized megawatts, not chips. AWS is doubling power capacity from 2025 to 2027 5; Microsoft added ~1 GW in one quarter 9; CoreWeave brought 300+ MW online in June 2026 alone 30; Oracle added 850 MW in Q1 FY27 14. Site problems now show up in cloud stocks: Oracle's force-majeure notice on Project Jupiter 25.
  2. GPU and memory supply (W05, W06). HBM and DRAM inflation added ~$20bn to Amazon's 2026 capex 1. SemiAnalysis: "GPU supply has gone to zero" 49. The Blackwell Ultra (GB300) and Vera Rubin ramps set when the next price tier arrives.
  3. Capital (W02). Hyperscalers are free-cash-flow negative or close to it (Alphabet Q2-26 FCF turned negative 11; Oracle FY26 FCF −$23.7bn 18); Amazon doubled its debt in six months 1. Neoclouds depend on delayed-draw term loans, private credit (Blue Owl, PIMCO, Magnetar, Blackstone), converts (Nebius $5.75bn 65) and vendor support (NVIDIA equity and backstops).
  4. Counterparty credit. Most neocloud and Oracle backlog is only as good as OpenAI's, Anthropic's and Meta's ability to pay. OpenAI's ~$1.4tn commitment stack (conf: med) is far beyond its current revenue (W09).
  5. Networking and liquid cooling (W07, W04). GB200 and GB300 racks need direct-to-chip liquid cooling at 130–150 kW per rack. Many older colocation sites cannot host them, which creates a two-tier colo market.
  6. Software and operations talent. SemiAnalysis's ClusterMAX 3.0 found most neoclouds weak on security and reliability; only 19 of 323 providers earned medals 49. Reliability at 100,000-GPU scale is a real moat (CoreWeave and Nebius are Platinum).

11 · Competitive dynamicsFrenemies, labs becoming clouds, and NVIDIA as kingmaker

  • Hyperscalers and neoclouds are frenemies. Microsoft is both CoreWeave's biggest customer (~67% of 2025 revenue; the top customer was 36% in Q2-26 per the 10-Q 79) and its biggest competitor. It uses neoclouds as off-balance-sheet overflow capacity: a lease is an operating expense, and the neocloud carries the asset and the obsolescence risk. As Microsoft's own capacity catches up, neocloud renewal risk rises; diversifying to Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, Jane Street and Google (via Fluidstack backstops) is the neocloud response.
  • Labs are becoming clouds. OpenAI's Stargate (with SoftBank, Oracle, MGX), Anthropic's $50bn own-DC plan with Fluidstack, and xAI's Colossus make the largest customers partial owners of their compute. Synergy already counts OpenAI and Anthropic among tier-two cloud providers 26.
  • Custom silicon versus NVIDIA. Google TPU (now also sold to Anthropic and Meta), AWS Trainium (Anthropic, OpenAI from 2027; "$25bn+ chips run-rate" 2), Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA. Every GPU-hour moved to custom silicon moves margin from NVIDIA to the hyperscaler, and removes demand from the neocloud model, which is NVIDIA-only.
  • NVIDIA as kingmaker. NVIDIA allocates scarce GPUs and invests in neoclouds: CoreWeave $2bn (Jan-2026), Nebius $2bn (Mar-2026), plus Crusoe, Together and Lambda. It also backstops demand (CoreWeave $6.3bn) 3334404244. Critics call this circular financing (W02); supporters say NVIDIA is seeding a competitive customer base against the hyperscalers' custom chips.
  • Price discovery and financialization. GPU compute indices (Silicon Data SDH100RT/SDB200RT, Ornn OCPI) and exchanges (SF Compute) are creating forward curves and eventually derivatives 505758, letting lenders mark collateral and hedge residual-value risk.
  • Inference APIs commoditize raw GPUs. Together AI, Crusoe Managed Inference, Nebius Token Factory, Bedrock, Foundry and Vertex sell tokens. Value may migrate from GPU-hours to tokens-per-dollar efficiency (W09, W10).
  • Consolidation. CoreWeave–Core Scientific failed (Oct-2025) 34; IREN bought Mirantis (Aug-2026) 48; Nebius bought Tavily 40. Expect ex-miners and sub-scale GPU clouds to be absorbed as their contracts mature.

13 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

The backlog is real, and prices are going up

  • $2.46tn of contracted backlog across five companies, growing 80%+. The biggest buyers keep raising commitments; AWS says 2027 capacity is largely reserved and 2028 demand is "striking" 2.
  • Pricing turned up in 2026: CoreWeave +25%, Nebius two hikes, Oracle renewals +20%, IREN $20m → $25m per MW. Old A100s still rent at old prices. The depreciation bear case assumed prices would collapse; so far they haven't.
  • Hyperscaler margins are expanding during the build-out: AWS 32.9% → 39.4% and Google Cloud 20.7% → 35.6% in a year. That is the opposite of what an overbuild looks like.
  • Inference is the long tail. Tokens processed compound (Google 16bn → 22bn tokens a minute in one quarter 13), and agentic and reasoning workloads use much more compute per task. Each GPU generation gets a second life in inference.
  • Financing is getting cheaper for contract-backed assets: IG term loans at SOFR+300 and 6% IG debt against Microsoft contracts 4147.

Bear case

A leveraged, concentrated, circular bet on a few labs

  • Concentration. OpenAI is ~45% of Microsoft's RPO, ~50% of Oracle's and a large slice of CoreWeave's. If OpenAI's growth disappoints, it cannot honor ~$1.4tn of commitments; cross-default chains run from labs to clouds to lenders.
  • Oracle is the canary: negative FCF, ~$130bn of debt, an equity raise, record CDS, a 57% share-price drawdown, a force majeure at a flagship site, and reported 14% gross margins on GPU rental 2225.
  • Depreciation flatters earnings. Six-year lives on assets with one-year product cycles; on four-year lives, hyperscaler EPS would be ~10–20% lower A and neocloud EBIT negative.
  • Neocloud equity is a levered option on GPU prices. A GB200 at $2.75 destroys levered equity (−9% IRR at 9% debt). If Vera Rubin makes Blackwell obsolete faster than contracts run, renewal prices crater; the H100 went from $4+ contracts to $2 spot in ~24 months 5053.
  • Circularity. NVIDIA invests in neoclouds that buy NVIDIA GPUs and backstops their capacity; labs raise equity from the clouds and chip vendors they buy from. Revenue growth may overstate end-demand.
  • Capacity arriving all at once. CoreWeave 1.5 → 8 GW, Crusoe 1 → 6 GW contracted, Oracle adding ~1 GW a quarter, AWS doubling. If demand growth slows in 2028, on-demand prices could reset as sharply as in 2024–25.

Our view

Through 2027, shortage economics dominate: contract-backed capacity is profitable and hyperscalers are the clear margin winners. The real test is 2028–2030, when the 2025–26 vintages roll off their first contracts and the OpenAI and Oracle ramps must be paid for from lab revenue rather than new equity. The cleanest early signal: renewal pricing on 2023–24 H100 contracts A.

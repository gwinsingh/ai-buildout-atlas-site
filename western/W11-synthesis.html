The 60-second version

  1. The West will spend about $1.0T on AI data centers in 2026 A, and roughly two-thirds of it is short-lived chips and servers that wear out in 5–6 years.
  2. Most of that money is not AI revenue yet. Of ~$1.12T flowing through the build-out, ~49% is Big Tech's old profit engines (search, social ads, retail, Office), ~18% is new debt and ~18% is end-customer AI money A.
  3. The suppliers mint the money. Nvidia takes ~38% of a ~$637B AI operating-profit pool and the memory makers ~23%. Clouds keep ~5%, and the model labs lose ~$48B A. Every $1 of capex turns into ~51¢ of supplier profit.
  4. Profit follows scarcity, and scarcity keeps moving: GPUs and packaging in 2023–24, power in 2025, memory in 2026, then power hookups, permits and, eventually, customers' willingness to pay.
  5. Customer money is growing ~3× a year but still trails the capex: ~$280B run-rate at the end of 2026 against ~$600B a year needed to pay for what is already built or committed. In the base case the gap closes around 2028–29.
  6. The thesis that "agents are where fresh money enters" is right about the growth engine and wrong about the fuel tank. Agents drive most of the growth, but legacy cash flow, debt and equity pay most of the bill today.

01 · Explain like I'm newThe build-out in one picture: funded upside-down

In a normal industry, customers pay for products, and that revenue pays for the factories. The AI build-out runs the other way round in 2026: the factories are being paid for first, mostly with money earned somewhere else, on the bet that customers will arrive.

This page is the synthesis of the ten layer pages (W01–W10). It adds no new primary research. It joins the layers, reconciles where they disagree, and answers six questions: what the layers are, where the investment goes, where the money comes in, how it flows, which layer earns the most, and whether agents are where the fresh money enters.

Analogy

Picture a town building a giant new railway before the timetable is set. Ticket sales are real and growing fast, but in year one they cover less than a fifth of the construction bill. The rest comes from the town's richest merchants, who pay out of their existing shops, plus bank loans and outside investors. Meanwhile the steel mill and the locomotive maker are paid in full, up front, in cash. So today the people getting rich are the suppliers, not the railway. Whether the railway itself ever earns its keep depends on how fast passengers show up.

Why is it risky to build ahead of customers?

Most of the spending buys chips that depreciate over 5–6 years. If customer revenue arrives on time, the chips pay for themselves and the builders look brilliant. If it arrives two or three years late, the builders carry the depreciation without the revenue, and the weakest ones (those funded by debt rather than by an old profit engine) can fail.

The good news: about half the money comes from four very profitable companies spending their own cash, which is far sturdier than the junk bonds and IPOs that funded the 1999–2001 telecom bubble 2. The bad news: the decision to keep spending sits with four CEOs and their shareholders, not with AI customers.

Definition

End-customer AI money: what consumers, enterprises and advertisers actually pay for AI products, AI cloud services and AI-driven ads, net of intermediate purchases (an app buying tokens from a lab is not counted twice). In 2026 it is ~$200B: ~$130B of direct spend on AI apps and models, ~$20B of enterprise AI cloud infrastructure and ~$50B of AI-driven ad uplift at Meta and Google A.

03 · MethodHow to read the numbers on this page

Almost every cross-layer number here is an Atlas estimate A, because no company reports "AI profit". Treat the layer totals as order-of-magnitude correct and directionally reliable, not precise (confidence medium for W05/W06, low elsewhere). Headline numbers follow data/western/_canonical.json; where dossiers disagree, §16 gives the value used and why.

  1. Calendar 2026 estimates (CY2026E). Fiscal years are shifted to calendar years: Nvidia's FY27 (Feb-2026 to Jan-2027) is treated as CY2026 less one month; Oracle's FY27 guidance is pro-rated. A one-month shift moves Nvidia's number by ~$10B, so CY figures carry ±5% error even where the underlying data is reported.
  2. Cash versus profit. The money map is a flow-of-funds: who pays whom in 2026, including capex. The profit pools are a P&L view: operating profit earned in each year. A cloud's AI profit is reinvested, so it does not appear as a separate cash flow; a lab's loss appears only as the equity that covers it.
  3. "AI-attributable". For multi-business companies only the AI slice counts: TSMC's AI wafers (~35% of revenue), memory makers' HBM plus AI-server DRAM/SSD (~27% of 2026 memory revenue), utilities' sales to AI data centers plus the data-center-driven capacity-price windfall, and Meta and Google's AI-attributed ad uplift.
  4. No double counting. Revenue double-counts along a value chain (TSMC's revenue sits inside Nvidia's costs, Nvidia's inside the clouds' capex). Operating profit does not, so profit is summed across layers and revenue is shown only for context.
  5. Western means non-China. Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Gulf are in; Chinese buyers and suppliers belong to Track 2.
  6. Outside cross-checks. The dossiers test their totals against McKinsey's "$7 trillion race" estimate of compute cost 13, Morgan Stanley's $2.9T financing framework 14, Goldman Sachs' 2026 capex outlook 15, the St. Louis Fed's finding that AI drove 39% of US real GDP growth in the first nine months of 2025 E 16, Dell'Oro's data-center capex tracker (+92% in 2Q26) 17, Synergy's cloud market data ($143.4B in Q2 2026, +43%) 18, Menlo Ventures' enterprise survey 19 and Epoch AI's chip-sales database 20.
  7. Scope of "AI capex" (~$1.0T). Big-5 AI and data-center capex (~$785B, excluding ~$30B of Amazon logistics), neocloud capex (~$65B), developer-built shells leased to tenants (~$70B), lab-owned builds (~$40B, mostly xAI) and sovereign/enterprise builds (~$35B).

Three things stand out

  • Legacy cash flow is the backbone. About half of every dollar flowing into the build-out in 2026 was earned by businesses that existed before ChatGPT. That makes the build-out far sturdier than the 1999–2001 telecom bubble, which ran on high-yield debt and IPOs 2. It also means the decision to keep spending sits with four CEOs and their shareholders, not with AI customers.
  • End-customer AI money (~$202B) is now about as large as new debt (~$197B). In 2024 it was a rounding error (~$24B against ~$290B of capex). At current growth rates it passes debt as the second-largest source in 2027.
  • Vendor financing is small in deployed dollars and large in commitments. Only ~$31B was spent in 2026, but vendors committed ~$110B of equity to labs, a $105B residual-value guarantee cap (Nvidia → OpenAI's Ohio leases) 5, a $6.3B capacity backstop (Nvidia → CoreWeave) and customer-funded capex at Oracle (~$75B of prepayments and bring-your-own-hardware) 1.

IT is 68%, in line with Microsoft's "~2/3 short-lived" 1.

Why memory is 15% here, not 6% or 47%

W01 put memory bought separately at ~$55B (6%). W06 cites TrendForce saying memory will take 47% of cloud providers' capex in 2026. The truth is in between. Buy-side disclosures (Microsoft ~$25B and Amazon ~$20B of 2026 capex is memory price inflation alone) imply memory at ~15–18% of hyperscaler capex before counting the HBM already inside Nvidia's price. The Atlas uses 15% direct plus ~6% HBM, for ~$207B of AI-attributable memory revenue A (confidence low). TrendForce's 47% probably uses a narrower denominator. Either way, memory is now the second-largest line item in an AI data center after Nvidia, and it is a price effect, not a volume effect: DRAM bit shipments grow only in the low-to-mid 20s percent 6.

Where the money ends up

Five things the map shows

  1. The build-out is funded upside-down. ~82% of the money entering the stack in 2026 is not end-customer AI spend. It is legacy profits, debt and equity betting that customers will arrive.
  2. Half of all the money becomes somebody's operating profit ($536B, 48%), and ~70% of that sits in three places: Nvidia, the memory makers and TSMC. That is the definition of a supplier's market.
  3. The labs are a pass-through with a loss attached. They take in ~$111B of revenue, spend ~$130B on compute and ~$45B on people, and cover the difference with ~$64B of equity and debt. Every dollar of lab revenue triggers ~$1.2 of compute spending.
  4. Advertising is the quiet giant. The ~$50B AI ad uplift at Meta and Google is larger than all AI-native apps and incumbent AI SKUs combined (~$33B), and it reaches capex with no intermediary.
  5. Debt now funds as much as end customers do, and it goes where the equity is thinnest: SPVs, neoclouds and Oracle.

Why the profit sits where it sits

Four forces decide who keeps the money, and the binding constraint matters most.

  1. Scarcity at the chokepoint. Profits go to whoever owns an input that cannot be expanded in under 2–3 years. In 2026: EUV lithography (ASML, ~65 tools a year), leading-edge wafers and CoWoS (TSMC), HBM and DRAM (three suppliers), InP lasers (Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom), gas turbines (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi, sold out to ~2030) and energized land with an interconnect 6 7 3 4.
  2. Pricing power from system integration and software. Buyers pay Nvidia for time-to-revenue, CUDA fungibility (a CUDA cluster can be re-rented to anyone, which lowers its financing cost) and rack-scale engineering. That is why Nvidia's gross margin held at ~75% even as CoWoS supply tripled 5.
  3. Cost of capital. The same GPU earns ~35–39% operating margins at AWS and GCP and ~0% at CoreWeave. The difference is not gross margin (CoreWeave's is 66%) but depreciation and a ~9% cost of debt 8. Balance-sheet strength is itself a profit pool.
  4. Position relative to the customer. Labs own the customer but not the scarce input, so they compete on price. Constant-capability token prices fall ~10× a year or more, and labs' gross margins (33–58%) sit below software norms 9. Apps sit one step further out with even thinner margins.

The pattern

Scarcity moves down the physical stack (chip → package → memory → power → land and permits), and each new chokepoint takes margin from the one before it. Nvidia itself flagged the latest transfer: memory costs will push its gross margin down ~300bp to a 71–72% trough 5. The end state is that scarcity reaches willingness to pay. At that point the profit pool shifts toward whoever owns customers and cash flow (hyperscalers, the profitable labs) and away from suppliers. That is the 2028 test.

The memory anomaly: AI-direct versus AI-induced profit

The memory makers' 2Q26 operating profit was ~$134B in a single quarter A (from reported figures: Samsung DS KRW 89.2tn + SK hynix KRW 60.5tn, now from SK hynix's own 2Q26 release 38, + Micron $33.7B). That is roughly double Nvidia's $63.7B R for the same quarter 6. For the full year the Atlas estimates Big-3 memory operating profit at ~$540B (confidence low). Only ~$149B of that is paid directly by AI buyers. The rest is an AI-induced shortage rent paid by every buyer of phones, PCs, cars and general servers, because HBM absorbs ~18–22% of DRAM wafer starts to produce ~8–9% of bits.

The least durable profit pool in the stack

If you count causally, memory is the single biggest profit-taker from the AI build-out in 2026; if you count who writes the checks, Nvidia is. The pool tables use the second definition. Memory margins have mean-reverted within 4–8 quarters of every previous peak (1995, 2000, 2010, 2018, 2022). New capacity lands in 2027–28 (Micron ID1, SK hynix Yongin/M15X, Samsung P4/P5). The base case has memory operating margins falling from ~75% to ~40% in 2028 A. See W06 for HBM.

09 · CircularityCircularity: the vendor-financing loops that tie the stack together

A supplier's cash, credit or guarantee goes to a customer, and the customer commits to spend it back with that supplier or a close partner. That is circular (vendor) financing. It is not fraud; telecom vendors did the same in 1999–2001. But it inflates both the supplier's revenue and the customer's apparent funding, and it ties the supplier's balance sheet to the customer's survival.

The Atlas maps 22 loops (two added in the Sep-2026 re-check). About $0.85T of the $2.46T cloud backlog (~34%) sits in contracts where the seller also invested in the buyer A, and ~40–60% of 2026 lab primary equity came from the labs' own suppliers and strategic partners A.

What share of 2026 lab funding is circular?

  • Vendor equity exceeds one year of the labs' compute bill. ~$113B of vendor money compares with ~$90B of 2026 compute rentals. In effect, the suppliers prepaid the labs' 2026 compute bill and then some A. W02 reached the same conclusion from H1 data (~$100B vs $60–90B) 2.
  • The share holds after the Sep-2026 re-check. The Series H vendor amounts are now confirmed and the totals are unchanged. Akamai's warrant flows to the lab, and the Apollo/Blackstone SPV is asset finance, so neither counts as lab equity. W09 independently gets $90–125B, or ~40–60%.
  • Much of it is conditional or deferred. Amazon's $35B is milestone-based and SoftBank's tranches are bank-funded. The circular money is therefore also the most fragile money: it depends on the lab hitting the milestones that justify the supplier's next check.

10 · The revenue gapThe revenue-versus-capex gap: the "$600B question" for 2026

End-customer AI money is growing ~2.9× a year and capex ~1.9× a year, so the ratio between them roughly halves every 18 months. But the money needed to pay for what is already built is still about twice what customers pay today.

The finding. On Sequoia's own yardstick the hole is 2.6× larger than in 2024, but coverage is unchanged at about one-sixth: end-customer revenue has grown as fast as Nvidia's revenue. The method is also harsh. It asks one year of revenue to repay one year of GPU purchases, although GPUs are depreciated over 5–6 years and shells over 15–25.

A depreciation-based requirement (the Atlas's preferred test)

What end-customer revenue would make the capex already spent or committed through 2026 earn its cost of capital at steady state? A

Against an exit-2026 run-rate of ~$280B, the gap is ~$320B a year (~2.1×) A. Including base-case 2027 capex, the requirement rises to ~$1.0T a year by 2029. Bain frames the same problem as $2T of AI revenue by 2030 against $500B/yr of capex 12.

The depreciation wall

  • Scale of the hit. The extra $110–160B of annual expense by 2027 compares with ~$480B of Big-4 trailing operating income. Q2 2026 D&A grew +45–46% y/y at Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta and +31% at Amazon R. Meta's operating income is already falling despite +28% revenue growth 1.
  • Useful-life sensitivity. Moving server lives from 6 to 4 years would add >$100B a year of Big-5 depreciation once three or four vintages are stacked, the same order as Michael Burry's ~$176B of "understated" 2026–28 depreciation. Microsoft has lengthened building lives to 25 years (from FY27), which lowers reported capex by ~$15B with "no change" in investment 8.
  • Counter-evidence that lives are fair. Oracle renews 4-year-old GPUs at a ~20% premium with 97.9% utilization; CoreWeave renews 2020-vintage A100s at or above old prices into 2029; H100 rents are +12% y/y. So far the market says GPUs last longer than the bears assume. The test comes in 2027–28, when 2023–24 H100 contracts roll off and Rubin is 10–35× cheaper per token.
  • Across the whole Western build, steady-state depreciation on the 2024–26 vintage is ~$255B a year, rising to ~$440B a year by 2028 if base-case 2027 capex happens A.

15 · The thesis, testedThe verdict on "agents are where fresh money enters"

Verdict

Right about the engine, wrong about the fuel tank. Agents, above all coding agents, are the growth engine of end-customer AI spending, and the growth is real, cash-paid and unprecedented. But in 2026 the build-out is fuelled mainly by money that has nothing to do with agents: Google's search ads, Meta's feed ads, Amazon's retail cash, Microsoft's Office franchise, bond markets and investors' equity. The thesis becomes fully true only if agents break out of coding into labor budgets (support, legal, finance, health, operations) by 2027–28. That is the single variable that decides between the base and bear cases.

"The application layer depends on agents"

Largely true for growth partly for the level

Agentic usage is ~45% of 2026E direct spend (~$60B of ~$130B), ~55–60% of the growth since 2025 and ~55% of the exit-2026 run-rate (conf. low). Anthropic's ramp was driven by agentic coding; its API is 70–75% of revenue. But the largest AI monetization, the ~$50B ad uplift, does not depend on agents at all.

A W10 §3, §7

"That's where fresh money enters the industry"

Partly true

End-customer money enters through five doors: labs (~$97B), ads (~$52B), enterprise AI cloud (~$22B), incumbent AI SKUs (~$19B) and AI-native apps (~$14B). Most lands at labs and incumbents, not an independent app layer. And end-customer money is only ~18% of the ~$1.12T flowing through the build-out; ~10–15% of lab API revenue is recycled VC.

A W10 §7.4; W11 §3

"It's growing very fast"

True

~$11B (2024) → ~$37B (2025) → ~$130B (2026E) of direct spend: ~3× a year, the fastest software ramp on record. Anthropic went from $1B to a >$65B run-rate in ~19 months (firm, end-July). The NYT reports a >$100B pace in September, which the company has not confirmed.

A W10 §3.4; W09 §0

Implied: "agent money will pay for the build-out"

Not yet plausible around 2028 in the base case

The ~$280B exit-2026 run-rate is ~47% of the ~$600B/yr depreciation-based requirement for capex already committed, and ~28% of the ~$1.0T needed once 2027 is included. It requires ~2× in 2027 and ~1.5× in 2028.

A W11 §7

Four refinements to the framing

  1. The app and model layers are merging. Labs sell agents directly (Claude Code, Codex, Cowork), incumbents bundle them (Copilot, Agentforce), and compute owners buy the apps (SpaceX–Cursor). "Agents" is a product category, not a layer.
  2. Advertising is the biggest profitable AI business. Meta's AI-driven Advantage+ runs at a $75B annual rate R, and the ad uplift reaches capex with no intermediaries 10.
  3. Fresh money comes in two kinds. Operating-budget money (enterprise opex, consumer wallets, advertisers) is durable. Capital-budget money (VC-funded startups paying labs, vendor equity paying clouds) is recycled and can vanish.
  4. The money that arrives at the top mostly does not reach the bottom as profit. In steady state only ~6¢ of an agent dollar becomes Nvidia profit. The chip layer's 2026 profits are paid by capex ahead of demand, which is exactly why the gap matters.

19 · GlossaryTerms on this page

Flow-of-funds
A cash map of who pays whom in a period, including capital spending and financing, not just profits.
Profit pool
The operating profit earned at each layer. It adds up across layers without double counting; revenue does not.
AI-attributable
The share of a multi-business company's revenue or profit caused by AI demand (e.g. TSMC's AI wafers, memory makers' HBM and AI-server DRAM).
Legacy cash flow
Operating cash from businesses that predate generative AI: search, social ads, retail, Office, non-AI cloud.
End-customer AI money
Consumer, enterprise and advertiser spending on AI products, AI infrastructure services and AI-driven ads, net of intermediate purchases (e.g. an app buying API tokens).
Circular financing
Also vendor financing: a supplier's equity, credit or guarantee to a customer who commits to buy from that supplier.
Strict vs broad circularity
Strict counts compute and chip suppliers only; broad adds strategic partners (SoftBank/Stargate) and component makers (memory).
Depreciation wall
The rising annual depreciation charge that follows a capex surge, as short-lived IT assets enter service.
Sequoia method
Nvidia data-center run-rate × 2 × 2 = the annual end-user revenue required (Sequoia's 2024 '$600B question').
Depreciation-based requirement
The revenue needed for capex to earn its cost of capital: depreciation + opex + return, grossed up for downstream margins.
Scarcity rent
Profit earned because an input cannot be expanded quickly (EUV, CoWoS, HBM, turbines, powered land), not because of lasting competitive advantage.
AI-induced shortage rent
Profit earned from non-AI buyers because AI demand created the shortage (e.g. PC and phone DRAM in 2026).
Take-or-pay
A contract where the customer pays for committed capacity whether or not it uses it.
Run-rate
The latest month (or quarter) annualized. Not contracted revenue.
Backlog (RPO)
Remaining performance obligations: contracted revenue not yet recognized. Five companies hold ~$2.46T of AI-heavy cloud backlog.
CDS
Credit default swap: the annual price of insuring a company's debt against default, in basis points. Rising CDS means lenders see more risk.
CoWoS
Chip-on-wafer-on-substrate: TSMC's advanced packaging that joins the processor and HBM on one base.
CUDA
Nvidia's software platform for programming its GPUs; the main reason Nvidia clusters are easy to re-rent and hard to leave.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: DRAM chips stacked vertically beside the processor. It needs ~3× the wafer area per bit of ordinary DRAM.
Hyperscaler
The giant cloud and platform companies: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and (for capex) Oracle.
Neocloud
A GPU-focused cloud built for AI (CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, Nscale, Lambda), usually debt-funded.
OCF and FCF
Operating cash flow is cash generated by the business; free cash flow is OCF minus capex.
SPV
Special-purpose vehicle: a separate company set up to own and finance one project (e.g. Meta Hyperion) off the sponsor's balance sheet.
IG bond
Investment-grade bond: debt from issuers rated BBB- or better. Oracle sits on the last rung.
DDTL
Delayed-draw term loan: a loan drawn as GPUs are delivered, typically secured on the GPUs and contracts.
BYOH
Bring your own hardware: the customer buys the chips and the cloud hosts them, which lowers the cloud's capex.
ODM
Original design manufacturer: contract builders of servers and racks (Foxconn, Quanta, Wiwynn).
IPP
Independent power producer: a generator that sells power in markets rather than as a regulated utility.
PJM
The grid operator for 13 US states incl. Virginia's data-center cluster; its capacity auction price signals power scarcity.
Jevons paradox
When a resource gets cheaper, total use can rise so much that spending grows. At firm level it fails if price cuts outrun volume.

The 60-second version

  1. AI's electricity use is real and accelerating. Data centers worldwide used ~415 TWh in 2024 and ~485 TWh in 2025 E, on track for ~945 TWh by 2030 in the IEA base case 36. In the US the share goes from 4.4% (2023) to somewhere between 9% and 17% by 2030, depending on whose forecast you believe 19.
  2. Power, not chips, is now the bottleneck. A GPU hall can be built in 18–24 months; a gas plant takes 4–7 years, a transmission line 7–10+. Gas turbines are sold out to 2029–30 383940, transformers are in deficit 85, and the US adds only 6.3 GW of gas in 2026 21.
  3. Scarcity shows up as price, and ordinary customers pay part of it. PJM's capacity price rose from $28.92 to ~$330/MW-day and still left the grid ~6.6–6.8 GW short 2324. Retail prices rose 9% y/y in Feb 2026, 26% in Virginia 22. Politics has flipped: pledges, a 417–3 House vote, and FERC rule rewrites 333437.
  4. Buyers pay almost any price for speed. Electricity is only ~8–12% of the annual cost of running AI compute A, while one idle GW of GPUs burns ~$500m a month in depreciation A. So hyperscalers sign 17–25-year contracts, fund plants up front, and build on-site gas.
  5. Profit goes to the scarce assets. Turbine and grid-equipment makers (GE Vernova: $176bn backlog) and regulated utilities ($1.3–1.4tn of 2026–30 capex) are the volume winners 386566. Nuclear-heavy IPPs won in 2024–25 and de-rated in 2026 83. SMRs are a 2030s story.

01 · Explain like I'm newWhat this layer is, and how electricity reaches a GPU

Every GPU is a very efficient space heater. Nearly all the electricity it draws turns into heat that has to be pumped away, which takes more electricity. W03 is everything upstream of the data center's fence that makes that electricity exist and arrive.

That means the power plants, the high-voltage wires and substations, the markets that price electricity and capacity, the regulators who decide who pays, and the equipment makers whose factories set the pace of the build-out: turbines, reactors, transformers, fuel cells and batteries. Inside the fence, the building, its switchgear and cooling belong to W04 Data centers; the chips that consume the power belong to W05.

Analogy

Think of an AI campus as a new city built overnight. A 1 GW campus draws about as much power as ~750,000–850,000 US homes A. You can build the houses (the data center) in 18–24 months. The water mains and power lines (the grid) take 5–10 years, and the reservoir (new power plants) takes 4–7 years for gas and 8–15 for large nuclear. So the whole industry now revolves around time-to-power: how fast you can get electrons to a site.

Homes math A: 1 GW at ~85% load ≈ 7.4 TWh/yr; an average US home uses ~10.5 MWh/yr; 7.4 TWh ÷ 10.5 MWh ≈ 0.7m homes, or ≈0.83m at 100% load.

Why can't a data center just plug into the grid like a factory?

Size and speed. A typical US data center in 2020 was ~25 MW; by 2025 the average was ~80 MW 15, and AI campuses are 1–5 GW, the peak load of a mid-sized US state. The grid near any given site was built for the load it already has. Adding a gigawatt means new plants somewhere, new or upgraded lines to carry the power, and new substations to receive it. Each of those needs studies, permits and equipment that is on multi-year backorder.

And the load is unusual: GPUs run near flat-out around the clock, and a training job can swing hundreds of megawatts in seconds. Grid operators worry about that as much as the volume.

Definition

Power vs energy. Power (MW, GW) is the rate of flow, like the width of a pipe; data centers are sized in MW. Energy (MWh, TWh) is power × time, like the water that has flowed. 1 MW for a year = 8,760 MWh; 1 GW for a year = 8.76 TWh. "BNEF: 194 GW by 2035" is capacity; "IEA: 945 TWh by 2030" is annual energy. Convert with TWh ≈ GW × 8.76 × utilization: at ~60–70% utilization, 50 GW ≈ 260–310 TWh/yr A.

Definition

PUE (power usage effectiveness) = total facility power ÷ IT power. At PUE 1.5, every 1 MW reaching the servers needs another 0.5 MW for cooling, conversion and lighting. The industry average is ~1.5 conf: med; new hyperscale AI halls with direct-to-chip liquid cooling run ~1.1–1.25. Lower PUE means more of each scarce grid MW reaches GPUs, which is why liquid cooling became mandatory.

Why AI broke the old data center: rack density

A legacy cloud rack drew ~5–10 kW. An NVIDIA GB200/GB300 NVL72 rack draws ~120–140 kW, and NVIDIA says "up to 200 kW" for NVL72-class systems 72. The IEA says AI server power density rose ~11× from 2020 to 2025 and will rise another ~4× by 2027, when one rack draws as much as ~65 households E 6. That concentrates demand into single 1–5 GW sites and makes power quality a grid issue, not just volume.

Why it matters

Every other layer's growth plan assumes the electrons show up. When they don't, GPUs sit in warehouses, as Microsoft's CEO said publicly in late 2025 conf: high on substance. W03 sets the pace of the whole build-out, even though it collects only a small slice of the money.

02 · How the power business worksWho builds, who sells, and who pays for electricity

US electricity is sold under two very different systems, and which one a data center lands in decides who pays for the new plants.

  • PPAs (power purchase agreements) are 10–20-year contracts at a fixed or indexed $/MWh. Hyperscalers sign virtual PPAs (financial swaps) for renewables and, increasingly, physical/firm PPAs for nuclear and gas.
  • Large-load tariffs are special rate classes for customers above ~25–100 MW: long minimum terms, minimum-demand charges (pay for 60–85% of contracted capacity even if unused) and collateral, so existing customers aren't stuck with a stranded plant if the data center never shows up (section 11).
  • Capacity auctions (PJM's Base Residual Auction) pay generators for being available at peak. The clearing price × capacity procured is a bill that lands on every retail customer.

Time-to-power is the new currency

BNEF measures the average development time from announcement to operation at 5.3 years for hyperscalers and 8.4 years for everyone else E 12. Every strategy in this layer is an attempt to shorten that:

  • Brownfield sites with existing grid connections: retired coal plants (Homer City, PA, 4.4 GW 87), bitcoin mines, nuclear sites.
  • Co-location at existing plants: Susquehanna and the FERC fights (section 11).
  • Behind-the-meter generation: on-site turbines, engines, fuel cells and batteries. The xAI Colossus, Crusoe/Stargate Abilene and Oracle/Bloom "Project Jupiter" model.
  • Bring your own generation and flexible load: accept curtailment in grid emergencies for a faster hookup. PJM's Interim Resource Adequacy Service (from Jun 2027) 28, OpenAI–Southern's 1 GW of flexible load in a 3.2 GW contract 56, Texas SB6.
  • Paying up: take-or-pay tariffs, collateral (AEP holds $2bn against its 45 GW Texas pipeline 55), and direct funding of substations and plants.

03 · Market sizeHow much power AI uses, and how fast forecasts are rising

Globally, data centers are still a small slice of electricity. Locally, in Virginia, Dublin, Texas, Georgia and Ohio, they dominate. And every forecast made in the last two years has been revised up.

The world: ~1.5% of electricity today, ~3% by 2030

The IEA puts global data-center electricity at ~415 TWh in 2024 (≈1.5% of world electricity) after ~12%/yr growth since 2017, more than four times the growth of total demand E 34. In 2025 it rose to ~485 TWh (+17%), with AI-focused sites up ~50% E 67. The base case reaches ~945–950 TWh by 2030 (~3%) and ~1,200 TWh by 2035, with a 700–1,700 TWh range across scenarios 3.

The US is the center of gravity: 45% of global data-center electricity in 2024 and the largest absolute increase to 2030. China adds proportionally more (+170%) from a lower base 34. US per-capita data-center use was ~540 kWh in 2024 and is heading above 1,200 kWh 4. For context, data centers are <10% of global electricity demand growth to 2030; air-conditioning, EVs and industry matter more globally 4.

The United States: from 4.4% to somewhere between 9% and 20%

LBNL measured US data centers at 176 TWh in 2023, 4.4% of US electricity, up from 58 TWh in 2014 1. Its 2028 scenarios run from 325 to 580 TWh (6.7–12%). EPRI's February 2026 study puts 2030 at 9% (low) to 17% (high), with new-load estimates 60% above its own 2024 study; Virginia goes from ~25% of state electricity today to 39–57%, and seven more states pass 20% (AZ, IN, IA, NE, NV, OR, WY) 9. BNEF sees ~5.9% of US consumption now and ~20% by the mid-2030s 1213.

The forecast ratchet

Forecasts keep going one way. In July 2026 BNEF raised its US data-center capacity forecast to 118 GW by 2030 and 194 GW by 2035, up from 106 GW for 2035 only seven months earlier (+83%) E 1214. Grid Strategies' five-year national peak-load growth forecast went from 24 GW (2022) to 166 GW (Nov 2025), of which ~90 GW is data centers; it thinks ~65 GW of that is realistic 11. For scale, FERC counted ~50 GW of US data centers online at end-2025 R 15.

Always check the denominator

US data-center "capacity" figures differ because they measure different things. FERC: ~50 GW online at end-2025 (facility capacity, 24% CAGR since 2020) 15. BNEF: ~25 GW operating in 2024 14. Goldman: 31 GW of average demand in 2025 against ~47.5 GW of capacity (70% utilization) 16. Some count nameplate, some IT load, some average draw, some include crypto mining.

Goldman Sachs expects US data-center demand to more than double from 31 GW (2025) to 66 GW (2027), taking data centers from 4.1% to 8.5% of summer peak 16. That requires 13.6 GW of new capacity in 2026 and 36.3 GW in 2027, and Goldman itself assumes only ~50–60% of scheduled capacity arrives on time.

Atlas view (conf: med)

2026: US data centers use ≈5.5–6% of US electricity, ~230–250 TWh (BNEF 5.9% × EIA ~4,135 TWh ≈ 244 TWh) A. 2030: ~9–11% (≈400–480 TWh) is realistic, at the low-to-middle of EPRI's range. The binding constraints are turbines, transformers and interconnection, not demand. Capacity: ~13–20 GW a year of additions in 2026–27 looks deliverable; the 36 GW "scheduled" for 2027 will slip.

What data centers actually pay for electricity

Our estimate A: US 2026 ≈ 244 TWh × an all-in average of $80–95/MWh (a blend of industrial rates near 9.8¢/kWh 20, commercial near 14.5¢, and wholesale-indexed hyperscale contracts) ≈ $20–23bn a year. Global 2025 ≈ 485 TWh × ~$85–100/MWh ≈ $41–49bn. Big-tech capex was over $400bn in 2025 and is forecast to rise ~75% in 2026 6, so the annual power bill is only ~5–8% of a single year's AI capex. That asymmetry (section 06) is why buyers pay nearly any price for speed.

04 · The bottleneckWhy power, not chips, became the binding constraint

In late 2025 Microsoft's CEO said the problem was no longer a chip shortage but a shortage of powered "shells": GPUs were sitting in inventory with nowhere to plug in conf: high on substance. Five things made that happen.

  1. Chips scaled faster than grids. TSMC's advanced-packaging capacity roughly doubled year after year (W06) and a GPU cluster installs in months. Transmission takes 7–10+ years, gas plants 4–6, and interconnecting even an existing site 2–5.
  2. The equipment is sold out. Gas turbines are booked into 2030–31 383940. Large power transformers take ~2–4 years and ran a ~30% US supply deficit in 2025 E 85. GE Vernova's Electrification backlog reached $41bn (+69% y/y) 39.
  3. The queues are clogged with speculative requests. ERCOT's large-load queue went from ~63 GW to 438 GW in 18 months, ~87–90% data centers 293031. Grid Strategies thinks utility forecasts overstate data-center load by ~25 GW (~40%) 11.
  4. Reserve margins are already thin. PJM cleared only 14.7–14.8% reserves against a 20% target in its last two auctions 2324.
  5. New supply is mostly the wrong shape. Of the record 86 GW the US adds in 2026, 67 GW is solar and batteries; only 6.3 GW is gas 21.

Gas turbines: a three-company oligopoly, sold out to 2030

GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Heavy supply the great majority of large (>100 MW) gas turbines worldwide conf: high on structure. All three are sold out and raising prices. GE Vernova's gas backlog plus slot reservations went 83 GW (end-2025) → 100 GW (Q1-26) → 116 GW (Q2-26), targeting ≥125 GW by year-end R 383940. In Q2 it added 20 GW of orders and reservations while shipping only 3 GW 39. About 20% of the 100 GW under contract in Q1 was explicitly for data-center customers 40.

Pricing follows scarcity. Q1-26 pricing was 10–20 points per kW higher than Q4-25 40. Reported equipment prices run ~$790/kW for heavy-duty, ~$950/kW for HA-class combined-cycle and ~$1,800/kW for aeroderivatives E 39 conf: med; equipment, not full plant. As of April only ~10 GW of combined 2029–30 capacity remained, and 2031 was expected to be "more than halfway contracted" by year-end 3940. Siemens Energy's backlog is €162bn with record Gas Services orders 41; Mitsubishi Heavy plans to roughly double gas-turbine output conf: med. The next tier fills the gap for on-site builds: Wärtsilä (Q2-26 orders €2.85bn, +33%) 42, Caterpillar/Solar Turbines, Baker Hughes, and refurbished aeroderivatives from ProEnergy/Solaris conf: med.

The queues: ERCOT's large-load line is five times its peak demand

Queues this large are mostly speculative: developers file the same campus in several places and see which connects first. That is why the next section's tariffs and collateral rules matter, and why the real share of queued load is the central unknown of this whole layer (somewhere in 25–60%).

05 · Money in / money outWhere the money comes from and where it goes

Data centers pay for electrons. Everyone else pays for capacity scarcity. And the money flowing out of this layer into new plants and wires dwarfs the money flowing in from data centers in any single year.

Hyperscalers, neoclouds and AI labs (W08, W09) pay utilities and IPPs for electricity and capacity, and increasingly pay for generation up front through prepayments, take-or-pay tariffs and "bring your own generation". Existing ratepayers pay part of the bill through capacity markets and grid upgrades, and that is where the politics lives. Utilities and IPPs in turn pay turbine makers, EPC contractors, transformer and switchgear makers (overlapping W04), gas producers and pipelines, nuclear vendors, and their own bondholders and shareholders (W02).

The flows out are an order of magnitude larger. US investor-owned utilities plan ~$1.3–1.4tn of capex over 2026–30 (~$260–280bn a year) E 6566, recovered from all customers over decades through rate base. That is more than twelve times the annual data-center power bill.

The capacity windfall, as one chain

In regulated states a well-designed large-load tariff makes the data center carry its generation and wires. In PJM, the capacity auction socializes the scarcity premium: when data-center load pushes the clearing price from $29 to $330/MW-day, every MW of load pays the higher price, including homes, hospitals and factories, and every existing generator earns it.

06 · Unit economicsWhat a megawatt-hour costs, and why speed beats price

A new gas plant on the grid makes power for ~$60–75/MWh. Hyperscalers are paying $80–120 for nuclear and on-site power instead, and it still makes sense, because electricity is a small line in the cost of AI compute.

How a new gas plant's cost builds up, and why on-site costs 60% more

New CCGT at 2026 costs A. Installed cost ~$2,200/kW (industry commentary puts full-plant cost at ~$2,000–2,800/kW, roughly 2–3× 2019 levels conf: med). A capital recovery factor of 8.5% (≈7.5% WACC, 30 years) gives $187k per MW-year; at a 75% capacity factor (6,570 MWh) that is $28.5/MWh. Fuel: heat rate 6.4 MMBtu/MWh × $3.75 = $24/MWh. O&M ≈ $7/MWh. Total ≈ $60/MWh at 75% CF, ≈$70 at 55% CF.

Behind-the-meter gas A. Engines or aeroderivatives at $2,000–2,400/kW with a 1.4–1.6× overbuild for redundancy (the IEA says on-site systems overbuild 30–70% 6) cost ~$3,000–3,600 per kW of load. An 11% capital recovery factor (shorter life, higher risk) over 7,450 MWh (85% load factor) gives $44–53/MWh of capital. Fuel at a heat rate of ~8.5–9.5 is $32–36/MWh; O&M and emissions controls add ~$10–15. Total ≈ $90–105/MWh, up to ~$120 with delivered-gas premiums: 30–60% more than grid power, which buys 2–4 years of time-to-power.

Existing plants trade at about half the cost of new ones

Existing gas plants change hands at ~50–60% of replacement cost, and replacement can't be delivered before ~2030. That gap explains the IPP M&A wave (Calpine, LS Power, Cogentrix) and why "powered land" (a site with an interconnection agreement and nearby generation) commands large premiums. Interconnection rights have become an asset class: Constellation is transferring capacity rights from Eddystone to Crane 47; Talen sold AWS its 960 MW Cumulus data-center campus for $650m (~$680/kW) in March 2024 R 91.

Power is ~10% of the cost of AI compute, but gates 100% of it

Per 1 MW of IT load in a GB300-class AI hall A: GPUs, servers and network (~$30m of capex over a 5-year life) cost ~$6.0m a year; the facility (~$11–13m/MW, see W04, over ~18 years) ~$0.65–0.7m; electricity (1 MW × PUE 1.2 × 8,760 h × 85% load ≈ 8,935 MWh × $70–100/MWh) $0.63–0.89m; staff and other ~$0.1–0.2m. Electricity is ~8–12% of a ~$7.4–7.8m annual total.

  • Speed dominates price. A GW of GPUs idle for a month burns ~$500m of depreciation; six months, ~$3bn. That is more than the entire lifetime premium of most power deals.
  • The power bill is the last cost to disappear. Take-or-pay minimums at 60–85% of contracted demand 36 are a real stranded-cost risk for the buyer and the protection for the utility.
  • A cross-check with the Atlas canonical figure. This TCO assumes ~$41–43m of capex per MW. The Atlas's canonical all-in cost of a GW is ~$50–60bn (Blackwell) to ~$60–70bn (Rubin) (W11). On those numbers electricity sits at the low end of the range, nearer 8%.

What it means for a household

For a typical PJM home (~900 kWh/month, ~2.5 kW peak-load contribution), capacity at $270/MW-day costs $270 × 2.5 kW ÷ 1,000 × 30 days ≈ $20 a month, versus ≈$2 at the 2024/25 price A. The market monitor's attribution implies increases of ~$16–18 a month in western Maryland and Ohio 26.

07 · Key playersWho builds the power: OEMs, utilities, IPPs and on-site specialists

Four kinds of company sell into AI's power demand. Equipment makers (turbines, transformers, fuel cells) sell to everyone. Regulated utilities build plants and wires and earn an allowed return. Independent power producers own plants and sell power and capacity at market or contract prices. Advanced-nuclear developers sell options on the 2030s.

11 · Bottlenecks & dependenciesQueues, auctions, regulators and the ratepayer backlash

The physical bottlenecks (turbines, transformers, lines) are covered in section 04. This section is about the institutional ones: who gets to connect, who pays, and how fast the rules are changing.

Interconnection queues

  • Generation queues: US generation and storage interconnection queues held roughly 2,000+ GW in recent LBNL "Queued Up" editions, with a median ~5 years from request to operation conf: med. Most is solar and storage that will never be built.
  • ERCOT (Texas): ~63 GW (end-2024) → 233 GW (end-2025) → ~410 GW (Mar 2026) → 438 GW (Jun 2026), ~87–90% data centers 293031. The PUCT approved "Batch Zero" on 18 Jun 2026: loads ≥75 MW are studied together, applicants were notified in Aug 2026, the final transmission plan comes in fall 2027 and Batch 1 opens summer 2027 31. Intermediate agreements carry security of $50,000/MW (20% refundable) 30. Texas SB6 (2025) lets ERCOT curtail large loads and requires remote disconnect and disclosure of backup generation.
  • PJM: 70 GW of large-load growth projected through 2038 28. The board proposes to exclude new loads without dedicated generation from future forecasts and to require them to curtail in emergencies from 1 Jun 2027 ("Interim Resource Adequacy Service") 28.
  • Utilities: Dominion's 53.8 GW and AEP's 69 GW are "contracted", but the stages range from construction letters to firm agreements 5558. Grid Strategies warns utility forecasts overstate data-center load by ~25 GW, some with "unrealistically high load factors" 11.

Reading the scoreboard

The market is sending the strongest price signal in its history and supply still isn't responding: 774 MW and 525 MW of new generation cleared. New plants can't physically arrive by 2027–29 because of turbines and interconnection, and a one-year capacity price, however high, isn't bankable for a 30-year plant. Hence the pivot to 15-year backstop contracts: effectively a return to long-term, quasi-regulated procurement.

  • Tech companies signed ~40% of all corporate renewable PPAs in 2025 7. Microsoft says it matched 100% of FY25 electricity with renewables on an annual basis 90. NextEra added 3.6 GW to its 35.1 GW backlog in Q2-26 alone 60.

Six disruptors to watch

  1. Flexible data centers. Duke University research (early 2025) estimated ~76–126 GW of new load could connect with modest curtailment (~0.25–1% of hours) conf: med. Now policy via PJM's IRAS, OpenAI–Southern's 1 GW flexible provision, SB6 and FERC's flexible service 283356.
  2. Efficiency. Energy per AI task has fallen by "at least an order of magnitude annually" 6. The bear case for power demand rests here.
  3. Geothermal, long-duration storage and nuclear uprates (Fervo–Google, Sage; Southern's ~6 GW of uprates and renewals 57) are the cheapest "new" firm clean MW.
  4. Turbine catch-up. GEV at 30 GW/yr by 2030 38 plus Siemens and MHI expansion and new entrants (Doosan, Baker Hughes, refurbished aeros) could flip turbines from shortage to glut in the early 2030s.
  5. Hyperscaler vertical integration. Google–Intersect, Microsoft's self-funded Pecos 2 GW, Meta's utility deals, and Vistra–NVIDIA–KKR Helix. If hyperscalers own generation, utility and IPP margins get squeezed.
  6. Consolidation. NextEra–Dominion, Constellation–Calpine, NRG–LS Power, Vistra–Cogentrix: scale matters for balance sheets, turbine-slot access and regulatory muscle.

13 · Bull vs bearElectrons are the new oil, or the queue is the bubble

Bull case

"Electrons are the new oil, and we're short"

  • Forecasts keep rising. BNEF +83% in seven months (106 → 194 GW) 1214; EPRI's new load 60% above its 2024 study 9; Grid Strategies 24 → 166 GW 11.
  • Scarcity is locked in to ~2030. Turbines sold out 40, transformers in deficit 85, PJM short 6.6–6.8 GW even at the cap 2324, only 6.3 GW of US gas in 2026 21.
  • Buyers are price-insensitive. Power is ~10% of AI TCO and delay costs ~$500m per GW-month A; 17–25-year contracts and collateral (AEP $2bn) 495556.
  • Utility earnings compound. $1.3–1.4tn capex 6566, 11–13% rate-base growth (Xcel) 63, cheap DOE money 57, M&A premiums 59.
  • OEM pricing power. GEV guides FY26 FCF to $11.5–12.5bn 38 conf: low (the claimed step-up from $6.5–7.5bn is doubted), and its output reaches only 30 GW/yr by 2030.
  • Existing nuclear is irreplaceable. New nuclear costs $120–220/MWh 84 vs contracted existing nuclear at ~$85–115 A.

Bear case

"The queue is the bubble"

  • Phantom load. ERCOT's 438 GW queue is ~5× its peak 31; ~25 GW of utility forecasts is overstatement 11; up to 50% of the 2026 pipeline may never materialize (Sightline) 36; new pipeline additions halved Q3→Q4-25 17.
  • Efficiency could win. Energy per task falls ~10×/yr 6. If AI revenue (W10) disappoints and capex (W01) flattens in 2027–28, the 2029–31 turbine wave lands in an air pocket, as in 2002–05.
  • Politics caps the upside. PJM capped at $325–333 2324; pledges and legislation shift costs to data centers 3437; FERC declared BTM netting unjust 33; IPPs de-rated (VST −33% y/y, TLN −26%, CEG ~37% off its high) 83.
  • Merchant prices aren't rising. ERCOT ~$30/MWh, flat 4453; Constellation's 920 MW of Q2 contracts were mostly non-hyperscaler 4344.
  • Nuclear timelines slip. Crane may face a grid delay to 2031 47; SMR first power 2028–31 at best 747779; Oklo ~80% off its high 83.
  • On-site is expensive and dirty. ~$90–120/MWh A, permitting pushback (xAI/EPA) 68, overbuild risk, and the reported Oracle force-majeure notice 83.

Analyst synthesis

The bull case holds through ~2029 for scarce assets: turbines, transformers, interconnection rights and existing nuclear. The bear case strengthens for 2030+, when OEM capacity roughly doubles and demand depends on AI monetization rather than a race to build. The biggest mispricing risk is in long-dated new-build gas without take-or-pay cover and in pre-revenue SMR equities.

17 · GlossaryTerms on this page

ATC
Around-the-clock: a flat 24/7 block of power, the typical hyperscaler "firm" product.
Aeroderivative
A gas turbine derived from a jet engine (e.g. LM2500/LM6000). Fast to deploy, less efficient; often used on-site.
Batch Zero
ERCOT's 2026 cluster-study process that studies large loads (75 MW and up) together rather than one by one.
BESS
Battery energy storage system.
BRA
Base Residual Auction: PJM's main capacity auction, held about three years (lately less) before the delivery year.
BTM
Behind-the-meter: generation on the customer's side of the utility meter, such as on-site turbines. Avoids some grid charges; FERC is now restricting "netting".
BYOG
Bring your own generation: a large load arrives with new supply so it does not burden the grid.
Capacity market
A market that pays generators ($/MW-day) for being available to generate, or loads for curtailing, at peak. Separate from energy ($/MWh).
Capacity factor
Actual output (or consumption, for a load factor) divided by the maximum possible over a period.
CCGT
Combined-cycle gas turbine: a gas turbine plus a steam turbine that uses its waste heat. ~60%+ efficient; the grid's baseload gas workhorse.
Co-location
A data center physically sited at a power plant, taking power directly from it.
ELCC
Effective load-carrying capability: how much a resource counts toward reliability (solar counts little at winter peaks).
EPC
Engineering, procurement and construction contractor that builds a plant (e.g. Kiewit, Bechtel).
ERCOT
The Texas grid operator, mostly outside FERC jurisdiction.
ESA
Electric service agreement between a utility and a customer.
FERC
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission. Regulates wholesale power markets and interstate transmission.
FLAP-D
Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin: Europe's legacy data-center hubs.
Front-of-meter
Supply delivered through the grid (paying transmission charges), as opposed to behind-the-meter.
HALEU
High-assay low-enriched uranium (5–20% U-235), needed by many advanced reactors. Supply is limited.
Heat rate
Fuel burned (MMBtu) per MWh generated. Lower is more efficient: CCGT ~6.3–6.6; engines and aeroderivatives ~8.5–9.5.
IMM
Independent market monitor. PJM's is Monitoring Analytics.
Interconnection queue
The line of generators or large loads waiting for grid-impact studies and hookup.
IPP
Independent power producer: owns plants and sells power and capacity into markets (Constellation, Vistra, Talen, NRG).
ISA
Interconnection service agreement.
IT load
The power drawn by the servers, storage and network gear themselves, before cooling and conversion overhead.
Large-load tariff
A special rate class for very large customers (roughly 25–100 MW and up) with minimum charges, long terms and collateral.
LCOE
Levelized cost of electricity: lifetime cost per MWh including capital, fuel and O&M.
Microreactor
A very small reactor, below roughly 20 MWe.
MW
Megawatt: a rate of power flow. 1 GW = 1,000 MW.
MWh
Megawatt-hour: an amount of energy. 1 MW for 1 hour = 1 MWh; 1 TWh = 1 million MWh.
NRC
Nuclear Regulatory Commission.
PJM
The largest US grid operator (RTO): 13 states plus DC, including Northern Virginia's "Data Center Alley".
PPA
Power purchase agreement: a long-term contract to buy a plant's output or attributes at a fixed or indexed price. A virtual PPA is a financial contract-for-differences with no physical delivery.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total facility power divided by IT power. 1.2 means 0.2 MW of overhead per 1 MW reaching the servers.
Rate base
A regulated utility's net invested capital, on which regulators let it earn an allowed return.
Reserve margin
Capacity above forecast peak demand kept for reliability. PJM targets ~20%.
ROE
Return on equity: the profit rate regulators allow a utility to earn on its equity-funded rate base (~9.5–10% today).
RTO
Regional transmission organization (or ISO): the operator that runs a regional grid and wholesale market, e.g. PJM, ERCOT, MISO.
Slot reservation
A paid option on a future turbine manufacturing slot; GE Vernova's key backlog metric.
SMR
Small modular reactor: a reactor below roughly 300 MWe, designed for factory-built, repeatable units.
Substation
A facility where transformers and switchgear change voltage (up for transmission, down for delivery) and switch circuits.
Take-or-pay
The buyer pays for the contracted volume whether or not it uses it.
TCO
Total cost of ownership: all capital and operating costs of running an asset, per year or over its life.
TWh
Terawatt-hour: 1 billion kWh. 1 GW running for a year = 8.76 TWh.
Uprate
Increasing an existing reactor's licensed output.
ZEC
Zero-emission credit: a state subsidy that keeps nuclear plants open (e.g. Illinois, to mid-2027).

The 60-second version

  1. About $1.0 trillion is being spent in 2026 on Western AI data centers A, up from ~$290B in 2024 and ~$550B in 2025.11 Five companies (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle) account for ~$815B of it A.2
  2. Most of it buys chips that wear out in 5–6 years. About two-thirds is IT equipment, and Nvidia alone receives ~35 cents of every dollar A.11
  3. The suppliers keep the profit. About 51 cents of every capex dollar becomes some supplier's operating profit: Nvidia (~$240B in 2026E), the memory makers (~$149B from AI buyers), TSMC and Broadcom A.11 The model labs lose ~$48B A.
  4. The money mostly doesn't come from AI customers yet. Of ~$1.12T flowing through the build-out in 2026, ~49% is Big Tech's existing profit, ~18% new debt and only ~18% end-customer AI money A.11
  5. But customers are arriving fast. End-customer AI money went ~$24B → ~$70B → ~$200B (2024 → 2026E), about 3× a year, driven by agents, especially coding agents A.10 Capex per revenue dollar fell from ~12× to ~5×.
  6. The open question is timing. The capex already committed needs ~$600B a year of end-customer revenue to earn a normal return, about 2× today's run-rate. Our base case gets there around 2028 A.11 Meanwhile the bottleneck has moved from GPUs to packaging to power to memory, and now to permits and politics.

How to read this page

Every number carries a provenance tag: R reported or filed, G company guidance, E third-party estimate, A our own estimate. Citations like 5 point to the layer dossier that holds the primary sources. Headline numbers come from the atlas's canonical list, so every page agrees. 2026 has been an extraordinary year and many figures come from press reports rather than audited filings. 01 · Start hereA power grid, a factory and a retail chain

The AI build-out is easier to understand if you picture three familiar things stacked on top of each other.

A power grid (the bottom). Everything starts with electricity. An AI campus needs gigawatts of it, the output of a large nuclear plant, continuously. Utilities, power plants, gas turbines, transformers and transmission lines are the bottom layer (W03 Energy & power). Then comes the building that turns electricity into a place where computers can run: the shell, the switchgear, backup power and the cooling that removes the heat (W04 Data-center infrastructure). Like a grid, it is slow to build (years), heavily regulated and long-lived (15–30 years).

A factory (the middle). Inside the building is a factory that makes one product: tokens, the small chunks of text, code, images or actions that AI models generate. Its machines are AI chips: Nvidia GPUs, Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium (W05 Compute silicon). The machines come from the most advanced manufacturing chain on earth: TSMC's fabs in Taiwan, memory makers in Korea and the US, Dutch lithography tools (W06). Switches, lasers and fiber wire thousands of machines into one computer, and server makers assemble the racks (W07). The factory owners, the cloud companies (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle, CoreWeave), rent out machine time by the hour (W08 Cloud).

A retail chain (the top). The model labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI) are the designers: they spend enormous sums training a model once, then sell its output by the token (W09). The applications are the storefronts where customers actually pay: ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft 365 Copilot, Cursor, and the AI-ranked ads on Instagram and Google (W10).

Two more pieces sit alongside the stack. The order book (W01 Demand & capex) records who is placing orders and how much they commit. The bank (W02 Capital & financing) explains how it is paid for: profits, bonds, private credit, venture capital, sovereign funds, and suppliers who invest in their own customers.

Analogy

Imagine a chain of stores that builds its factories and power stations before most shoppers have walked in, and pays for them out of the profits of an older business it already owns. That is the AI build-out in 2026.

The twist that makes AI unusual: the factory is being built before most of the customers have arrived. In a normal industry, shoppers' money flows down the stack and pays the factory and the power company. In 2026 most of the money enters in the middle instead: from Big Tech's existing businesses and from investors, in anticipation of customers A.11 Whether the customers arrive fast enough decides who wins and who loses.

Explain like I'm new: what exactly is being "built out"?

Physical things. Warehouse-sized buildings full of computer racks, each rack holding dozens of AI chips that draw as much power as a small neighborhood. Around them: electrical rooms, cooling plants, backup generators, fiber links, and new power plants and transmission lines to feed them. A 1-gigawatt campus with chips costs about $50–60B E.4

"Capex" (capital expenditure) is money spent on long-lasting assets like these. It is recorded as an asset and written off (depreciated) over years: roughly 5–6 years for chips and servers, 15–25 years for buildings. That is why a capex boom today becomes a depreciation bill tomorrow.

Definition

End-customer AI money: what consumers, businesses and advertisers actually pay for AI products, AI cloud services and AI-driven ads, net of what AI companies pay each other. It is the only money in the system that does not have to be paid back or earned elsewhere first.

02 · The stackTen layers, four blocks, one flow of money

The atlas splits the build-out into ten layers, W01 to W10, and a synthesis (W11). To follow the money it helps to group them into four blocks sitting on a financing base A.11 Customers pay at the top. Model makers turn compute into products. Compute owners buy and run the capacity. Suppliers build the machines, buildings and electrons, and today they keep most of the profit.

Each row below is a layer. Hover or tab through a row to see what it does and how it makes money; click it to open that layer's page. The bar shows each layer's 2026E operating profit (red means a loss).

Why it matters

Read the profit column top to bottom and you see the build-out's central fact: operating margins fall the closer a company is to the end customer. Memory makers earn 70–80%, Nvidia and TSMC ~60–66%, clouds 30–39%, contractors 10–17%, server assemblers 3–4%, and the labs −47% A.11 That is the reverse of a mature software industry, and it will persist until scarcity moves to the one thing no supplier can manufacture: customers' willingness to pay.

03 · The layersThe stack, one layer at a time

Each profile answers four questions: what the layer does, who the big players are, how big it is, and how it makes money. Sizes are 2026 estimates of the AI-related part unless noted A.11 The pills show each layer's 2026E AI revenue and operating profit; each heading links to the full layer page.

W01 · Demand & capex: the buyers Spends ~$1.0T

What it does
Covers the companies that decide to buy chips, pour concrete and sign 15-year power contracts, and the evidence that someone will pay for what they build.
Who
The "Big 4" hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta), plus Oracle, the neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe), labs building their own capacity (xAI inside SpaceX, OpenAI's Stargate, Anthropic's projects) and sovereign buyers (the UAE, Saudi Arabia, the EU, India).
How big
The Big 4's 2026 capex guidance adds up to ~$733B (Amazon ~$220B, Alphabet $195–205B, Microsoft ~$175B, Meta $130–145B), ~1.8× 2025 G.1 In Q2 2026 they spent $165.1B in one quarter, 96% of their operating cash flow (41% in 2023) RA.1 Their contracted cloud backlog totals ~$2.46T, ~43% of it owed by OpenAI and Anthropic RA conf: low–med.1
How it makes money
It doesn't, directly: this is the spending layer. The return comes later through cloud rentals (W08), better ads and AI products.
The strain is visible
Alphabet posted its first negative free-cash-flow quarter since its 2004 IPO (−$5.9B in Q2 2026) R, and Big-4 depreciation is heading from $139B (2025) to ~$250–300B (2027E) RA.1

W02 · Capital & financing: where the money comes from Fees ~$30B

What it does
Pays for the build-out.
Who
Big Tech treasuries; bond investors such as PIMCO and BlackRock; private-credit and infrastructure funds (Blue Owl, Apollo, Brookfield, BlackRock-GIP); banks (JPMorgan, MUFG); venture capital; sovereign funds (MGX, GIC); SoftBank; and suppliers that invest in their customers (Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, AMD).
How big
Hyperscaler bond issuance reached ~$220B by 10 August 2026, against $28–35B a year in 2020–24 RE.2 Global VC hit $510B in H1 2026, and OpenAI plus Anthropic took 43% of it R.2 New structures appeared, such as Meta's off-balance-sheet Hyperion SPV bond ($27.3B at 6.58%) R.2
How it makes money
Financiers earn fees and interest spreads, roughly $30B a year of new income A.2 The big "profits" are paper gains on private AI stakes, such as Amazon's ~$53B one-quarter gain on Anthropic R.2
Why it matters
2026 is the year the build-out stopped paying for itself out of cash flow. Debt now funds about as much as AI customers do A.11

W03 · Energy & power: the electrons Rev. $46BOp. $15B

What it does
Generates and delivers the electricity.
Who
Utilities (NextEra, Dominion, AEP, Southern, Duke, Entergy), independent power producers (Constellation, Vistra, Talen), turbine and grid-equipment makers (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi), fuel-cell and on-site suppliers (Bloom), and small-modular-reactor developers (Oklo, TerraPower, X-energy).
How big
Global data-center electricity use was ~485 TWh in 2025 (+17%) E.3 US data centers used 4.4% of US electricity in 2023, heading to 6.7–12% by 2028 E. US utilities plan ~$1.3–1.4T of capex over 2026–30 E.3
How it makes money
It sells electrons (AI data centers pay ~$16B a year A), earns regulated returns (~9.8% allowed ROE) on a fast-growing rate base, sells gas turbines that are sold out to ~2030 (GE Vernova's gas backlog plus reservations: 116 GW R), and collects scarcity prices: the PJM capacity price rose more than 11×, from $29 to $333/MW-day R.3
The catch
Power is only ~8–12% of the cost of running AI compute, but it gates 100% of it A, and household bills are rising (Virginia +26% y/y in Feb 2026), which has turned power into a political fight R.3

W04 · Data-center infrastructure: the buildings and the kit inside Rev. $331BOp. $52B

What it does
Everything between the high-voltage line and the chip rack: land, shell, electrical chain, cooling, construction labor, and the companies that develop, own and lease data centers.
Who
Equipment makers (Vertiv, Schneider, Eaton, ABB, Legrand, Trane); landlords and developers (Equinix, Digital Realty, Aligned, QTS, Vantage, Crusoe); bitcoin miners turned landlords (Core Scientific, Applied Digital, TeraWulf); specialty contractors (Quanta, EMCOR, Comfort Systems).
How big
Vacancy in North American primary markets is 1.4% (Northern Virginia 0.24%) E.4 More than 66 GW is under construction, 95% pre-committed E. A building costs ~$11–18M per MW without chips; with chips, 1 GW costs ~$50–60B E.4
How it makes money
Equipment makers earn 20–28% operating margins on sold-out gear (Vertiv guides $14.0B of 2026 sales G). Landlords earn ~$120–200/kW/month rents and 9–13% development yields A. Contractors earn scarcity rents on skilled electricians. AI-related revenue is ~$331B and operating profit ~$52B A.11

W05 · Compute silicon: the machines Rev. $456BOp. $283.5B

What it does
Makes the chips that do AI's arithmetic: GPUs (general-purpose parallel processors) and custom ASICs (chips designed for one customer's workloads).
Who
Nvidia dominates. Broadcom designs custom chips for Google (TPU), Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. AMD is the second GPU source. Marvell, Alchip and MediaTek design chips for AWS and others; Cerebras is a newly listed challenger.
How big
Nvidia's Q2 FY27 (May–Jul 2026) revenue was $96.2B, of which $89.0B was data center (+117% y/y); it guides $108B for the next quarter RG.5 The accelerator market is ~$365B in 2026E, ~82% of it Nvidia by dollars A.5
How it makes money
By selling the scarcest, most integrated system in the stack (GPUs, NVLink racks, networking and CUDA software) at a ~75% gross margin and ~65% operating margin R.5 Nvidia's revenue per gigawatt of data center rose from ~$18B (Hopper) to ~$25B (Blackwell) to ~$40B (Vera Rubin) G. This layer earns ~44% of all AI operating profit A.11

W06 · Semiconductor supply chain: the factories behind the chips Rev. $323BOp. $203.4B

What it does
Manufactures what the chip designers design: the foundries that print the logic (TSMC), the advanced packaging that joins logic and memory (TSMC's CoWoS), the memory makers that supply HBM and DRAM (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron), the equipment makers (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, KLA, Tokyo Electron) and the chip-design software vendors (Synopsys, Cadence, Arm).
How big
TSMC has 72.5% of global foundry revenue E and essentially all leading-edge AI chips. Its 2Q26 gross margin was a record 67.7% R, and 2026 revenue should be ~$173B GA.6 The memory market is forecast at ~$889B in 2026 after DRAM contract prices rose ~5× in a year E.6
How it makes money
Through chokepoints. Micron's gross margin is 84.9%; SK hynix's operating margin is ~76% R.6 HBM uses ~3× the wafer area per bit of normal DRAM, so AI demand starves the rest of the memory market and every PC and phone maker pays more E. AI-attributable operating profit is ~$203B (~32% of the pool), the fastest-rising share in the stack A.11

W07 · Networking, optics & systems: the wiring and the racks Rev. $130BOp. $25B

What it does
Turns thousands of chips into one working machine: switches, network cards, lasers, optical transceivers, fiber and copper cables inside and between racks; and the server makers who assemble racks.
Who
Nvidia (NVLink, InfiniBand, Spectrum-X), Broadcom (Tomahawk switch chips), Arista, Cisco, Celestica; optics makers (InnoLight, Coherent, Lumentum, Fabrinet); connectivity chips (Credo, Astera Labs); cables (Amphenol, Corning); server makers (Dell, Supermicro, HPE) and contract manufacturers (Foxconn, Quanta, Wiwynn).
How big
Nvidia's networking business runs at ~$70B a year A.7 Dell carries a $95B AI-server backlog R. A GB300 NVL72 rack sells for ~$3.7M, ~75–80% of which is Nvidia silicon A.7
How it makes money
Unevenly. Chip and laser makers earn 35–75% gross margins (lasers are "a 2-year problem… until 2028," says Arista); assemblers earn 3–10% (Foxconn's operating margin is 3.75%) R.7 Excluding Nvidia and Broadcom, the layer's AI operating profit is ~$25B A.11

W08 · Cloud: the factory owners who rent out machine time Rev. $118BOp. $31.5B

What it does
Owns AI compute and rents it by the GPU-hour or by committed contract.
Who
AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure; the neoclouds CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe, Nscale and IREN.
How big
The worldwide cloud-infrastructure market was $143.4B in Q2 2026 (+43%), the fastest growth in eight years E.8 AWS $42.2B (+37%), Azure +43%, Google Cloud +82%, Oracle's OCI +121% R. Backlog (RPO): Microsoft $678B, Oracle $664B, Google Cloud $514B, AWS $496B, CoreWeave $104B R.8
How it makes money
By renting scarce capacity on multi-year take-or-pay contracts. An H100 GPU rents for a median $3.39/hour (+12% y/y), above its ~$2.10 five-year break-even EA.8 Hyperscalers earn 35–39% segment margins thanks to cheap capital and their own chips. Neoclouds have high gross margins (CoreWeave 66%) but lose money at the operating line because of depreciation and ~9% debt costs: CoreWeave's GAAP operating margin was −2% and Nebius's −30% in Q2 2026 R.8 AI cloud revenue is ~$118B and operating profit ~$33B A.11

W09 · Model labs: the designers Rev. $103BOp. −$48B

What it does
Trains large AI models and sells their output through APIs (priced per million tokens), subscriptions and, increasingly, agents that complete whole tasks.
Who
OpenAI and Anthropic (independent frontier labs); Google DeepMind (Gemini), Meta Superintelligence Labs and Microsoft AI (inside their parents); SpaceXAI (xAI, merged into SpaceX in Feb 2026); Mistral, Cohere, SSI, Thinking Machines and Reflection.
How big
Anthropic's run-rate went from ~$1B (Dec 2024) to $65B at end-July 2026 E, press-reported and confirmed by our fact-check. A September report of more than $100B is unverified conf: low. OpenAI's run-rate was $40B in July E,9 so the confirmed comparison is ~1.6×. Valuations: OpenAI $852B (March round of $122B), Anthropic $965B (May, $65B Series H); both have filed confidentially for IPOs R.9 ChatGPT has more than 1B weekly users R.
How it makes money
Gross margin is the spread between the token price and the GPU-seconds needed to produce the token: ~33% reported at OpenAI in 2025 (~43% implied by its leaked revenue and cost lines), ~58% at Anthropic in Q2 2026. An April-2026 amendment ended OpenAI's share of Microsoft's revenue and dropped the "AGI clause" from their contract. But training the next model costs tens of billions. OpenAI lost ~$20.9B on $13.1B of revenue in 2025 and projects $278B of negative free cash flow over 2026–30 E.9 Anthropic reportedly made its first operating profit in Q2 2026 E conf: med. As a layer, labs lose ~$48B in 2026E A.11

W10 · Applications & agents: where end-customer money enters Rev. $83BOp. $26.5B

What it does
Sells AI to the people and companies who ultimately pay: consumer subscriptions, enterprise seats and licences, usage-priced agents and API calls, outcome-priced services, and AI-improved advertising.
Who
The labs' own products (ChatGPT, Claude, Claude Code, Codex, Gemini); incumbents (Microsoft 365 Copilot with 30M+ paid seats R, GitHub Copilot, Salesforce Agentforce, ServiceNow); AI-native apps (Cursor, acquired by SpaceX for $60B, Cognition, Replit, Lovable, Perplexity, Harvey, Sierra, Decagon); and ad platforms (Meta's AI-driven Advantage+ at a $75B run-rate R).10
How big
Direct end-customer spend on AI apps and models is ~$130B in 2026E, up from ~$11B in 2024. Add ~$20B of enterprise AI cloud services and ~$50B of AI-driven ad uplift, and total end-customer AI money is ~$200B A.10
How it makes money
Unevenly. Ads and incumbents' bundles are profitable. The fastest-growing AI-native apps run ~25% gross margins because every task burns tokens E. Pricing is shifting from seats to usage to outcomes (Intercom's Fin charges $0.99 per resolved ticket) so that AI can compete for labor budgets, which are far larger than IT budgets E.10

04 · The spendingA trillion dollars a year, mostly on short-lived chips

Western AI data-center investment roughly doubled in each of the last two years: ~$290B in 2024, ~$550B in 2025 and ~$1.0T in 2026 A.11 Our base case has it rising to ~$1.35T in 2027 (bull $1.5T, bear $1.2T) A. The figure is a reconciliation: W01 counts ~$0.95T, W02 ~$1.075T and W10 ~$0.9T, depending on whether they include developer-built shells leased to tenants and non-AI capex; the canonical range is $0.9–1.1T.11

Who spends it. Mostly five companies. The Big 4's 2026 guidance sums to ~$733B; add Oracle and the Big 5 reach ~$815B A.12 Each of them raised its guide during the year: Alphabet's February guide was $175–185B; by July it was $195–205B G.1

What it buys. About two-thirds is IT equipment that wears out in 5–6 years; about one-third is buildings, power and cooling A.11 Nvidia alone receives ~35 cents of every dollar. Memory bought directly is ~15% (plus ~6% more as HBM inside accelerators), which makes memory the second-largest line item in an AI data center. That is a price effect, not a volume effect: DRAM contract prices rose ~5× in a year while bit shipments grow only in the low-to-mid 20s % E.6

The strain on the buyers

2026 is the year the build-out stopped paying for itself. In Q2 2026 the Big 4 spent $165.1B of cash capex in a single quarter R, equal to 96% of their operating cash flow, against ~41% in 2023 A.1 Their trailing free cash flow fell to ~$150B, from a $235B peak in Q1 2024 A. Alphabet posted its first negative free-cash-flow quarter since its IPO (−$5.9B) R; Oracle burned −$23.7B of free cash flow in FY26 R and was cut to BBB- by S&P on 9 July R.2 Buybacks are being cut, and bond issuance and new equity (Alphabet's programme of up to $90B) fill the gap.2

05 · The money mapWhere the money comes from, and where it ends up

About $1.12T flows through the Western build-out in 2026 A.11 Only about a fifth of it comes from people paying for AI. The largest source, by far, is the existing profit of Big Tech's older businesses: Google Search, Meta's ads, Amazon's retail, Microsoft Office and non-AI cloud.

The Sankey diagram below follows every dollar from its source (left) to where it ends up (right). Customers pay apps, labs and ad platforms. Labs pay clouds and build their own capacity. Clouds and builders, funded mainly by their own legacy cash flow and bonds, spend ~$1.0T of capex. That capex pays chip vendors, memory makers, networking and server vendors, equipment makers and builders; the chip vendors in turn pay TSMC and the HBM makers A.11 Hover a band or a node to trace its path.

Five things the map shows

  1. The build-out is funded upside-down. In a mature industry, customers' money flows down the stack and pays for capacity. In 2026, ~82% of the money entering the stack is not end-customer AI spend: it is legacy profits, debt and equity betting that customers will arrive A.11
  2. Half of all the money becomes somebody's operating profit, and ~70% of that profit sits in three places: Nvidia, the memory makers and TSMC. That is the definition of a supplier's market.
  3. The labs are a pass-through with a loss attached. They take in ~$111B of revenue, spend ~$130B on compute and ~$45B on people, and cover the difference with ~$64B of equity and debt. Every dollar of lab revenue triggers ~$1.2 of compute spending A.
  4. Advertising is the quiet giant. The ~$50B AI ad uplift at Meta and Google is larger than all AI-native apps and incumbent AI products combined (~$33B), and it reaches capex with no intermediary A.10
  5. Debt now funds as much as end customers do, and it goes where the equity is thinnest: SPVs, neoclouds and Oracle A.2

Key insight

Circular money is real but not fake demand. We map 20 vendor-financing loops (suppliers investing in customers who then buy from them). About 40–60% of 2026 lab equity came from the labs' own suppliers and partners, depending on the definition A, and ~$0.8T of the $2.46T cloud backlog sits in contracts where the seller also invested in the buyer.11 The loops don't fake demand, since end-customer revenue is real and growing ~3× a year. But they transmit shocks: one lab disappointment would hit several suppliers' books in the same quarter.

06 · Follow one dollarFollow one dollar down the stack

Two dollars tell the whole story. The first is a dollar a customer pays for an AI coding agent: watch it shrink as each layer takes its cut. The second is a dollar of hyperscaler capex: watch how much of it turns into supplier profit.

So why does Nvidia earn ~$240B while end-customer AI money is only ~$200B? Because in 2026 the chip layer is not paid out of customers' dollars. It is paid out of capex bought ahead of demand A.11 The picture above is where money will settle once the build-out matures. The cash view is where it lands today:

P&L view: Nvidia profit per $1 of coding-agent spend~6¢A steady state
Cash view: capex per $1 of end-customer AI money, 2026E~$5A 12× in 2024
Cash view: paid to Nvidia per $1 of end-customer AI money~$1.75A 35% of ~$5
What a loss-making lab spends per $1 of revenue~$1.58A compute $1.17 + people $0.41

A first-party agent (Claude Code, Codex) skips the app step, so the lab keeps ~55–60¢ of gross profit and ~40–45¢ goes to compute (Anthropic's Q2 2026 compute was ~56¢ per revenue dollar) A.9 That is why labs are moving up the stack, and why Cursor sold itself to a compute owner (SpaceX, $60B).10 In cash terms a loss-making lab spends ~$1.58 per $1 of revenue: ~$0.81 on rented compute, ~$0.36 on its own data centers and ~$0.41 on people. The extra ~$0.58 comes from equity, about 45% of it from the lab's own suppliers. For such a lab, a dollar of agent revenue pulls more than a dollar of investor money down the stack with it A.11

$1 of hyperscaler capex: about half becomes supplier profit

Now start from the other end. Take $1 of AI capex and follow it to its suppliers. ~51¢ becomes some supplier's operating profit: Nvidia ~23¢, the memory makers ~15¢, TSMC ~3.5¢, Broadcom/AMD/Marvell ~3¢. The other ~49¢ pays wages, materials and components A.11 No previous infrastructure build (railways, electrification, telecom fiber, cloud 1.0) has had a supplier layer this profitable; in the 2000 telecom build, equipment vendors ran operating margins of roughly 10–25% conf: med.

Over time, that dollar becomes a depreciation charge of ~12.8¢ a year for the buyer (two-thirds over ~6 years, one-third over ~20). To earn a 10% return on it, the buyer needs ~$0.35–0.45 of annual revenue at a ~50–60% margin on that capacity A.1 And the ~18¢ that TSMC and the memory makers earn per capex dollar gets reinvested in new fabs (TSMC capex $60–64B in 2026 G; memory makers ~$110–130B), which pays ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, KLA and TEL: a second capex wave upstream.6

Two more dollars worth following

  • $1 of AI-driven ad revenue at Meta. Meta keeps ~31–40¢ of operating margin, and the whole dollar is available to fund its own $130–145B of capex, with no lab or cloud in between A.10 That is why the ad giants can outspend everyone.
  • $1 of vendor equity (for example Amazon → OpenAI). Amazon's $50B commitment to OpenAI came paired with a $100B AWS commitment RE.2 Each $1 of equity "buys" ~$2 of future AWS backlog, which becomes ~$0.70–0.80 of Amazon capex, which flows ~51¢ to suppliers' profit. Meanwhile Amazon marks up its stakes at each new round (~$53B of gains on Anthropic in one quarter). The same dollar shows up as equity, backlog, capex, supplier revenue and a mark-to-market gain. That is what "circularity" means in practice.

07 · Who keeps the profitThe suppliers mint money; the labs burn it

Revenue double-counts along a value chain: TSMC's revenue sits inside Nvidia's costs, and Nvidia's inside the clouds' capex. Operating profit does not, so we add it up across layers to see who actually keeps the money.11 The positive AI operating-profit pool grew from ~$134B (2024) to ~$261B (2025) to ~$637B (2026E) A. Net of the labs' and apps' losses it is ~$589B.

Scarcity sets the price, and it moves. Compute silicon's share of the pool fell from ~62% (2024) to ~44% (2026E) as the semiconductor supply chain, led by memory, rose from ~19% to ~32% A.11 Nvidia's own share fell from ~55% to ~38% while the memory makers' rose from ~9% to ~23%. Nvidia itself flagged the transfer: memory costs will push its gross margin down ~300bp to a 71–72% trough G.5

The money minters and the money burners

The minters are Nvidia (~$240B), the memory makers (~$149B from AI buyers, far more in total), Broadcom (~$38B), TSMC (~$35B AI share), the hyperscalers' AI clouds (~$33B), and Meta and Google's AI-improved ads (~$25B) A.11 The burners are OpenAI (−$37B), xAI (−$10B), AI-native apps as a group (−$4B) and the neoclouds (−$1.5B at the operating line: Nebius lost $176M in Q2 alone, and interest deepens net losses). Oracle is profitable but burning cash R.8 Anthropic is the exception that matters: if its reported ~$0.56B Q2 operating profit on ~$11B of revenue holds up in its IPO filing, it shows a frontier lab can be profitable once revenue outgrows the next training budget conf: med.9

The memory anomaly

Memory makers' Q2 2026 operating profit was ~$134B in a single quarter, roughly double Nvidia's $63.7B A.6 Only ~$149B of their ~$540B full-year profit is paid directly by AI buyers. The rest is an AI-induced shortage rent paid by every buyer of phones, PCs and cars, because HBM absorbs ~18–22% of DRAM wafer starts to make ~8–9% of bits conf: low. Counted causally, memory, not Nvidia, is 2026's biggest profit-taker. It is also the least durable pool: memory margins have mean-reverted within 4–8 quarters of every previous peak.11

Why the suppliers win

Four forces decide who keeps the money A.11

  1. Scarcity at the chokepoint. Profit goes to whoever owns an input that cannot be expanded in less than 2–3 years: EUV lithography (ASML, ~65 tools a year), leading-edge wafers and CoWoS (TSMC), HBM and DRAM (three suppliers), indium-phosphide lasers, gas turbines (sold out to ~2030) and energized land. Buyers pay almost any price because a month of delay on 1 GW of GPUs costs ~$500M of idle depreciation.3
  2. Pricing power from integration and software. Nvidia's gross margin held at ~75% even as CoWoS supply tripled, because buyers pay for time-to-revenue, CUDA fungibility (a CUDA cluster can be re-rented to anyone, which lowers its financing cost) and rack-scale engineering.5
  3. Cost of capital. The same GPU earns 35–39% operating margins at AWS and Google Cloud and ~0% at CoreWeave. The difference is depreciation and a ~9% cost of debt, not gross margin. Balance-sheet strength is itself a profit pool.8
  4. Distance from the customer. Labs own the customer but not the scarce input, so they compete on price: token prices at constant capability fall ~10× a year or more.9

10 · The storyHow we got here: five acts, 2022 → 2026

The build-out happened in five acts. Each act is defined by where the scarcity sat, because that is where the money went.11

Act 1: The shock (Nov 2022 – 2023)

  1. 30 Nov 2022

    ChatGPT launches

    It reaches ~100 million users in about two months, the fastest adoption of any consumer product to that point. The demand shock begins.1

    Turning point
  2. May 2023

    Nvidia guides to ~$11B against ~$7B expected

    The market realizes the bottleneck is Nvidia's H100 and TSMC's CoWoS packaging (only ~13,000 wafers a month at end-2023). H100s rent for ~$8/hour and are rationed. Nvidia's data-center revenue goes from $15B (FY23) to $47.5B (FY24) R.568

    Turning point
  3. Mid-2023

    The lull before the spending

    Big-4 capex is only ~37–41% of operating cash flow: the trough of the cycle and the last moment of "capital-light" Big Tech.1 Who earned the rent: Nvidia and TSMC.

Act 2: The arms race (2024)

  1. 2024

    Big-4 capex rises ~55%

    Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta all say they would rather overbuild than underbuild.1

  2. Jun 2024

    Sequoia asks "AI's $600B question"

    How will customers ever pay for the GPUs being bought? The revenue-gap debate begins.12

    Turning point
  3. Sep 2024

    xAI's Colossus 1 comes online in Memphis

    Built in 122 days. Speed to power becomes a competitive weapon.1

  4. Late 2024

    Supply catches up with GPUs

    CoWoS triples to ~35–40k wafers a month and H100 rental prices fall toward $2–3 an hour. The bottleneck moves toward HBM memory and electrical equipment.68 Who earned the rent: Nvidia (gross margins 75–79%), SK hynix (the HBM leader) and TSMC.

Act 3: Power, mega-projects and circular deals (2025)

  1. 21 Jan 2025

    Stargate announced; DeepSeek R1 shock

    Stargate: $500B, 10 GW (OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle, MGX). The same month DeepSeek R1 shows frontier-quality models can be trained cheaply; Nvidia briefly loses ~$600B of market value, but demand keeps rising (cheaper AI leads to more AI use).9

    Turning point
  2. Jul 2025

    Power becomes the constraint

    PJM capacity auctions clear at ~$270/MW-day (2025/26) and then $329/MW-day (2026/27), against ~$29 two years earlier R. Gas turbines sell out to ~2029–30; transformers are backordered. Hyperscalers lock up nuclear output (Microsoft–Three Mile Island, Amazon–Talen, Meta–Clinton).3

  3. Sep–Oct 2025

    The circular-deal wave

    Oracle's backlog jumps to $455B on a ~$300B OpenAI contract. Nvidia signs a 10 GW letter of intent with OpenAI. AMD gives OpenAI warrants for up to 160M shares for 6 GW of GPU orders. Broadcom signs 10 GW of custom chips. Meta finances Hyperion through a $27.3B off-balance-sheet bond. Microsoft's OpenAI recapitalization adds $250B of Azure commitments.12

    Turning point
  4. Nov 2025

    Labs sign more than $1T of compute

    Anthropic–Microsoft–Nvidia ($30B of Azure plus up to $15B invested); OpenAI–AWS ($38B). OpenAI's commitments reach ~$1.4T. Michael Burry argues GPUs are being depreciated too slowly.28

  5. Q4 2025

    Memory prices start to explode

    HBM absorbs DRAM wafers; contract prices begin a rise of ~5× in a year.6 Who earned the rent: Nvidia, plus turbine makers, electrical-equipment makers, utilities and power producers, and memory makers.

Act 4: Agents arrive, memory bites, balance sheets stretch (H1 2026)

  1. Jan–Feb 2026

    Agents hit software; SpaceX acquires xAI

    Claude Cowork (12 Jan) and its plugins trigger a software-stock sell-off: ~$285B of market value erased on 3 Feb, and the SaaS index falls 26% in Q1.10 SpaceX acquires xAI (2 Feb).2

    Turning point
  2. Q1 2026

    DRAM contract prices nearly double in a quarter

    Microsoft says ~$25B of its 2026 capex plan is component price inflation.16

  3. 31 Mar 2026

    OpenAI closes $122B at $852B

    Amazon ($50B) and Nvidia ($30B) are among the investors; about two-thirds of the round comes from its own suppliers R.2

  4. Apr 2026

    Anthropic's run-rate passes OpenAI's

    $30B vs ~$25B E. Enterprise agents, led by Claude Code, overtake consumer chat as the growth engine.910

    Turning point
  5. May–Jun 2026

    Public markets reopen

    Anthropic raises $65B at $965B (28 May). Cerebras lists. SpaceX raises ~$75B in the largest IPO ever (12 Jun). Anthropic (1 Jun) and OpenAI (8 Jun) file confidentially for IPOs. Alphabet launches an equity programme of up to $90B, including $10B from Berkshire Hathaway, and suspends buybacks.2 Memory makers overtake TSMC on margin; Nvidia is still the biggest single earner.

Act 5: Peak supplier profits meet physical and political limits (Jul–Sep 2026)

  1. Jul 2026

    The build-out stops paying for itself

    Big-4 capex reaches 96% of operating cash flow; Alphabet posts its first negative free-cash-flow quarter; S&P cuts Oracle to BBB- (9 Jul). New York enacts what W01 reports as the first state data-center moratorium reported; re-verify.12

    Turning point
  2. Aug 2026

    Record supplier profits

    Nvidia reports a $96.2B quarter (26 Aug) R, shortly after Micron's June guide to an ~86% gross margin. Cursor is sold to SpaceX for $60B (closed 14 Aug): the app and model layers are merging.5610

  3. Sep 2026

    Revenue catches up, and so does friction

    Anthropic's run-rate is reported above $100B (18 Sep; unverified conf: low; $65B at end-July is confirmed). Loudoun County, the world's biggest data-center market, pauses new applications (18 Sep). Texas halts new data-center permits pending a grid audit (23 Sep). Oracle issues a force-majeure notice on the Project Jupiter Stargate campus after a gas pipeline is rejected (24 Sep).19 Who is earning the rent: Nvidia, the memory makers and the owners of powered, permitted land.

11 · BottlenecksWhat limits the build-out now

Scarcity moves down the physical stack, from chip to package to memory to power to land and permits, and each new chokepoint takes margin from the one before it A.11 The end-state is that scarcity reaches the one input no supplier can manufacture: willingness to pay. At that point the profit pool shifts toward whoever owns customers and cash flow.

Who can stop whom

No EUV → no 3nm/2nm chips → no GPU. No CoWoS → no package. No HBM → no GPU. No transformer or turbine → no energized hall. No permit → no site. No lab revenue → no take-or-pay renewal. The first four are engineering problems with known fixes and dates. The last two are political and commercial, with no fixed dates A.11 Physical fixes are coming: CoWoS roughly doubles in 2026 to ~120–130k wafers a month E, new memory fabs land in 2027–28, and turbine output rises from ~20 to ~30 GW a year by ~2030.36

12 · The big questionIs the build-out ahead of demand?

End-customer AI money is real and compounding at ~3× a year: ~$24B (2024) → ~$70B (2025) → ~$200B (2026E), with an exit-2026 run-rate of ~$280B A.11 Capex grows ~1.9× a year, so the ratio of capex to customer money roughly halves every 18 months: 12× → 8× → 5×.

The "$600B question", updated

In June 2024 Sequoia took Nvidia's data-center run-rate, doubled it (GPUs are about half a data center's cost) and doubled it again (end users need a ~50% margin): $600B of required revenue against ~$100B identified.12 On that method the 2026 requirement is ~$1.6T against a ~$280B run-rate, a hole 2.6× larger. But coverage is unchanged at about one-sixth: customer revenue has grown as fast as Nvidia's A.11 The method is harsh, because it asks one year of revenue to repay one year of GPUs that last 5–6 years.

Our preferred test uses depreciation. Capex already spent or committed through 2026 (~$1.84T) generates ~$257B a year of depreciation, ~$70B of operating costs and needs ~$150B of return on capital. Grossed up for lab and app margins and net of capacity that serves non-generative workloads, that requires ~$600B a year of end-customer AI revenue A. Against the ~$280B exit run-rate, the gap is ~$320B a year (~2.1×), rising to ~$1.0T a year by 2029 once 2027 capex is included.11

  • Base case (50%). Agents spread from coding into customer support, legal, finance and back-office work. End-customer AI money roughly doubles in 2027 and reaches ~$650B in 2028, when it catches up with what the existing build requires. Capex plateaus around $1.5T a year, held back as much by power and permits as by money A.11
  • Bear case (30%). Revenue growth slows in 2027 (coding saturates, prices fall faster than usage grows), a lab cannot fund its commitments, and 2028 capex drops ~25%. First to suffer: the memory makers, the neoclouds, Oracle and the lenders behind them.
  • Bull case (20%). Agents start taking over labor budgets, and AI revenue passes $2T a year by 2030 (Bain's test).13

Are agents where the fresh money enters?

Right about the engine, wrong about the fuel tank. Agentic usage, mostly coding, is ~45% of 2026's ~$130B of direct spend and ~55–60% of its growth conf: low. But end-customer money is less than a fifth of what flows into the build-out, it lands mostly at labs and incumbents rather than an independent app layer, and advertising remains the largest profitable AI business A.11 The thesis becomes fully true only if agents break out of coding into labor budgets by 2027–28, which is the single variable that decides between the base and bear cases.

Bull case

Customers catch up by 2028, and the build was worth it

  • End-customer money is growing ~3× a year and capacity is sold out; H100s rent 12% higher than a year ago.
  • The funding comes mainly from the most profitable companies on earth, not from fragile start-ups.
  • Agents are moving from coding into support, legal and finance, where labor budgets dwarf IT budgets.
  • Anthropic's reported first operating profit shows a frontier lab can earn money once revenue outgrows training.

Bear case

The bill arrives before the customers do

  • $1T of capex against ~$200B of customer money; Big-4 free cash flow is shrinking and debt fills the gap.
  • Two labs owe ~43% of a $2.46T backlog on take-or-pay terms, while their revenue is month-to-month.
  • Circular vendor money makes one lab's stumble hit Nvidia, Amazon, Oracle and SoftBank at once.
  • Depreciation doubles by 2027, and memory margins have always mean-reverted within 4–8 quarters.

15 · ChinaThe other build-out (Track 2)

This page covers the Western (non-China) build-out: the US, Europe, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, the Gulf and the rest of the non-China world. Chinese buyers and suppliers belong to Track 2, which uses the same layer IDs with a C prefix (C00–C11) so the two can be compared line by line.11

Our hypotheses to test in Track 2

  • Fewer dollars, more compute per dollar. China spends roughly an order of magnitude less but gets more compute per dollar, thanks to cheaper power and construction.
  • A flatter profit pool. No ~75%-margin chip monopolist (Huawei Ascend, Cambricon, Hygon and in-house chips instead of Nvidia), and open-weight price wars at the model layer.
  • A different bottleneck. China's binding constraint is chips (EUV, HBM, advanced packaging), not power: it adds several times US generating capacity each year.

17 · GlossaryThe 40 essential terms

$600B question
Sequoia's 2024 test of how much end-customer revenue AI needs to justify GPU spending. Updated in W11: ~$1.6T on Sequoia's method; ~$600B a year on a depreciation basis.
agent
An AI system given a goal that plans, uses tools and iterates on its own (e.g. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor's agent). Uses 4–15× more tokens than a chat.
AI build-out
The global investment program in data centers, chips, networking and power to train and run AI models.
ASIC
Also called XPU: a custom chip designed for one customer's AI workloads (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Meta MTIA).
behind-the-meter
Electricity generated on site (gas turbines, fuel cells) rather than taken from the grid.
capacity market
An auction (e.g. PJM) in which utilities pay power plants to be available at peak times. AI load has driven its prices up more than 11×.
capex
Capital expenditure: spending on long-lasting assets (servers, buildings), recorded as an asset and depreciated over years.
colocation
Renting space and power in someone else's data center (a wholesale lease), priced per kW per month.
copilot
An AI assistant embedded in an existing workflow that suggests while a human decides (GitHub Copilot, M365 Copilot).
CoWoS
TSMC's advanced-packaging process that joins GPU dies and HBM on one substrate. The key bottleneck in 2023–24.
data center
A building housing computers, with its own electrical, cooling and network infrastructure.
depreciation
Spreading an asset's cost over its useful life (servers 5–6 years, buildings 15–25). Longer lives mean higher reported profits today.
DRAM / NAND
The two main memory types: DRAM (working memory) and NAND (flash storage, SSDs).
EUV
Extreme-ultraviolet lithography: the machines, made only by ASML, that print the finest chip features.
FCF
Free cash flow: operating cash flow minus capex. Negative at Alphabet (Q2 2026), Amazon (TTM) and Oracle.
foundry
A factory that manufactures chips designed by others; TSMC is the dominant one.
GPU
Graphics processing unit: a chip with thousands of parallel arithmetic units. Nvidia's GPUs are the workhorse of AI.
GW / MW
Gigawatt / megawatt of power. A 1 GW AI campus draws roughly as much as a large nuclear reactor produces. AI capacity is now counted in GW.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: stacked DRAM placed next to the GPU. The largest single cost item in an AI chip and the scarcest in 2026.
hyperscaler
A very large cloud or platform company: Microsoft, Alphabet/Google, Amazon, Meta, Oracle.
model lab
A company that trains and sells large AI models (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind).
neocloud
A GPU-specialist cloud such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe or Nscale.
NVL72
A rack of servers. Nvidia's NVL72 links 72 GPUs into one giant GPU.
ODM / OEM
Contract manufacturers (Foxconn, Quanta) vs branded server makers (Dell, Supermicro) that assemble racks.
open-weight model
A model whose parameters are published and can be run by anyone (Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral).
optics
Laser-based transceiver modules that turn electrical signals into light for fiber links.
powered land
A site with a grid connection or on-site generation already secured. The scarce real estate of AI.
PPA
Power purchase agreement: a long-term contract to buy electricity from a specific plant.
private credit
Loans made by non-bank funds (Blue Owl, Apollo, Blackstone) rather than banks or public bond markets.
profit pool
The operating profit earned at each layer of a value chain. It shows where the money is actually kept.
PUE
Power usage effectiveness: total facility power ÷ IT power; ~1.1–1.3 for modern AI halls.
reasoning model
A model that 'thinks' (generates hidden intermediate tokens) before answering, which multiplies tokens per task.
RPO
Remaining performance obligations, or backlog: revenue under contract but not yet recognized. $2.46T at the five biggest AI clouds.
run-rate
Also ARR: the latest month (or quarter) of revenue annualized. Not contracted, and it can fall as fast as it rose.
scale-up vs scale-out
Links inside a rack (NVLink) vs links between racks (InfiniBand or Ethernet).
SPV
Special-purpose vehicle: a separate company that owns and finances a data center off the tenant's balance sheet (e.g. Meta's Hyperion).
take-or-pay
The customer pays for committed capacity whether it uses it or not.
token
A chunk of text (~¾ of a word) or other data: the unit in which AI output is produced and priced ($ per million tokens).
training vs inference
Building a model (a one-time, huge compute job) vs running it to answer requests (continuous, and growing with usage).
vendor financing
Also 'circular financing': a supplier investing in or lending to a customer who then buys from that supplier (Nvidia → OpenAI, Amazon → Anthropic).

18 · ConfidenceWhat we are least sure about

Almost every cross-layer number on this page is an Atlas estimate A, because no company reports "AI profit". Treat the layer totals as order-of-magnitude correct and directionally reliable, not precise: confidence is medium for compute silicon and the semiconductor supply chain, low elsewhere.11

  • Anthropic's September run-rate. A report that it passed $100B (18 Sep 2026) could not be verified conf: low. This page uses the confirmed $65B (end-July). If the true figure is ~$65–80B, lab 2026 revenue falls ~$4–7B (Anthropic toward $56B), direct end-customer spend to ~$123–126B and the exit run-rate to ~$255–265B. The broad conclusions do not change.
  • Memory attribution. The split of the ~$540B memory windfall between AI-direct (~$149B) and AI-induced (~$390B) is our judgement conf: low; buy-side and supply-side data are hard to reconcile.
  • Big Tech's legacy cash (~$547B) is a residual: any error in capex, AI revenue or external financing lands here. Its range is ~$480–600B.
  • Hyperscaler AI-cloud profit (~$33B) is below W08's $40–60B; no hyperscaler discloses AI gross margin.
  • The ~$50B AI ad uplift rests on attributing ~40% of Meta's post-2023 revenue growth to AI ranking conf: low.
  • Labs' 2026 results (OpenAI −$37B, Anthropic ~+$3.5B, xAI −$10B) extrapolate from one or two quarters of leaked data.
  • The OpenAI + Anthropic share of backlog (~43%) rests on one hard data point (OpenAI ~45% of Microsoft's RPO, Dec 2025) conf: low–med.
  • Scenario probabilities are judgement, not models; 2024 profit pools for W03, W04 and W07 are rough back-casts.
  • Fact-checking. The W01–W05 pass could not reach its web sources and relied on knowledge and arithmetic; the W06–W10 pass fetched ~60 sources and confirmed most of 2026's extraordinary claims: SpaceX absorbing xAI, the ~$75B SpaceX IPO, the $60B Cursor sale, Anthropic's $965B valuation and $65B run-rate, OpenAI's $852B round. Neither found fabrications. New York's data-center moratorium is reported by W01 and flagged for re-verification.

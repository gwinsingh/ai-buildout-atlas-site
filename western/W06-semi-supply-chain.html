The 60-second version

  1. The AI build-out runs through one company in one place. TSMC took 72.5% of global foundry revenue in 2Q26 E and makes essentially all leading-edge AI accelerator logic, in Taiwan. It earned $40.2bn that quarter at a record 67.7% gross margin R1.
  2. Packaging, not transistors, is the tightest bottleneck. Every AI GPU needs one CoWoS package. TSMC's CoWoS capacity went from ~13k wafers a month at end-2023 to ~72.5k at end-2025 and is heading to ~120–130k by end-2026 E1210. Nvidia holds ~60% of it 13.
  3. Memory is in the biggest boom in its history. HBM uses about 3× the wafer area per bit of ordinary DRAM, so it starves everything else. DRAM industry revenue nearly tripled in two quarters, from $53.6bn (4Q25) to $154.7bn (2Q26) E3533, and Micron's gross margin (84.9%) is now above TSMC's R47.
  4. Nvidia keeps most of the value. Of the ~$35k a hyperscaler pays for a B200-class GPU, we estimate ~$5.45k is manufacturing cost. Nvidia keeps ~84% of the price as chip-level gross profit, about 88–89% of the gross profit earned along the whole chain A.
  5. The toolmakers are at records too. ASML raised its 2026 guide to €43–45bn G50, and global equipment billings hit $40.5bn in 2Q26, up 23% R56. The US is re-industrialising with equity: it owns ~10% of Intel, and TSMC has committed $265bn to Arizona R2318.
  6. Watch TSMC's 3Q26 results and 2027 capex (~15–16 Oct), whether DRAM contract-price increases keep slowing (13–18% in 3Q26), and who wins HBM4 at Nvidia.

01 · What this layer isWho actually makes an AI chip

An AI chip like Nvidia's Blackwell is not made by Nvidia. Nvidia designs it; a chain of specialists in Taiwan, Korea, Japan, the Netherlands and the US turns that design into a physical part.

Nvidia designs the chip with software rented from Synopsys or Cadence (EDA tools), using building blocks licensed from Arm and others. It sends the design to TSMC, which prints the logic dies on silicon wafers in Taiwan using machines from ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, KLA and Tokyo Electron. TSMC then places the logic dies next to stacks of HBM memory bought from SK hynix, Samsung or Micron, on a silicon interposer, using its CoWoS packaging lines. The result sits on a plastic-and-copper substrate from Ibiden or Unimicron and is tested on Advantest testers. Only then does it go to Foxconn or Quanta to be built into a server (W07).

Analogy

Think of Nvidia as a star architect. TSMC is the only construction company in the world that can pour the skyscraper's foundations to spec. ASML makes the only cranes tall enough, and the HBM makers supply the elevators. The architect earns most of the money, but no building goes up if any one of those suppliers stops.

Why doesn't Nvidia just build its own factory?

A leading-edge fab now costs $20–30bn or more, takes 2–3 years to build, and only pays off if it runs full for years on many customers' chips conf: med. TSMC spreads that cost over Apple, AMD, Broadcom, Qualcomm and Nvidia, and it has 30+ years of process know-how that cannot be bought.

Intel and Samsung are the only other companies still trying to make chips at the leading edge, and both are losing money on it (section 06). Designing chips and leaving manufacturing to a foundry ("fabless") has been the winning model since the 1990s.

Definition

Semiconductor supply chain: everything upstream of the chip designers in W05. Foundries (TSMC, Samsung, Intel, Rapidus), advanced packaging (CoWoS, SoIC, EMIB), memory (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron), wafer-fab and back-end equipment (ASML, Applied, Lam, KLA, TEL, Advantest, BESI, Disco), EDA and IP (Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens EDA, Arm) and substrates and outsourced assembly (Ibiden, Unimicron, ASE, Amkor). Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Netherlands count as part of the Western-aligned chain. Chinese-owned suppliers are marked CN and covered in C06.

Why this layer matters for the money map

  • It is where AI capex turns into physical capacity. Hyperscaler dollars (W01) flow to Nvidia (W05); Nvidia pays TSMC, SK hynix and the others; TSMC and the memory makers pay the equipment makers.
  • It holds the hardest bottlenecks: CoWoS, HBM and EUV tools. A bigger cheque this quarter does not fix them, because fabs take 2–3 years to build and EUV tools 12–18 months to deliver.
  • It is the most geographically concentrated layer. Leading-edge logic is ~90%+ in Taiwan. HBM is almost all in Korea plus Micron (Taiwan, Japan, US). EUV comes only from the Netherlands. This is where export controls, tariffs and Taiwan risk bite hardest.

03 · Inside the packageCoWoS and HBM: why packaging became the bottleneck

AI chips are now too big to fit on one piece of silicon. The reticle limit, the largest area a lithography tool can print in one shot, is ~858 mm², and a B200 uses two dies of ~800 mm² each. They also need memory millimetres away. Advanced packaging solves both: it joins several dies on one base with far denser wiring than a circuit board.

Why packaging is the chokepoint

CoWoS needs (a) silicon interposers and bridges made in TSMC's own fabs, (b) specialised bonding, moulding and test tools from Disco, BESI, ASMPT and Advantest, (c) large ABF substrates and (d) HBM arriving on time. Every Nvidia GPU uses one CoWoS package, so CoWoS capacity roughly caps accelerator output. Section 07 has the numbers.

HBM, generation by generation

HBM is DRAM stacked 8, 12 or 16 high and wired with TSVs. It sits millimetres from the GPU and moves data over a very wide bus, giving each stack about 1–2+ TB/s of bandwidth. Each new generation doubles something: the bus width (HBM4), the stack height or the pin speed.

Why HBM4 matters to TSMC

From HBM4 on, the base die at the bottom of each stack moves from a DRAM process to a logic process. SK hynix and Micron buy those base dies from TSMC, so TSMC now earns a slice of every HBM4 stack. Samsung makes its own on its 4nm foundry process, one reason Samsung Foundry's utilisation is recovering in 2H26 E27. It also opens up "custom HBM": Google or Nvidia can put their own logic into the base die, moving value toward TSMC and the chip designers.

Why HBM is a chokepoint

  1. Only three suppliers: SK hynix, Samsung and Micron. CN CXMT is trying and is ~2–3 generations behind (see C06).
  2. Wafer hunger. HBM uses ~3× the wafer area per bit of standard DRAM, because of the larger die, TSV area and stack yield loss. It was ~18% of DRAM wafer input at end-2025 but only ~8% of bits E40.
  3. Qualification. Each generation must pass Nvidia's validation. SK hynix led HBM3E. For HBM4, TrendForce expected all three suppliers to be validated for Rubin by 2Q26, with Samsung possibly first E44.
  4. Stacking tools. Thermo-compression (TC) bonders come from Hanmi, Hanwha Semitech, ASMPT and SEMES; hybrid bonders from BESI and others. BESI (Netherlands) leads hybrid bonding, with Applied Materials as partner and ~9% shareholder conf: med.

04 · Process nodesSmaller, costlier, and only one company at scale

A node name such as "3nm" is now a marketing label, not a physical size. What matters is transistor density, speed and power per generation. Each step costs more to develop and to build: a leading-edge fab now costs $20–30bn+ conf: med, and only TSMC currently earns enough from it to keep going. Samsung (SF2) and Intel (18A/14A) are the only other attempts, and their yields and customer lists lag.

06 · The foundriesTSMC, and the two companies trying to catch it

TSMC is a pure-play foundry: it makes chips designed by others and sells no products of its own. In 2Q26 it took 72.5% of all foundry revenue E19 and ~90%+ of leading-edge (7nm and below) foundry revenue conf: med.

Revenue and margins, quarter by quarter

TSMC's revenue more than doubled in nine quarters, from $18.9bn in 1Q24 to $40.2bn in 2Q26 R1, and it guides 3Q26 to $44.6–45.8bn G1. Full-year 2025 was $122bn (+35.9%) R8; 2026 is guided to grow "slightly above 40%" in dollars, i.e. ~$171–175bn G3 (canonical ~$173bn).

Why the margin jumped from ~53% to ~68%

  1. Price increases on N5/N4 and below for 2026 E20, with N3 at full utilisation.
  2. A mix shift to high-performance computing (HPC).
  3. Higher CoWoS pricing.
  4. Operating leverage on a mostly fixed depreciation base.

Offsets: the N2 ramp dilutes 3–4 points in 2H26, and overseas fabs (Arizona, Japan, Germany) dilute 2–3 points, widening to 3–4 points G3. The long-term "through-cycle" gross-margin target is still ≥56% G8, so current margins are well above plan.

Two-thirds of TSMC is now AI and HPC

HPC (AI accelerators, CPUs and networking chips) was 66% of 2Q26 revenue, up 20% on the quarter, against 58% for full-year 2025 R28. AI accelerators alone were a "high-teens %" of 2025 revenue, roughly $20–23bn A (122 × 17–19%). Management guides the 2024–29 AI-accelerator revenue CAGR at "mid-to-high 50s %" and in July 2026 called the trend "stronger and stronger" G38. Overall 2024–29 revenue CAGR is guided at ~25% G8.

A narrower number in circulation

A widely shared Morgan Stanley note (June 2026) puts TSMC "AI revenue" at $27.1bn (2026) → $86.3bn (2027) E14. Its definition appears narrower than TSMC's conf: low, so we do not use it for sizing.

Monthly revenue is still accelerating

July 2026 revenue was NT$467.58bn (+44.7% YoY) R6 and August a record NT$514.81bn (+53.3% YoY, +10.1% MoM) R5. January–August totalled NT$3,386.87bn (+39.3%) 5. The 2Q26 monthly average was ~NT$423bn A, so July–August are running ~16% above it, consistent with the 3Q guide.

Capex: the arms race is still accelerating

TSMC's 2026 capex guide rose from $52–56bn in January to $60–64bn in July G38, against $40.9bn in 2025 R8, with some reports putting the new top end at $64bn 4. 2Q26 capex alone was $15.7bn 3. Management says capex over the next three years will be "even more significantly higher than the past three years" G3. The split: 70–80% advanced process, ~10% specialty, 10–20% packaging, test and masks 3. Capex is heading to ~0.36 of revenue in 2026 A (62 ÷ 173).

Pricing, capacity and the balance sheet

  • Node pricing. N3 ~$20k per wafer E13; N2 ~$30k E15; A16 rumoured up to $45k E16 conf: low. TSMC raised prices on all nodes at 5/4nm and below for 2026 E20. N2 and A16 are booked "well into 2028" E13.
  • Capacity. 2nm ~90k wpm end-2026 → ~110k mid-2027; 3nm 180k+ → ~210k E10. 2Q26 wafer shipments were 4.34m 12-inch equivalents (+16.6% YoY) R2.
  • Balance sheet, 2Q26. Operating cash flow NT$783bn (~$24.5bn) in the quarter; cash ~NT$3.5tn (~$110bn); 2026 dividend NT$24/share (+33%) R3. Return on equity 45.9% 2.
  • Moat. Process leadership (N3/N2 yields), CoWoS, an ecosystem of EDA and IP partners (OIP), customer trust as a pure play that never competes with its customers, and scale that lets it outspend the next two rivals combined.
  • Risks. Taiwan geopolitics; customer concentration (Nvidia ~20%+ of revenue, Apple ~20% conf: med); US tariffs on Taiwanese chips; margin dilution from overseas fabs; a single-customer (Nvidia) shock.

08 · MemoryThe biggest memory boom in history

AI did not only create demand for HBM. By diverting wafers to HBM, it created a shortage of ordinary DRAM and NAND too, and prices for all of them exploded.

Conventional DRAM contract prices rose 45–50% QoQ in 4Q25, ~93–98% in 1Q26 and a forecast 58–63% in 2Q26 E353437, with another 13–18% forecast for 3Q26, server DRAM included 3839. DRAM industry revenue went from $53.6bn (4Q25) to ~$97bn (1Q26) and $154.7bn (2Q26) E353433.

By product, TrendForce's February forecast for 1Q26 had server DRAM ~+90%, PC DRAM >+100%, LPDDR4X/5X ~+90%, NAND +55–60% and enterprise SSD +53–58% E36. NAND was forecast at +70–75% for 2Q26 37 and +10–15% for 3Q26 38. Across 2026, enterprise SSD prices are forecast to rise ~235% and server DRAM ~270% cumulatively E42.

Why HBM causes the shortage

An HBM bit uses about 3× the wafer area of a standard DRAM bit. HBM took ~18% of DRAM wafer starts at end-2025 and is heading to ~22% (end-2026) and ~30% (end-2027), yet only ~8–13% of bits E40. That crowds out DDR5 and LPDDR and pushes PC, phone and server prices up.

Two numbers on memory's share of cloud capex

TrendForce forecasts memory at 47% of big-cloud (CSP) capex in 2026 and 68% in 2027 E42. At ~$830bn of top-nine CSP capex 71, 47% implies ~$390bn, which looks high conf: low. The Atlas canonical estimate is that memory is ~21% of AI capex in 2026 (~15% bought directly plus ~6% as HBM inside accelerators) A; see W11. Treat the TrendForce figure as directional.

Who sells the DRAM

SK hynix's share fell in 1H26 even as its profits soared (32.1% → 24.9%). It has the highest HBM mix, and HBM contracts were priced a year in advance, so HBM did not reprice as fast as commodity DDR5. In 1Q26 DDR5 64GB RDIMM overtook HBM in revenue per wafer E40, which was unheard of. Samsung, with more commodity exposure, captured more of the spike.

NAND is riding the same wave: the top five NAND brands grew revenue 77% in 2Q26 to $68.87bn, and Micron moved up to third place E45.

Key insight

For the first time, commodity memory makers are out-earning TSMC on margin. This is a shortage rent, not a structural moat: memory has three suppliers, capacity takes 2–3 years to add, and HBM absorbs wafers. In every past cycle (1995, 2000, 2010, 2018, 2022) memory margins fell back within 4–8 quarters once capacity caught up. What is different this time: multi-year agreements with prepayments (Micron's ~$100bn committed and ~$22bn of deposits R47), customised HBM4E base dies, and HBM's ~3× wafer penalty. Together these may stretch the cycle (bull) or produce a bigger overbuild in 2028 (bear).

DRAM makers' through-cycle operating margins historically averaged roughly 20–35% conf: med. The Atlas estimates memory makers' share of the whole AI profit pool at ~23% in 2026, up from 9% in 2024, while Nvidia's is ~38% A (see W11).

17 · Competitive dynamicsNine contests that will reshape the layer

  1. TSMC vs Intel vs Samsung in foundry. TSMC's share rose to 72.5% in 2Q26 E19. Intel's 18A is yielding "ahead of expectations" R21 but external revenue is only $293m a quarter. The 14A volume decision (2028) and a named anchor customer (Tesla; Apple reported) are the swing factors. Samsung SF2 yields ~60% E27, with Tesla, Google I/O dies and Broadcom as wins. US policy (tariffs, the CHIPS equity stake, pressure on US designers to "buy American") is the main lever that could lift Intel's share.
  2. CoWoS alternatives. Intel EMIB-T (Google and AWS evaluating; 15–20k/month in 2027 → 40–45k in 2028) E10; OSAT CoWoS-like capacity at ASE/SPIL and Amkor, +50–60k wpm in 2026 11; panel-level packaging (CoPoS, FOPLP) from 2028 1164.
  3. The HBM share war. Samsung has regained #1 DRAM revenue and was expected to qualify HBM4 at Nvidia first E4433. SK hynix is defending with the highest HBM mix and agreements with ~10 customers R31. Micron is ramping HBM4 fastest (2× the speed of HBM3E) 47. Custom HBM (logic base dies from TSMC) moves value toward TSMC and the ASIC designers.
  4. Hybrid bonding vs TC bonding for 16-high and taller HBM: BESI and Applied against Hanmi, Hanwha, ASMPT and SEMES. JEDEC's relaxed 775 µm HBM4 height delayed the switch conf: med; HBM4E/HBM5 at 16–20 high is the likely switch point.
  5. Backside power and GAA (TSMC A16, Intel 18A PowerVia). Whoever makes backside power work first at high yield gains an edge in AI performance per watt.
  6. Arm becomes a chip vendor (March 2026) 62, risking conflict with licensees (Qualcomm litigation history, Nvidia, the hyperscalers). RISC-V (SiFive, Tenstorrent, CN Chinese vendors) is a long-run threat to Arm royalties.
  7. AI for EDA. Synopsys (agentic AI with TSMC, Sept 2026) and Cadence ("AI Super Agents") argue AI raises EDA's value per engineer. The bear case: AI coding agents commoditise parts of the flow.
  8. China localisation. CN SMIC is #3 foundry (5.4%) E19; CXMT (DRAM, entering HBM), YMTC (NAND) and domestic toolmakers (Naura, AMEC, SMEE) are all advancing. China's share of Western equipment revenue is falling: Applied 35% → 28% YoY R53; Lam 26% 54.
  9. Mature-node price rises are spilling over from AI (power ICs, MLCCs), and foundries are cutting mature-node output E80.

18 · Bull vs bearA durable build-out, or a cyclical peak

Bull case

The build-out keeps compounding through 2028–30

  • Demand is visible for years. C.C. Wei sees strong demand "probably 2029, 2030" 3. CSP capex +98% in 2026 and +50% in 2027 E42, and AI server shipments are forecast up ~31% in 2026 77. Micron has ~$100bn of committed minimum revenue through 2030 R47.
  • Pricing power along the whole chain. TSMC raised prices on all ≤5nm nodes for 2026 20; HBM prices could rise 70–140% in 2027 42; ASML's guide is up ~25% in six months.
  • A structural memory shift. Agentic AI inference needs huge KV-cache memory, SSDs for vector databases and more CPUs, broadening demand beyond training GPUs E4170.
  • Discipline plus prepayments. Multi-year agreements and customer deposits reduce the risk of a classic memory crash, and the US government acts as a backstop investor (Intel).
  • Equipment intensity rises with each node (GAA, backside power, High-NA, hybrid bonding), so equipment spend grows faster than wafer output.

Bear case

A cyclical peak dressed up as a new paradigm

  • 80%+ memory gross margins are a historic outlier. Every prior supercycle ended in overbuild. Micron (ID1/ID2, New York), SK hynix (Yongin, Ohio?), Samsung (P4/P5) and CXMT all add capacity for 2027–28. NAND is already seen easing in 2H27 E46.
  • Customers are hitting affordability limits. Cloud providers have negative free cash flow 46; memory at 47–68% of their capex 42 cannot last. Nvidia is cutting HBM configurations 43; PCs (−9% to −14%) and phones are squeezed by memory costs.
  • Concentration risk. Taiwan geopolitics, one customer (Nvidia ~60% of CoWoS) and one EUV supplier.
  • Margin dilution at TSMC from N2 (3–4 points) and overseas fabs (3–4 points) G3, with capex/revenue heading to ~0.36 A.
  • Policy whiplash and valuation. Section 232 tariffs; export-control reversals (H20 ban → 15% share → H200 25% fee); possible US pressure on TSMC. Micron at a $1tn market cap and the Intel stake up ~5× price in a lot of good news.

Our judgment

The logic, foundry and CoWoS chokepoint (TSMC, ASML) is structural: returns stay high for as long as AI capex grows. The memory windfall is a cyclical peak on a structurally higher base. We expect 2027 to hold up on HBM4E and inference demand, with the main risk of margin normalisation in 2028 conf: med.

22 · GlossaryTerms on this page

ABF
Ajinomoto Build-up Film: insulating film used to build the fine-wiring layers of chip package substrates.
Advanced packaging
Joining several dies (logic, memory) in one package with very dense connections: 2.5D side by side, 3D stacked.
A16 / A14
TSMC's 1.6nm-class and 1.4nm-class nodes (A = ångström).
ATE
Automated test equipment (Advantest, Teradyne).
Backside power
Routing power through the back of the wafer (TSMC Super Power Rail, Intel PowerVia), freeing the front for signals.
Base die
The bottom die of an HBM stack; from HBM4 it is made on a logic process, often by TSMC.
CHIPS Act
The 2022 US law with $39bn of manufacturing incentives and a 25% (now 35%) investment tax credit.
CoPoS
Chip-on-Panel-on-Substrate: TSMC's panel-based successor to CoWoS.
CoWoS (-S / -L / -R)
Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate: TSMC's 2.5D packaging using a silicon, bridge (LSI) or organic (RDL) interposer.
CSP
Cloud service provider (hyperscaler).
DUV / EUV / High-NA EUV
Deep-UV (193 nm) lithography; extreme-UV (13.5 nm); EUV with a 0.55 numerical aperture for finer features.
EDA
Electronic design automation: the software used to design chips.
EMIB(-T)
Intel's Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge (T = with through-silicon vias).
Foundry
A company that manufactures chips designed by others.
GAA / nanosheet
Gate-all-around transistors, where the gate wraps the channel on all sides (TSMC N2, Intel 18A, Samsung SF2).
Gross margin (GM)
(Revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue.
HBM (HBM3E / HBM4 / HBM4E)
High Bandwidth Memory: stacked DRAM with TSVs, placed next to the processor.
HPC
High-performance computing: TSMC's platform for AI accelerators, CPUs and networking chips.
Hybrid bonding
Direct copper-to-copper and oxide bonding of dies with no solder bumps, allowing pitches under 10 µm.
Interposer
A silicon or organic slab with dense wiring that connects dies side by side.
KV cache
Memory holding attention keys and values during LLM inference; it drives memory demand for agentic AI.
LTA / SCA
Long-term (strategic customer) agreement fixing supply and price over several years, sometimes with prepayments.
Node (N3, N2)
A process-technology generation; the names are marketing labels.
OSAT
Outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (ASE, Amkor).
Photomask
A quartz stencil carrying one layer of the chip's pattern; a leading-edge chip needs ~80–100.
Reticle limit
The maximum area a lithography tool can expose at once (~26 × 33 mm ≈ 858 mm²).
RDIMM / DDR5 / LPDDR5X / SOCAMM
Server memory module / DRAM standard / low-power DRAM / compact LPDDR module used by Nvidia.
RISC-V
An open, royalty-free CPU instruction set; a long-run alternative to Arm.
RTL
Register-transfer level: the code engineers write to describe a chip's logic.
Section 232
US law allowing tariffs on national-security grounds.
SoIC
TSMC's 3D die stacking (System on Integrated Chips).
Substrate
The many-layer organic board a chip package sits on, fanning its connections out to the motherboard.
TC bonder
Thermo-compression bonder that stacks HBM dies using micro-bumps.
TSV
Through-silicon via: a vertical copper connection through a die.
WFE
Wafer-fab equipment (front-end tools).
wpm
Wafers per month (a capacity unit).
Yield
The share of good dies per wafer.

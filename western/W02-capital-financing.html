The 60-second version

  1. 2026 is the year the build-out stopped paying for itself. Big-5 capex of ~$815bn A outran operating cash flow of ~$755bn; Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle all have negative free cash flow 41131.
  2. Big Tech became the bond market's biggest borrower. Hyperscalers sold ~$220bn of bonds by 10 August, against ~$30bn a year in 2020–24, and Alphabet and Oracle sold new stock while Alphabet stopped buybacks entirely 365.
  3. A growing slice sits off balance sheets. Meta's Hyperion and El Paso SPVs carry ~$40bn of bonds, and Oracle has ~$288bn of data-center leases signed but not yet started 1922.
  4. The labs' mega-rounds are mostly supplier money. About two-thirds of OpenAI's $122bn round came from Amazon and Nvidia, and each $1 of hyperscaler equity comes back as $2–40 of cloud commitments 6366.
  5. The risk is concentrated, not everywhere. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta can absorb a bust. Oracle (BBB−), SoftBank (borrowing at 9–10%) and the neocloud debt stack cannot, and ~$1tn of backlog rests on two cash-burning labs.

01 · Explain like I'm newWhere the money for AI comes from

Building AI means spending around a trillion dollars a year on chips, servers, buildings and power. That money has to come from somewhere, and each source has a price and a way of going wrong.

There are only four places money can come from. A company can pay out of profits it already earns (operating cash flow). It can borrow, by selling bonds or taking bank loans and private credit. It can sell ownership, through new stock, venture capital or sovereign wealth funds. Or a supplier can lend or invest so its customer keeps buying, which is vendor or circular financing. This layer maps all four, what each one costs, and who carries the risk if AI revenue arrives late.

W01 (Demand & capex) covers who spends. This page covers who funds the spending.

Analogy

Picture a town building a stadium. The richest families pay from savings (hyperscaler cash flow). The town issues bonds (investment-grade debt). A developer builds the stadium on land it owns and rents it to the team on a lease with an escape clause (Meta's SPVs). The concrete supplier takes stadium shares instead of cash (Nvidia investing in OpenAI). A wealthy investor mortgages his other houses to buy a stake (SoftBank's bridge and margin loans). If ticket sales, meaning AI revenue, arrive on time, everyone gets paid. If they don't, what matters is who signed what.

Why can't Microsoft or Google just pay cash, like they used to?

Until 2025 they mostly did. Their ads, software and retail businesses threw off so much cash that even $400bn+ of capex could be paid out of pocket, with buybacks and dividends on top.

In 2026 capex nearly doubled, to ~$815bn for the five biggest spenders, while operating cash flow grew about a quarter. The gap has to come from somewhere: cash piles, new bonds, new stock, or putting buildings into separate companies that borrow on their own. None of this is a sign of distress at Alphabet or Microsoft. It is a sign the build-out is now bigger than even their profit machines.

Definition

Circular (vendor) financing: a supplier's cash, credit or guarantee goes to a customer, and the customer commits to spend it back with that supplier. It is legal and old (telecom equipment makers did it in 1999–2001). It inflates both the supplier's revenue and the customer's apparent funding, and it ties the supplier's balance sheet to the customer's success.

Why it matters now

From 2023 to 2025 the build-out was paid for mostly from Big Tech's free cash flow. In 2026 capex grew faster than cash flow, so the mix changed. The new mix adds debt, leverage, off-balance-sheet obligations and circular deals, and that is where systemic risk can build up.

Six mechanisms worth knowing

  • Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capex. When capex exceeds operating cash flow, the gap is filled from cash on hand, debt or equity.
  • SPVs with a lease and a residual value guarantee. A separate company owns the data center and borrows against it. The tech company leases it on short renewable terms (four years, extendable to ~20) and promises to cover any shortfall in the asset's value if it walks away. It does not control the SPV, so the debt stays off its balance sheet; only an operating lease and a footnote appear 1819.
  • 144A-for-life bonds are private placements sold only to large institutions with no SEC registration: faster and more private, but less liquid.
  • A DDTL (delayed-draw term loan) is drawn as GPUs arrive, secured by the GPUs and the customer contract that pays for them, and paid down from that contract's cash 45.
  • Mandatory convertible preferred pays a fixed dividend (6.25% at Alphabet) and must convert into common stock by a set date (May 2029). Rating agencies count it as mostly equity 6. An ATM offering sells new shares gradually into the market.
  • RPO (remaining performance obligations, or backlog) is contracted revenue not yet recognised. Lenders increasingly lend against it.

03 · What needs fundingThe bill: ~$815bn from five companies in one year

The five biggest spenders guide to about $815bn of capex in 2026 A, up from ~$445bn in 2025 and ~$265bn in 2024. The Big-4 alone (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta) sum to about $733bn at guidance midpoints G98. Including developer-built shells, neoclouds, xAI and the labs' own sites, this page's funding model covers a ~$1.075tn Western build in 2026E A. The Atlas-wide canonical figure is ~$1.0tn (range $0.9–1.1tn; see W11), and W02's number sits at the top of that range because it counts every leased shell.

Measured against what each company earns in cash, the picture splits in two. Microsoft still covers its capex and its $50bn of shareholder returns from operations. Amazon and Alphabet now spend more than they take in. Oracle spends more than one and a half times its operating cash flow.

The industry's reference map for this is Morgan Stanley's estimate of $2.9tn of global data-center spending over 2025–28 E42. It assumed hyperscaler cash flow would carry about half and private credit more than a quarter. Our read: corporate bonds are running well ahead of that path. Big-5 bonds alone will total ~$350bn+ over 2025–26, against $200bn for all four years in the framework. Cash flow is running behind it, because free cash flow turned negative sooner than expected.

04 · Money in / money outSources and uses: where the trillion comes from

A company's sources of cash have to equal its uses. Lay the Big-5 side by side for 2025 and 2026 and the shift is plain: cash flow grew, but capex grew far faster, so bonds and new stock filled the gap, and shareholders got less.

Walk the 2026 numbers as a bridge. Start with ~$755bn of operating cash. Subtract ~$815bn of capex and ~$75bn of buybacks and dividends, and you are ~$135–140bn short. The Big-5 are raising ~$325bn of bonds and equity, more than twice the gap, so the rest piles up as cash. They are pre-funding 2027, when industry capex is expected to rise again to ~$1.1tn E25. Alphabet's cash and securities rose from $126.8bn to $242.5bn in the first half of 2026 alone R4.

Key insight

Buybacks are the first casualty of the AI build-out. Big-5 shareholder returns fall from ~$140bn to ~$75bn A. Alphabet's buyback suspension alone freed ~$55bn: it bought back $0 in H1 2026, against $28.3bn in H1 2025 R6.

The whole Western build, not just the Big-5

Widen the lens to everyone building: developer-owned shells leased to hyperscalers and labs, neoclouds, SpaceX/xAI, and the labs' own and sovereign sites. The total is ~$575bn in 2025 and ~$1.075tn in 2026E A. (Atlas canonical: ~$550bn and ~$1.0tn. The difference is how many leased shells are counted.) The hidden double-count

About $217bn of H1 2026 venture money went to OpenAI and Anthropic 59. 05 · The bond marketBig Tech became the bond market's biggest borrower

Hyperscaler bond issuance averaged about $28–35bn a year in 2020–24. It rose to $93–121bn in 2025 (the range reflects different definitions) and reached about $220bn by 10 August 2026 E363840. Tech now accounts for 10–11.8% of the US investment-grade market 37. Forecasts of all AI-related debt in 2026, including chipmakers and utilities, run from $300bn to $570bn E38, and BofA says Big-5 borrowing "may exceed $300bn a year" 40.

Demand has been deep, but the price is rising. Amazon's March deal drew $126bn of orders and Alphabet's August deal $115bn, 4.6× covered 1210. Yet spreads over Treasuries widened in every maturity bucket in 2026, and the headline hyperscaler average went from ~50bp to ~78bp in two months (Jun–Aug 2026) E1036. Alphabet's £1bn 100-year bond, sold in February, traded below 90 pence by July 9. The 10-year Treasury was 4.69% (20 Aug 2026) and the Fed funds target 3.50–3.75% R36.

The coupons tell the same story by credit quality. At the same maturity, Oracle (BBB−) paid about 35–45bp more than Meta (AA−). Meta's off-balance-sheet Hyperion bond, rated only one notch below Meta, paid 6.58%. SoftBank, a high-yield holding company, paid 8.6–9.75% in dollars in September 2026 14192885.

07 · Off balance sheetSPVs, developer leases and private credit

The new template for funding a campus: put the buildings in a separate company, let investors own most of it, sign a short renewable lease, and promise to make the lenders whole if you leave. The debt never appears on the tech company's balance sheet.

Anatomy of an SPV: Meta's Hyperion

Hyperion (Richland Parish, Louisiana) was announced on 21 Oct 2025 and described as Wall Street's biggest private-credit deal ever 21. The SPV, Beignet Investor LLC, sold $27.3bn of senior secured, fully amortising bonds at 6.58%, due May 2049, rated A+, one notch below Meta R1819. PIMCO took about $18bn E20.

A year later Meta repeated the structure in El Paso with BlackRock (GIP + HPS). It priced about 40bp wider: there was more supply of the same paper, hyperscaler spreads had widened, Meta's CDS was at record levels, and investors wanted a "template premium" E24101. Hyperion Phase 2 reportedly still has no named partner.

The SPV premium

Meta pays ~5.25% on a 10-year bond. Its off-balance-sheet Hyperion bond, rated only one notch lower, costs 6.58%. So Meta pays ~100bp+ a year to keep ~$27bn off its balance sheet and stay flexible: ≈$300–350m a year A (27.3 × 1.1–1.3%). El Paso paid another ~40bp on top.

Stargate Abilene: the developer-lease model

Abilene is the other template. A developer (Crusoe) builds on a powered site, a joint venture with infrastructure investors owns it, a bank lends against the lease, and a hyperscaler (Oracle) rents the whole campus for 15 years to serve a lab (OpenAI) 495051. Every layer is only as good as the layer above it.

Other Stargate and Oracle sites (older knowledge, not re-verified; conf: med). Vantage "Frontier" (Shackelford County, Texas, ~1.4 GW) and "Lighthouse" (Port Washington, Wisconsin) were financed with a bank loan of ~$38bn led by JPMorgan and MUFG in late 2025. Doña Ana County, New Mexico ("Project Jupiter") got ~$18bn. The Michigan (Saline Township) site reportedly changed partners after Blue Owl stepped back in Dec 2025. xAI's Colossus 2 in Memphis used a different template: a ~$20bn GPU SPV (~$7.5bn equity incl. up to $2bn from Nvidia, ~$12.5bn debt) that buys GB200s and leases them to xAI for five years 5455.

Leases: the largest "hidden" financing

A lease works like debt for the tenant but sits outside reported debt, and developers borrow against it. Oracle has $288bn of leases signed but not yet started (Aug 2026; ~$260bn at its FY26 year-end) R31. CoreWeave has $35.5bn 43. Alphabet has $85.2bn of future lease payments 36. Meta's uncommenced obligations are large (one aggregator cited ~$279bn; conf: low). Microsoft's neocloud contracts total ~$60bn (conf: med). One loop, step by step

An illustrative walk-through A:

  1. Amazon wires $50bn of equity to OpenAI 67.
  2. OpenAI commits $100bn to AWS over 8 years, about $12.5bn a year 66.
  3. AWS books it as backlog (AWS backlog $496bn 17) and buys Trainium chips (made by TSMC) and Nvidia GPUs to serve it. Amazon's 2026 capex is ~$220bn.

12 · Unit economicsWhat capital costs, and what a gigawatt must earn to carry it

The same GPU costs very different amounts to finance depending on whose balance sheet it sits on. Microsoft and Alphabet borrow at or near Treasuries. CoreWeave pays ~8.4%. SoftBank pays 9.75%.

Why it matters

A ~400–500bp cost-of-capital gap is a structural advantage for hyperscalers over neoclouds in what is otherwise a commodity GPU-rental business. Neoclouds survive by borrowing their customers' credit: DDTL 4.0 was rated A3 because Meta was the counterparty 45.

Financing cost per gigawatt

A 1 GW AI campus costs ~$12–14bn for the powered shell (Abilene $15bn / 1.2 GW; El Paso ~$14bn / 1 GW; Hyperion ~$27bn / ~2 GW) and ~$30–35bn of IT (Oracle's ~400k GB200s at Abilene ≈ $40bn for 1.2 GW), about $45–50bn all-in A. (The Atlas canonical all-in cost is ~$50–60bn per GW for Blackwell and ~$60–70bn for Rubin; see W04.) If 70% is debt-funded at 6.5% for the shell and 8.5% for the IT, and the assets are depreciated over 25 and 5–6 years, the campus must earn about $9bn a year before power, operations or any profit.

Debt-service ratios

  • Hyperion: $27.3bn at 6.58% amortising over ~20 years implies debt service of ~$2.4–2.5bn a year, so Meta's rent must be ≈$2.7bn a year at a 1.12× cover ratio A20: about 2% of Meta's 2026 operating cash flow.
  • Oracle: interest of $1.4bn a quarter (+55%) ≈ $5.6bn a year against ~$32bn of FY26 operating cash flow, about 17% A33. This is why S&P expects leverage in the mid-4× range.
  • CoreWeave: Q2 interest of $640m was ~25% of revenue ($2.575bn) 4346. At the Big-5, interest is negligible next to operating cash flow.
  • SoftBank: ~$2.8bn a year of interest to carry $30bn of OpenAI equity.

14 · BottlenecksNine places the funding chain could pinch

One more notch takes it to high yield, and IG index funds and many insurers would have to sell or cut their holdings (~$120bn of Oracle bonds are in the Bloomberg US IG index) E34.   2. Buyer concentration. One investor, PIMCO, took ~$18bn of a single $27bn bond 20. SPV paper is 144A, illiquid, and depends on a handful of insurance and asset-management balance sheets.
  3. Duration appetite. Tech issuers are selling 30- to 100-year paper to fund assets whose IT lasts 5–6 years. Alphabet's century bond fell below 90p within five months 9.
  4. Counterparty concentration. About half of Oracle's $664bn RPO is OpenAI (an estimate; Oracle has not confirmed it) 32. CoreWeave's top two customers are 62% of revenue. Microsoft's RPO ex-OpenAI grew only 25% 17. Much of the debt stack rests on two private, cash-burning labs.
  5. Bank bridges. SoftBank's bridge (due Mar 2027) and multi-billion construction loans depend on take-out markets (bonds, ABS, IPOs) staying open 83.
  6. Private-credit liquidity. The OBDC II gating (Feb 2026) shows that "permanent capital" is less permanent than advertised 99.
  7. Rates. Every 100bp adds ~$4bn a year of interest on ~$390bn of 2026 AI debt A.
  8. Physical delays decide credit quality. CoreWeave reported 393 MW and 355 MW undelivered at 30 Jun 2026 43; late power postpones the revenue that services debt. In Hyperion, construction risk sits with Meta 26.
  9. Revenue conversion. Lenders lend against backlog. Oracle says about half its RPO converts within ~36 months 32; slower conversion leaves debt service pressing on free cash flow.

15 · Competitive dynamicsThe balance sheet is the moat

  • The AAA balance sheet is the moat. A 400–500bp cost-of-capital gap separates hyperscalers from neoclouds, which survive by borrowing their customers' credit.
  • The SPV template is spreading. Hyperion (Oct 2025, Blue Owl) was followed by El Paso (Jul 2026, BlackRock), with Hyperion Phase 2 pending 22101. Expect Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle to use similar JV and lease structures, at a price that is rising (~+40bp per repeat).
  • Equity is back. Alphabet's ~$85–90bn equity programme and Oracle's $20bn ATM break a decade of Big Tech shrinking its share count. AI capex now dilutes existing shareholders.
  • The IPO window. SpaceX ($75bn), Cerebras ($5.6bn) and the Anthropic and OpenAI filings mean public equity is replacing private rounds as the marginal funder, and the labs' finances will be public for the first time.
  • Vendors versus financiers. Nvidia, AMD and Amazon compete to fund customers in order to win their compute commitments. The winner is whoever best turns a balance sheet into purchase commitments.
  • Disruptors. (a) Customer-funded capex: Oracle's "bring your own hardware" and $75bn of prepayments move financing to the customer 31. (b) Sovereign "compute as infrastructure": Gulf funds take equity risk at lower return hurdles. (c) Retail access: a $3bn retail tranche in OpenAI via banks 63 and ETF exposure bring private AI to individual investors.

16 · Bull vs bearWell-funded industrial capex, or borrowed and circular money?

Bull case

This is well-funded industrial capex, not a bubble

  • Mostly funded by profitable businesses. Big-5 operating cash flow (~$755bn in 2026E) covers ~83% of capex even after dividends A, and the spenders are AA/AAA rated.
  • Demand is contracted. ~$2.46tn of RPO; Alphabet says demand "meaningfully exceeds supply"; AWS growth accelerated for a fifth straight quarter to +37% 717.
  • End revenue is exploding. Anthropic's run-rate went from ~$9bn to ~$65bn in seven months E; OpenAI had 900M+ weekly users and 50M+ subscribers 63; Google Cloud's margin rose to 35.6%.
  • Bond demand is deep. $126bn of orders for Amazon, $115bn for Alphabet; AA spreads still 40–118bp.
  • The structures move risk to people who want it. PIMCO and insurers want 20-year A+ amortising paper at ~6.6%; Meta keeps flexibility and pays for it.
  • Leverage is low. Hyperscaler liabilities-to-assets were ~48% against 77% for the S&P 500 (Q3 2025) 42; Alphabet still has ~$120bn of net cash.

Bear case

The marginal dollar is borrowed, circular and concentrated

  • Free cash flow has gone negative at Alphabet (Q2), Amazon (TTM) and Oracle (−$23.7bn in FY26, ~−$42bn expected in FY27). Buybacks are cut and equity is issued to fund capex: the end of "capital-light Big Tech".
  • Circularity inflates both sides. ~66% of OpenAI's $122bn round came from suppliers; Amazon's $50bn returns as a $100bn AWS commitment; paper gains (Amazon ~$53bn in one quarter) flatter earnings.
  • Counterparty concentration. ~$1tn of backlog depends on two labs with a ~$105bn combined run-rate and large losses (OpenAI projected $14–17bn of 2026 losses, conf: low) 65.
  • Hidden leverage. ~$40bn of Meta SPV bonds, $288bn of uncommenced Oracle leases, $35bn at CoreWeave, and guarantees (~$13bn for El Paso alone).
  • Asset-liability mismatch. 20–40 year debt funds GPUs with 5–6 year accounting lives and possibly 2–3 year economic lives; DDTL 5.5 finances 3-year contracts with 5-year debt.
  • Pricing is turning. Oracle BBB−, CDS at records, El Paso +40bp over Hyperion, spreads 50 → 78bp in two months, Alphabet's century bond below 90p. SoftBank borrows at 9–10% to buy OpenAI at $852bn.

Analyst synthesis

The AI build-out looks more like railways funded by incumbents than telecom funded by start-ups, except at the edges, where it rhymes with 1999–2001: SoftBank's leverage, CoreWeave's ~7× debt-to-equity, vendor financing and customer concentration. The key difference from fiber is that ~70% of the capital goes to short-lived chips. A glut would not leave dark fiber that someone can use cheaply later; it would leave rapidly depreciating GPUs, so losses would come faster and be more permanent. Shells, power and grid connections look like railways: long-lived and eventually useful.

19 · China angleA different cost of capital (Track 2 teaser)

  • State-bank credit, not global bonds. Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and Baidu fund AI capex mostly from cash flow and state-bank credit. Onshore rates are much lower (China's 10-year government yield is ~2%, older knowledge).
  • State guidance funds play the role of Western sovereign and vendor money: the "Big Fund" III (~RMB 344bn, 2024) plus local-government AI and compute funds. Local "compute vouchers" are the counterpart to Western vendor financing.
  • Converts and dollar bonds: Alibaba and others have used USD/HKD converts. Compare offshore issuance with the West's $250bn+.
  • The circularity equivalent: Huawei's Ascend ecosystem and state-owned carriers buying domestic accelerators.
  • Export controls shape collateral. Western GPU lending relies on liquid secondary GPU markets; China's restricted-chip market changes collateral values.
  • Cross-track: Gulf sovereigns (MGX, QIA, PIF) invest in both ecosystems' supply chains.

20 · GlossaryTerms on this page

144A
A private bond placement sold only to large qualified institutions, with no SEC registration.
ABS / CMBS
Asset-backed or commercial-mortgage-backed securities: bonds repaid from pooled payments such as data-center rents.
ATM offering
At-the-market: selling new shares gradually at market prices through brokers.
Backstop
A promise to buy unused capacity, such as Nvidia's $6.3bn CoreWeave backstop.
BDC
Business development company: a US vehicle, often sold to retail investors, that makes private loans.
Bridge loan
A short-term loan (e.g. one year) meant to be refinanced later with bonds or asset sales.
CDS
Credit default swap: insurance against a borrower's default. Its price in bp a year is a live gauge of credit risk.
Circular financing
A supplier funds a customer, and the customer spends the money back with that supplier.
Convertible note
A bond that can convert into shares; it pays a low coupon because it includes an equity option.
DDTL
Delayed-draw term loan: drawn in stages as assets (GPUs) are delivered, secured by the GPUs and the customer contract.
DSCR
Debt-service coverage ratio: cash available for debt service divided by required payments. 1.12× means 12% headroom.
FCF
Free cash flow: operating cash flow minus capex.
IG / HY
Investment grade (BBB− and above) / high yield (BB+ and below, "junk").
LTV
Loan-to-value: debt divided by collateral value.
Margin loan
A loan secured on shares; it can be called if the share price falls.
Mandatory convertible preferred
A preferred share that pays a dividend and must convert into common stock by a set date.
Neocloud
A GPU-focused cloud provider such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, Lambda or Nscale.
OCF
Operating cash flow: cash a business generates from its operations before investment.
Private credit
Loans made by non-bank funds (Blue Owl, Apollo, Blackstone…) rather than banks or public bond markets.
RPO / backlog
Remaining performance obligations: contracted revenue not yet recognised.
RVG
Residual value guarantee: the tenant's promise to cover a shortfall in the asset's value if it leaves.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the floating-rate benchmark for US dollar loans.
SPV / JV
Special-purpose vehicle / joint venture: a separate legal entity that owns an asset and carries its debt.
Spread (bp)
Extra yield over Treasuries. 100bp = 1 percentage point.
Triple-net lease
The tenant pays taxes, insurance and maintenance in addition to rent.
Warrant
A right to buy shares at a set price, such as AMD's 160M-share warrant to OpenAI.

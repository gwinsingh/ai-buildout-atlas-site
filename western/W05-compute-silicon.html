01 · Explain like I'm newWhat a compute chip actually does

An AI model is a very large grid of numbers. Training it, or asking it a question, means multiplying those grids together trillions of times a second. Compute silicon is the hardware that does that multiplication.

A GPU (graphics processing unit) is a chip with thousands of small arithmetic units working in parallel. It was built for video games, which also push many numbers at once, and turned out to suit neural networks. A custom ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit; Broadcom calls them XPUs) does the same job but is designed by, or for, one customer's workloads: Google's TPU, Amazon's Trainium, Microsoft's Maia, Meta's MTIA. CPUs (Intel Xeon, AMD EPYC and Arm chips such as NVIDIA Grace/Vera, AWS Graviton, Google Axion and Microsoft Cobalt) run the orchestration, data preparation and, increasingly, the tool calls that AI agents make.

Two things sit right next to every accelerator and matter as much as the chip: very fast stacked memory (HBM) and an advanced package that joins the two (CoWoS). Both are made in the supply chain covered in W06.

Analogy

Think of an AI data center as a restaurant kitchen the size of a small city. The GPU is a line of thousands of cooks who chop fast but only do what they are told. HBM is the counter beside them where the ingredients sit; if it is small or far away, the cooks stand idle. The scale-up network (NVLink) is the pass-through window that lets 72 cooks act as one giant cook. The CPU is the head chef who reads the tickets and hands out work. A custom ASIC is a kitchen one restaurant chain built for its own menu: cheaper to run, but it cannot easily cook anyone else's recipes. CUDA is NVIDIA's twenty-year-old recipe book and tool set. Every chef has trained on it, which is why switching kitchens is painful.

Why can't ordinary processors (CPUs) do this job?

They can, just slowly and at far higher energy cost. A CPU spends much of its silicon making one stream of instructions run fast. An accelerator spends it on raw arithmetic units and memory bandwidth, which is what neural networks consume.

In money terms, the same training run on CPUs would need so many more chips and so much more power that it would not pencil out. That is why CPUs now play the head-chef role (orchestration, data preparation, agent tool calls) rather than the line-cook role.

Definition

Accelerator: any chip specialised for AI math. The umbrella term for GPUs, TPUs and other ASICs, NPUs, LPUs and XPUs. In this page, "accelerator silicon" means NVIDIA data-center compute plus AMD Instinct GPUs plus custom ASICs valued at the design partner's price, plus Intel and start-ups. Networking chips are counted in W07.

Why it matters

Compute silicon is the single largest line item in an AI data center and, today, the largest profit pool in the AI stack. Every other layer either feeds it (foundry, HBM and packaging in W06; power and buildings in W03/W04) or rents out what it produces (clouds in W08, labs in W09). If you want to know whether the build-out is sustainable, ask whether this layer's margins are.

02 · How it worksFLOPs, memory, racks and software: the primer

FLOPs and precision: why "FP4" matters

A FLOP is one floating-point operation, a multiply or an add. Chips are rated in TFLOPS (1012 per second) or PFLOPS (1015). One NVIDIA Rubin GPU is rated at 50 PFLOPS of NVFP4 inference R 13; a GB300 NVL72 rack is roughly 1.1 exaFLOPS of dense FP4 (prior knowledge).

Precision is how many bits represent each number. AI training moved from FP32 to BF16/FP16, then FP8 (Hopper, 2022), then FP4 (Blackwell, 2024–25). Halving the bits roughly doubles the math per clock and halves memory traffic, provided model accuracy holds. That is why vendors quote ever-lower precisions. Always check which precision, and whether the figure is sparse or dense: NVIDIA often quotes sparse numbers, which assume half the weights are zero and are 2× the dense figure. This page uses dense figures wherever possible.

Memory bandwidth and HBM: the real bottleneck

A modern accelerator can do math far faster than it can fetch data. Memory bandwidth (TB/s) usually decides inference speed, because every generated token needs the model's weights and the KV cache (the model's short-term memory of the conversation) read from memory. HBM stacks 8–16 DRAM dies vertically beside the GPU. It gives up to ~22 TB/s per GPU on Rubin (HBM4), against 3.35 TB/s on the H100 (HBM3) R 13. Capacity has grown from 80 GB (H100) to 141 GB (H200), 192 GB (B200), 288 GB (B300, MI355X, Rubin) and 432 GB (AMD MI455X, prior knowledge).

HBM is now the largest single cost in an accelerator, and the reason NVIDIA's gross margin dips in late 2026 G 2.

Training vs inference, and why inference is splitting in two

Training creates the model. It needs huge compute, BF16/FP8 math, large scale-up domains and very reliable networking across tens of thousands of chips. NVIDIA and Google's TPU dominate it. Inference runs the model and is now the larger, faster-growing workload. It has two phases: prefill (reading the prompt, compute-bound) and decode (generating tokens one by one, memory-bandwidth-bound). That split drives the 2025–26 wave of disaggregated inference hardware.

Scale-up vs scale-out: why the rack became "one giant GPU"

Scale-up connects GPUs with very fast, low-latency links so they behave like one big chip. NVIDIA's NVLink domain grew from 8 GPUs (HGX H100) to 72 (GB200, GB300 and Vera Rubin NVL72) and will reach 144 GPU packages (576 dies) with Rubin Ultra "Kyber" in 2027 (prior knowledge; conf: med on naming). NVLink 6 gives 3.6 TB/s per GPU and 260 TB/s per rack R 13. The alternatives are AMD's UALink/Infinity Fabric (72-GPU Helios), Google's ICI with optical circuit switches (a 9,216-chip Ironwood pod) R 35 and AWS's NeuronLink.

Scale-out connects racks over InfiniBand or Ethernet (NVIDIA Spectrum-X, Broadcom Tomahawk; see W07). NVIDIA's networking revenue passed $14.8bn a quarter in Q1 FY27 R 5.

Why it matters economically: once the rack is the product, the vendor sells the GPU, CPU, NVLink switch, NICs, DPUs and software as one system. That raises dollar content per GPU and makes any single chip harder to swap out.

The software layer: CUDA and its rivals

CUDA (launched 2006) is NVIDIA's programming model plus hundreds of libraries: cuDNN, cuBLAS, NCCL, TensorRT-LLM and Dynamo, its disaggregated-inference server, which reached 1.0 in Q1 FY27 R 5. Frameworks such as PyTorch "just work" on it. The rivals: AMD ROCm (open-source, much improved in 2025–26, now co-developed with OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic R 192225), Google XLA/JAX on TPUs, the AWS Neuron SDK on Trainium, OpenAI's hardware-agnostic Triton, and Microsoft's Maia SDK (PyTorch plus Triton) R 39.

The moat is not that the alternatives fail to compile. It is (a) the long tail of optimised kernels and tooling, (b) developer familiarity, (c) day-zero support for new model architectures and (d) fungibility: a CUDA cluster can be resold or re-rented to anyone, which lowers its financing cost. Jensen Huang: "The NVIDIA compute platform is fungible and durable and can be redeployed to support other customers" R 2.

Key insight

The CUDA moat has weakened at the very top and still holds in the long tail. The five or six largest buyers can each afford their own compiler teams, and they are also NVIDIA's largest customers. Neoclouds, enterprises, sovereigns and start-ups cannot, which is why NVIDIA's non-hyperscaler business is now growing faster than its hyperscale business.

03 · Market sizeHow big the market is, and how fast it grew

Accelerator silicon grew from about $43bn in CY2023 to about $188bn in CY2025 and should roughly double again to about $365bn in CY2026 A. NVIDIA's data-center revenue alone went from $15.0bn in FY23 to $193.7bn in FY26 R 6.

Why sources disagree on the size of this market

There is no single "AI accelerator market" number everyone agrees on, for four reasons:

  1. Merchant vs custom. NVIDIA, AMD and Intel sell at full market prices. Custom ASICs show up at the design partner's price (Broadcom, Marvell, Alchip, GUC, MediaTek), roughly cost plus a 50–65% gross margin, and Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta then use them at internal cost. So $1 of TPU spend buys more FLOPs than $1 of NVIDIA spend, and dollar shares understate ASIC compute share.
  2. Chip vs system. NVIDIA's Data Center revenue includes CPUs, NVLink switches, NICs, DPUs, software and full compute trays, not just GPUs. Broadcom's "AI semiconductor" revenue includes both XPUs and AI Ethernet switching.
  3. Fiscal calendars. NVIDIA's year runs roughly Feb–Jan (so FY27 ≈ Feb-2026 to Jan-2027), Broadcom's roughly Nov–Oct, Marvell's roughly Feb–Jan.
  4. Counting units. Blackwell packages contain two dies. Huang's "20 million GPUs" for the $500bn backlog counts dies (prior knowledge, GTC DC Oct-2025).

Inside Data Center: compute, networking, and who buys

Networking went from about $3bn a quarter to about $17.5bn in two years R/A, growing faster than compute because NVIDIA now sells whole racks and the fabric between them 25. From FY27, NVIDIA also splits Data Center by customer type: Hyperscale ($48.7bn in Q2 FY27, +101% y/y) and ACIE ($40.3bn, +138% y/y) R 4. Concentration looks high but is diversifying at the margin. In FY23 no customer exceeded 10% E 9. In Q2 FY26 two customers made up 39% R 53. 04 · Key playersWho makes the chips, at a glance

Three business models compete. Merchant vendors (NVIDIA, AMD, Intel) sell to everyone at market prices. Design partners (Broadcom, Marvell, Alchip, GUC, MediaTek) co-design chips for one buyer and earn a foundry-like margin. Buyers (Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI) design chips in-house to avoid the merchant margin. Start-ups try to find a niche, usually inference, and are increasingly absorbed by incumbents.

05 · NVIDIANVIDIA: the incumbent that sells whole "AI factories"

NVIDIA designs GPUs, CPUs, networking and software, outsources manufacturing to TSMC, buys HBM from SK hynix, Samsung and Micron, and has assembly done by Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron and others. It sells chips, boards, compute trays and, increasingly, whole rack-scale systems to hyperscalers, neoclouds, sovereigns, labs and enterprises.

One new architecture a year

In five years (A100 to Rubin), low-precision throughput per GPU rose about 160× A (50 PF NVFP4 ÷ 0.312 PF BF16). Memory bandwidth rose about 11× (2 to 22 TB/s) and the scale-up domain grew 9× (8 to 72 GPUs). The gap between compute growth and memory growth is why HBM became the chokepoint and why SRAM-heavy decode chips (Groq, Cerebras) are being bolted on.

What a GPU sells for, and NVIDIA's revenue per gigawatt

NVIDIA does not publish prices. The estimates below combine company statements G, press and sell-side ranges E and our arithmetic A. The cleanest anchor is NVIDIA's own revenue per gigawatt of AI capacity: ~$18bn for Hopper, ~$25bn for Grace Blackwell and ~$40bn for Vera Rubin G 2. Content per GW more than doubled across two generations.

How we back out "all-in" revenue per GPU from revenue per GW
  • Hopper: ~$18bn/GW G. An H100 HGX node draws ~10.2 kW for 8 GPUs, ~1.3 kW per GPU at IT level and ~1.6–1.7 kW at facility level (PUE ~1.25). 1 GW ÷ 1.65 kW ≈ 600k GPUs; $18bn ÷ 600k ≈ $30k per GPU. This matches reported H100 pricing, which validates the method.
  • Grace Blackwell: ~$25bn/GW G. A 132 kW rack ÷ 72 ≈ 1.8 kW per GPU, plus network and storage ≈ 2.4 kW per GPU at facility level, so ~415k GPUs per GW. $25bn ÷ 415k ≈ $60k per GPU ≈ $4.3m per rack including scale-out networking.
  • Vera Rubin: ~$40bn/GW G. At an assumed ~3.5–3.8 kW per GPU at facility level, ~270k GPUs per GW; $40bn ÷ 270k ≈ $150k per GPU all-in ≈ ~$10–11m per NVL72 rack including networking. Treat this as an upper bound (conf: low).
  • Cross-check: $500bn of Blackwell+Rubin bookings ≈ 20m GPU dies (Huang, Oct-2025), so ≈ $25k per die ≈ $50k per two-die package including networking.

The CUDA and software moat, quantified where possible

  • 20 years of CUDA (2006 to 2026). The GTC 2026 anniversary update covered cuDF, cuVS, NeMo and NIM E 14. Dynamo 1.0 reached production in Q1 FY27 R 5.
  • Software as lock-in. NVIDIA sells NVIDIA AI Enterprise licences per GPU per year but gives away most libraries to lock in the hardware. The money shows up in gross margin, not in a software revenue line.
  • Fungibility premium. Because any customer can use a CUDA cluster, lenders accept GPUs as collateral (GPU-backed ABS, neocloud debt; see W02).
  • Where the moat is weakest: Google (TPU/JAX since 2015), Amazon (Trainium/Neuron), Meta (owner of PyTorch, MTIA) and OpenAI (Triton, its own Broadcom chip), who are also the largest customers. NVLink Fusion is NVIDIA's answer: let custom XPUs plug into NVIDIA racks, CPUs and networking rather than leave the ecosystem. Marvell joined on 31 Mar 2026 and NVIDIA invested $2bn in it R 46.

NVIDIA as investor and financier

NVIDIA now supports demand with three tools: equity in labs and neoclouds, which then buy GPUs; balance-sheet guarantees (the residual-value guarantee behind OpenAI's Ohio site); and capacity buy-backs (the $36bn of cloud commitments, where NVIDIA rents back capacity it sold). None of this is illegal or unusual on its own; vendor financing was common in the 1999–2001 telecom build-out too. But it means NVIDIA's reported revenue partly depends on credit NVIDIA itself extends.

The 10-Q's own tables, read directly on the 25 Sep 2026 re-check, show how fast the supply book grew. Supply and capacity commitments rose from $119bn to $279bn in one quarter, "primarily related to the procurement of memory", and $92bn of that falls due in the rest of FY27 R 66. This is NVIDIA locking up HBM and DRAM ahead of Rubin, and it is the same memory squeeze that pushes its gross margin down (W06).

07 · Custom siliconThe hyperscalers' own chips, and the partners who build them

There are four reasons to build your own chip: avoid paying NVIDIA's ~75% gross margin, tune the chip to your own models and data centers, secure supply, and gain bargaining leverage. The costs are $0.5–1bn+ of engineering per generation, 2–3-year design cycles and the risk of betting on the wrong model architecture.

The design-partner model splits the work. The hyperscaler owns the architecture and software. A partner (Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip, GUC) does the physical design, SerDes and I/O, packaging and the TSMC/HBM supply chain, and earns a ~50–65% gross margin on the chip.

Where the markup comes from

  1. Time to revenue. A GW of AI capacity costs ~$50–60bn. A buyer who deploys 6–12 months earlier with a proven stack earns tokens sooner; the premium over an ASIC is cheap next to the cost of idle capex.
  2. Software and fungibility (CUDA). The asset can be resold or re-rented, which lowers the buyer's cost of capital.
  3. Systems engineering. NVLink, NVL72 racks, networking and reference designs are integration work buyers would otherwise do.
  4. Supply lock-up. NVIDIA holds the largest share of CoWoS and HBM allocations, backed by $279bn of supply commitments R 66. Scarcity sustains price.
  5. R&D amortisation. ~$36bn of annual opex spread over ~8–9m GPUs ≈ $4k per GPU A: material, but small next to ~$25–100k of gross profit per GPU.

Math got cheap; memory did not

Our estimate of dollars per dense TFLOP at the lowest supported precision fell from ~$40 (A100, BF16, 2020) to ~$14 (H100, FP8) to ~$4 (B200, FP4) to ~$1–3 (Rubin, NVFP4; chip-only vs all-in) A. At constant FP8 precision the decline is closer to 3–4× over 2023–26. Meanwhile dollars per GB of HBM barely fell and are rising in 2026: the bottleneck and the bargaining power sit with the memory makers (W06).

Token economics: why buyers pay up

NVIDIA's pitch is revenue per watt. Data centers are power-limited, so the chip that produces the most tokens per MW wins regardless of chip price. Management claims Rubin cuts token cost 10× (GTC) to 35× (call) versus Blackwell Ultra G 214. If power, not dollars, is the binding constraint, a buyer rationally pays up for the most efficient chip. That is how NVIDIA's revenue per GW can more than double while customers still see lower cost per token (see W08 and W09).

13 · Competitive dynamicsEvery frontier lab now runs three or more chip platforms

This is the most important competitive fact of 2026. Anthropic runs on TPU (up to 1m), Trainium (up to 5 GW), NVIDIA (up to 1 GW via Azure) and AMD (up to 2 GW). OpenAI runs on NVIDIA (~12 GW through 2030 G 2), AMD (6 GW), Broadcom custom (10 GW), Cerebras (750 MW) and Trainium (~2 GW). Meta runs on NVIDIA, AMD (6 GW), MTIA, Trainium3 and, in talks, TPU.

Structural shifts, 2025 to 2027

  • From chip to rack to "AI factory". Competition is now between 72+-GPU scale-up systems, not individual chips. AMD needed Helios; TPUs have always been pod-scale.
  • Disaggregated inference. Prefill on Rubin CPX, decode on Groq LPX or Cerebras, KV cache on Vera/BlueField/CXL memory. Specialised silicon is being bolted onto NVIDIA and AMD platforms, which lets the incumbents absorb start-up innovation (the Groq and Taalas deals).
  • The labs are multi-sourcing (the sankey above).
  • NVIDIA's counter-moves: NVLink Fusion (open its rack to others' XPUs) 46, a standalone CPU (Vera) 1, the Groq licence (buy the best decode architecture) 16, customer financing 7, and an annual cadence that makes each ASIC obsolete sooner.

14 · Bull vs bearThe strongest case on each side

Bull case

Demand outruns supply through 2027, and content per GW keeps rising

  • Supply-constrained through 2027. FY28 +70% guidance is supply-limited with demand "~100%" G 2. Hyperscaler capex runs ~$800bn in 2026 and ~$1.3trn in 2027; cloud backlog is above $2trn G 2. Labs are compute-starved: over $400bn of VC in H1 2026, ~70% spent on compute, per NVIDIA.
  • Inference and agents multiply compute per user (15–100× per agent G 2). Nearly 20 AI-native companies have passed $1bn ARR, so end demand is monetising.
  • Content per GW keeps rising: $18bn → $25bn → $40bn G 2. Even if GW growth slowed, NVIDIA could grow through share of wallet.
  • The customer base is diversifying: ACIE and sovereign buyers +138% y/y R 4. China is pure upside.
  • Custom silicon grows the pie rather than stealing it. Broadcom +221%, Amazon chips triple-digit, AMD DC +107% and NVIDIA DC +117% are all growing together. No evidence yet of a zero-sum market.
  • The moat is widening to systems, networking, CPUs and software, and NVIDIA is buying the best alternative architectures (Groq).

Bear case

Buyers substitute, credit props up demand, and old GPUs lose value

  • ASIC share gains at the largest buyers. Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic are all building or buying alternatives; Anthropic alone has lined up ~8 GW+ of non-NVIDIA silicon. I/O Fund sees NVIDIA's share falling toward ~70% E 17. When growth slows, every custom chip already designed becomes a direct substitute.
  • Concentration and circularity. 44% of H1 FY27 revenue came from three direct customers R 66; the AI labs NVIDIA backs with its balance sheet are expected, as a group, to be ~25% of FY28 revenue G 2; OpenAI's campus leases are backstopped by a $105bn NVIDIA guarantee R 7; NVIDIA holds ~$94–99bn of ecosystem equity plus $25bn committed 618. If one lab fails, revenue, receivables and the guarantee go wrong at once.
  • The "NVIDIA tax" invites substitution. Each $1 of NVIDIA revenue carries ~66¢ of operating profit that buyers would like to keep. AMD's warrants (up to 160m shares each to OpenAI and Meta) show rivals will pay dearly to break in 2225.
  • Depreciation of old GPUs. Hyperscalers depreciate servers over 5–6 years. If Rubin cuts token cost 10–35× G 2, Hopper and early Blackwell lose rental value fast. H100 rental rates fell from ~$8/hr in 2023 to ~$2/hr in 2025 (conf: med), forcing impairments and stress on GPU-backed debt (W02). NVIDIA's own cadence is the cause.
  • Memory inflation. Gross margin guided down ~300 bp to 71–72% G 2; HBM makers now hold pricing power.
  • Geopolitics. China is already zero; escalation over Taiwan or rare earths could hit supply rather than demand.
  • Capex digestion. 2027's ~$1.3trn of hyperscaler capex must be earned back by W08–W10 revenue. Any pause hits W05 first and hardest: in 2022 NVIDIA's gross margin fell to 45.9% in one quarter R.

18 · GlossaryTerms on this page

Accelerator
Any chip specialised for AI math (GPU, TPU, NPU, LPU, XPU).
ACIE
NVIDIA's reporting sub-market "AI Clouds, Industrial and Enterprise": non-hyperscale data-center customers such as neoclouds, sovereigns and enterprises.
ASIC / XPU
Application-specific integrated circuit: a chip built for one job or one customer, such as Google's TPU. Broadcom calls custom AI ASICs "XPUs".
ASP
Average selling price.
BF16 / FP8 / FP4 / NVFP4 / MXFP4
Number formats with 16, 8 or 4 bits. NVFP4 is NVIDIA's 4-bit format with fine-grained scaling; MXFP4 is the OCP-standard equivalent.
Convertible bond
A bond the holder can later swap for the issuer's shares at a set price. NVIDIA's $3.5bn MediaTek purchase (Aug-2026) is this kind of investment, not a takeover.
CoWoS (-S / -L)
TSMC's 2.5D packaging that puts logic dies and HBM on a silicon interposer or bridge. CoWoS-L supports larger multi-die packages.
CPU
Central processing unit: a general-purpose processor. In AI systems it orchestrates work, prepares data and runs agent tool calls.
CUDA
NVIDIA's programming platform and library ecosystem (2006), the core of its software moat.
Dense vs sparse FLOPS
Sparse ratings assume 2:4 structured sparsity (half the weights zero) and are 2× the dense figure. Dense is what most workloads get.
Decode / prefill
The two phases of LLM inference: generating tokens one at a time (memory-bound) and processing the prompt (compute-bound).
Design partner
A company (Broadcom, Marvell, Alchip, GUC, MediaTek) that turns a buyer's chip architecture into manufacturable silicon and manages its supply chain, for a ~50–65% gross margin.
Disaggregated inference
Running prefill and decode on different hardware pools, each tuned to its bottleneck.
DSO
Days sales outstanding: how long customers take to pay.
EMIB-T
Intel's embedded-bridge packaging with through-silicon vias; a CoWoS alternative.
Exaflop (EF)
1018 floating-point operations per second.
FLOP / FLOPS
A floating-point operation (a multiply or add) / operations per second. TFLOPS = 1012, PFLOPS = 1015.
GPU
Graphics processing unit: a chip with thousands of parallel arithmetic units; the workhorse of AI.
HBM (HBM3 / 3e / 4 / 4e)
High-bandwidth memory: DRAM dies stacked vertically beside the processor to feed it data fast. Now the largest cost in an accelerator.
Inference / training
Running a finished model to answer requests / creating the model by adjusting its weights on data.
KV cache
The model's short-term memory of a conversation (stored keys and values from earlier tokens), read on every new token.
Kyber
NVIDIA's 2027 rack architecture for Rubin Ultra: vertical compute blades, 144 GPU packages.
LPU
Groq's "language processing unit": an SRAM-centric, deterministic inference chip.
Neocloud
A GPU-focused cloud such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe or Lambda.
NVL72
A rack of 72 NVIDIA GPUs in one NVLink domain.
NVLink / NVLink Fusion
NVIDIA's scale-up interconnect. Fusion licenses it to third-party CPUs and XPUs so they can plug into NVIDIA racks.
ODM
Original design manufacturer (Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron): builds servers and racks for others.
Residual-value guarantee
A promise to cover the shortfall if a leased asset's value or re-lease proceeds fall below a floor (NVIDIA for SB Energy / OpenAI).
ROCm
AMD's open-source GPU software stack, its answer to CUDA.
Scale-up / scale-out
Tightly coupled links within a rack or pod / network links between racks.
SerDes
Serializer/deserializer: the high-speed I/O circuit that moves data on and off a chip; Broadcom's core IP.
Superpod
Google's maximum TPU pod (9,216 Ironwood chips).
TCO
Total cost of ownership: chip plus power, facility, networking, software and people over the asset's life.
TDP
Thermal design power: a chip's rated power draw.
UALink
Open scale-up interconnect standard backed by AMD and others.
Warrant
A right to buy shares at a set price. AMD granted OpenAI and Meta warrants for up to 160m shares each at $0.01, vesting on milestones.
